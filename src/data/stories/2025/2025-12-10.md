Mira knew she should be in bed. It was well past midnight, and she'd already extended her library pass three times with increasingly creative excuses to Madam Oakwood. But the Ancient Runes essay wasn't going to write itself, and Professor Babbling had set an impossibly high standard with her comments on Mira's last piece of work: "Excellent research, but I know you're capable of deeper analysis."

Deeper analysis. The phrase had been circling Mira's mind for days, taunting her with its vagueness. What did deeper even mean? She'd cited twelve different sources, cross-referenced three runic alphabets, and traced the evolution of Dagaz through four centuries of magical texts.

Apparently, that wasn't deep enough.

She rubbed her eyes beneath her reading glasses and looked around the library. The main floor was empty, of course—even the most dedicated students had given up hours ago. The enchanted lamps had dimmed to a soft glow, just enough light to read by if you squinted. Madam Oakwood had long since retired to her private quarters, trusting Mira with the run of the place after four years of proving herself a respectful, careful researcher.

Or possibly just tired of arguing with her about extended hours.

Mira stood and stretched, her spine crackling in a way that suggested she'd been hunched over books for too long. The essay was due in two days, and she had perhaps half of what she needed. The problem was that all the obvious sources were exhausted. She needed something new. Something no one else in her class would think to reference.

She needed the old section.

The library's oldest wing was technically accessible to students—there was nothing forbidden about it, nothing restricted. It was simply... forgotten. The books there were so old that most had been superseded by newer editions or more recent scholarship. Madam Oakwood kept them for historical value, but few students bothered to venture into the cramped, dusty aisles when everything they needed was available in the bright, organized main section.

But if Mira wanted deeper analysis, maybe she needed to go deeper. Literally.

She picked up her bag and her lamp, casting a quick Lumos to strengthen the light, and made her way to the back of the library. Past the study tables and the comfortable reading chairs. Past the section on Transfiguration theory and the surprisingly extensive collection of magical creatures field guides. Past the bronze bust of some long-dead headmaster whose name had worn off the plaque.

The old section began without fanfare. One moment the bookshelves were the clean, well-maintained oak she was used to. The next, they were darker wood, older, their surfaces bearing the scars of centuries—water damage, scratches from careless wands, the initials of students who'd been brave or foolish enough to deface library property.

The air smelled different here. Dustier, yes, but also richer somehow. Like old paper and leather and secrets.

Mira ran her fingers along the spines as she walked, reading titles in the wandlight. _Runes of the Elder Druids_. _Magical Linguistics: A Comparative Study_. _The Symbolic Language of Stone Circles_.

She pulled out the last one—it looked promising—and flipped it open. The publication date made her smile: 1847. The print was small and cramped, illustrated with careful hand-drawn diagrams. Definitely not in the standard bibliography.

This was more like it.

She tucked the book under her arm and kept exploring, turning down aisles she'd never noticed before. The old section was larger than she'd realized, extending back into what must be the oldest part of the castle. The stone walls were visible here between the shelves, rough-hewn and ancient.

That's when she noticed the alcove.

It wasn't hidden, exactly. But it was tucked away behind a particularly tall shelf, in a corner where the lamplight didn't quite reach. Mira raised her wand higher and stepped around the shelf.

The alcove was small—barely six feet across—but it felt immediately, powerfully inviting. A worn armchair sat against one wall, its cushions flat with age but still intact. A tiny table held the remnants of a melted candle. And the walls—all three of them—were lined with books.

Not library books. These were personal volumes, their spines showing the wear of being loved rather than just catalogued. Someone had brought them here. Built this little nest of knowledge and comfort in the forgotten corner of a forgotten section.

Mira set her bag down and approached the shelves, curious. The books were an eclectic mix: _Pride and Prejudice_ next to _Advanced Potion Making_. _Sonnets from the Portuguese_ beside _The Standard Book of Spells, Grade 6_. A battered copy of _Alice's Adventures in Wonderland_ leaned against _Magical Theory_ by Adalbert Waffling.

She pulled out the Alice book—she'd loved it as a child—and opened it to a random page.

There, in the margin next to a passage about the Cheshire Cat, someone had written in faded blue ink: _Sometimes disappearing is the most powerful magic of all. —M.K., 1983_

Mira blinked. She turned the page, and found another note, this time in different handwriting, purple ink: _Or reappearing when you're needed. Came back to this today after a hard week. Still true. —J.L., 1996_

And below that, in fresh black ink that couldn't be more than a few years old: _Been thinking about this since my mum passed. What does it mean to vanish and leave only your smile behind? Is that grief? Memory? Both? —S., 2022_

Mira's heart was beating faster now. She set Alice down carefully and reached for another book. _Magical Theory_. Opened it.

There—notes in the margins. Different inks, different hands, different years. Some were simple comments on the text. Others were longer reflections, or questions posed to no one in particular. Or to everyone in particular, across the span of decades.

She tried Pride and Prejudice. More notes. Some were literary analysis—a heated debate about Mr. Darcy that appeared to have played out across multiple readers and multiple decades, each new student adding their opinion to the conversation.

But others were more personal. Next to the line "I have been meditating on the very great pleasure which a pair of fine eyes in the face of a pretty woman can bestow," someone had written: _Asked him today. He said yes. I can't believe he said yes. —R.T., 1989_

And next to that, added years later: _Found this tonight after my own crushing rejection. Nice to know it works out sometimes. Happy for you, R.T., whoever you are. —anonymous, 2003_

Mira sat down in the armchair, barely noticing the dust that puffed up around her. She was holding Magical Theory now, turning pages slowly, reading decades of students teaching each other across time.

_This chapter saved me during my O.W.L.s. If you're reading this while studying, take a breath. You've got this. —1977_

_Failed my first Charms essay this year. Came here to feel sorry for myself. Found your note. Tried again. Got an E. Thank you, whoever you were. —M.C., 1985_

_Sometimes I come here just to remember I'm not alone. Even when I feel invisible. —2001_

_Your note about feeling invisible helped me more than you know. Adding my own: if you're reading this and struggling, please know that you matter. Your story isn't over. —2009_

Mira's eyes were stinging. She wasn't sure when she'd started crying, but her cheeks were wet and her glasses were fogging up. She took them off, wiped them on her robe, and kept reading.

There were study tips and book recommendations. Jokes and doodles. Confessions of anxiety before exams. Celebrations of triumphs. Small kindnesses left for strangers who would become friends across the gulf of years, connected only by this shared space and these shared words.

It was the most beautiful thing Mira had ever found.

She didn't know how long she sat there, reading through the books one by one. The collection was small—maybe twenty volumes—but each was dense with marginalia, with conversation, with the layered voices of students who'd found this alcove and made it their own.

Finally, she picked up the Alice book again and turned to her favorite passage—the one where Alice spoke to herself about giving advice but never following it. She read the existing notes: observations about identity, about self-knowledge, about growing up.

And then, her hand only shaking a little, she pulled out her own quill and added to the conversation:

_I came here tonight looking for sources for an essay. Found something much better—evidence that the search for knowledge is never solitary. Thank you to everyone who left pieces of themselves here. You've reminded me that learning isn't just about being perfect. It's about being part of something larger. —M.P., 2025_

She sat back, looking at her words next to all the others. Her contribution to this decades-long dialogue.

The essay suddenly seemed less important. Or rather, important in a different way. It wasn't about proving to Professor Babbling that she could be deep enough. It was about connecting with the material, with the history, with the long chain of students who'd wrestled with the same questions she was wrestling with now.

Mira gathered her things slowly, reluctant to leave but aware that dawn couldn't be far off. She'd need at least a few hours of sleep before classes.

As she was preparing to go, she heard footsteps.

Her heart jumped—was it Madam Oakwood? Or worse, Filch? But the steps were too light, and when Finn appeared around the bookshelf, she nearly collapsed with relief.

"Mira? What are you doing here?"

"I could ask you the same thing," she countered.

Finn held up a book on magical plants. "Couldn't sleep. Wanted to check something about Venomous Tentacula propagation after, you know, yesterday's excitement." He looked around the alcove, eyes widening. "What is this place?"

Mira hesitated, then made a decision. "Sit down. I want to show you something."

She handed him Pride and Prejudice, opened to the page with the notes about asking someone out. Watched as his freckled face shifted from confusion to wonder to something like awe.

"This is..." he breathed. "How long has this been here?"

"Decades. Maybe longer." Mira explained what she'd discovered, showing him book after book, note after note.

By the time she finished, Finn was grinning. "This is the coolest thing I've ever seen. It's like... it's like the castle is full of ghosts, but not the dead kind. The living kind. Everyone who's ever been here, they're still here. In words."

"Exactly," Mira said.

They sat in comfortable silence for a moment, passing books back and forth, reading selected passages to each other.

"We should tell Zara and Elliot," Finn said finally.

"I was thinking the same thing."

"But not anyone else," Finn added quickly. "This feels like it should stay... I don't know. Not secret exactly. But special. Reserved for people who really need it."

"People who find it," Mira agreed. "The way it's always been."

"Should we add to it?" Finn asked, holding up the plant book he'd brought. "Leave it here for the next person?"

Mira considered. "Maybe not on the first visit. Let's come back, the four of us. See what feels right."

Finn nodded. "It's a deal."

They left the alcove together, Mira carrying her newly discovered runes text, Finn still holding his plant book. As they navigated back through the old section toward the main library, Finn spoke again.

"You know what I love about this? It's not showy. It's not grand magic or important history. It's just... people being kind to each other across time. Leaving notes for friends they'll never meet."

"The best kind of magic," Mira said softly.

At the library entrance, they parted ways—Finn toward Hufflepuff's dormitories near the kitchens, Mira up toward Ravenclaw Tower. The castle was silent except for the occasional snore from a portrait and the distant sound of Peeves singing somewhere on another floor.

Mira climbed the spiral staircase to her common room, answered the door's riddle on autopilot, and stumbled up to her dormitory. She fell into bed fully clothed, too tired to even remove her glasses properly.

But before sleep claimed her, she thought about the notes in those books. About students supporting each other across decades. About how everyone who'd written in those margins had been exactly where she was now—stressed, uncertain, trying to find their way. And they'd all made it through. All left a piece of themselves behind to help whoever came next.

She'd found what she was looking for tonight. Not a citation for her essay—though the runes book would help with that too. But something deeper. Evidence that she was part of something larger than herself. A chain of seekers and students and scholars stretching back through time and forward into a future she'd never see.

Professor Babbling wanted deeper analysis? Mira would give her deeper analysis. Not just of runes, but of what it meant to study them. Of why people had been decoding symbols and seeking meaning in carved stones for thousands of years. Of how knowledge was never solitary—it was always a conversation, always a gift passed from one mind to another across whatever distance separated them.

That was deep enough. That was true.

---

The next morning, Mira woke later than usual and had to rush to make it to Ancient Runes on time. But as she slid into her seat, she caught Professor Babbling's eye and smiled—a real smile, not a nervous student-pleasing one.

"Rough night, Miss Patel?" the professor asked, noting her slightly disheveled appearance.

"Good night, actually," Mira corrected. "Very good night."

At lunch, she pulled Zara and Elliot aside and told them about the alcove. Their reactions were exactly what she'd hoped for—Elliot's eyes lit up with curiosity about the historical annotations, while Zara immediately wanted to know if there were any Quidditch books in the collection.

"We should go tonight," Elliot said. "All four of us."

"After dinner," Mira agreed. "I'll get permission from Madam Oakwood for late research. She already thinks I'm obsessed."

"You are obsessed," Zara pointed out.

"Fair."

That evening, the four of them gathered in the old section of the library, squeezing into the alcove together. It was crowded with all of them there, but in a good way—like being in a blanket fort as children, the smallness making everything cozier.

Mira watched her friends discover the notes, saw their faces as they realized what they'd found. Finn immediately gravitated toward the plant book Mira had noticed earlier, reading aloud a debate between two students from the 1960s about the proper care of Mimbulus mimbletonia. Zara found a book about famous Quidditch matches and nearly squealed over a note from someone who'd played for England years after graduating. Elliot picked up a slim volume of poetry and read in silence, their expression growing softer with each page.

"This is perfect," Zara said finally, setting down her book and looking around at all of them. "This is exactly what I didn't know I needed."

"Should we add something?" Finn asked, echoing his question from the night before. "Continue the tradition?"

They discussed it quietly, passing books back and forth, reading favorite passages to each other. Finally, Elliot pulled out their ever-present sketchbook and carefully removed a blank page.

"What if," they said slowly, "we leave a note. Not in the books—those feel like they have their own conversations going. But here, in the space. For whoever finds it next."

Together, they crafted a message. Each of them writing a line, their different handwriting styles creating a physical representation of their friendship:

_To whoever finds this place: You're not alone. Whatever you're searching for, whether it's answers or peace or just a quiet moment away from expectations—you deserve to find it. This alcove has held generations of students just like you. Add your voice if you wish, or simply rest here knowing others have rested here too. The books are a gift. The conversation is ongoing. You're part of it now. —M.P., F.M., Z.O., E.N., December 2025_

Elliot pinned the note to the wall using a sticking charm, and they all stepped back to admire it.

"Think anyone will find it?" Finn asked.

"Someone will," Mira said with certainty. "Someone always does."

As they filed out of the alcove, turning down the lamp and leaving the space as they'd found it—but also completely transformed by their presence—Mira felt something settle in her chest. A sense of rightness.

She'd come to the library looking for sources. She'd found community instead. Found evidence that the struggles she faced weren't unique, that others had been here before and would be here after. Found proof that the best magic wasn't always the flashiest—sometimes it was just people leaving kindness for strangers across the barrier of time.

That night, she added to her December journal:

_December 10th: Found a hidden alcove full of conversations spanning decades. Learned that we're never the first to struggle, never the first to search, never truly alone in our questions. The students who came before us left gifts—words, encouragement, connection. And now we've added to that gift for whoever comes next._

_Professor Babbling wanted deeper analysis. I think I finally understand what she meant. Depth isn't about more sources or longer citations. It's about connection. About understanding that every piece of knowledge is a conversation between past and present, between teacher and student, between one mind struggling with a question and another mind that struggled with the same question years ago._

_Magic is about connection. Learning is about connection. And sometimes the most profound connections happen between people who never meet, who exist in different times but share the same space, the same questions, the same hope that their words will matter to someone someday._

_In that alcove, surrounded by notes from strangers who became companions across time, I felt less alone than I have all term. And I hope that someday, our note will make someone else feel the same way._

Outside her window, snow fell soft and silent over the castle grounds. Somewhere in the library, the alcove waited in the darkness—full of books and notes and the accumulated kindness of generations.

And in seven different centuries' worth of ink, voices whispered across time: You're not alone. Keep going. We believe in you. You matter.

The conversation would continue. It always had. It always would.

And now Mira was part of it.
