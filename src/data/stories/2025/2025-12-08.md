The Hufflepuff common room was particularly cozy on Monday evening, with the fire crackling warmly and the December wind rattling the windows. Finn had claimed one of the low tables near the hearth, and his friends had gathered around him—Mira cross-legged on a cushion with a Transfiguration textbook in her lap, Zara sprawled comfortably on the sofa with her Quidditch strategy notes, and Elliot perched on the arm of a chair, sketching idly in their notebook.

It was the kind of comfortable silence that came from knowing each other well enough that conversation wasn't always necessary. They'd fallen into this routine over the past week—gathering after dinner in whichever common room was most convenient, doing homework together or just being present in the same space.

"My dad sent this in the morning post," Finn said, breaking the quiet. He reached into his bag and carefully withdrew a small spherical object wrapped in protective cloth. "I mentioned in my last letter that we'd been learning about memory charms in class, and he thought I'd like to see it."

He unwrapped the cloth with reverent care, revealing a Remembrall about the size of a large marble. The glass sphere was old—not just old, but *ancient*—with a faint cloudiness that spoke of decades of use. Inside, wispy smoke drifted in slow, lazy patterns, currently a calm, clear white.

"Is that what I think it is?" Mira looked up from her book, eyes widening behind her glasses.

"Family heirloom," Finn said, his voice warm with pride. "It belonged to my great-grandmother. She got it when she started at Hogwarts in 1947, and it's been passed down ever since. My grandmother used it, then my dad during his school years, and someday..." He smiled. "Someday it'll be mine officially. For now, Dad just wanted me to borrow it for a bit."

"That's amazing," Zara said, sitting up to get a better look. "Does it still work?"

"Watch." Finn held the Remembrall in his palm and furrowed his brow in concentration. After a moment, the smoke inside turned a deep, rich red. "I'm forgetting something. Probably that Herbology essay that's due Wednesday." He relaxed, and the smoke gradually cleared back to white.

"May I?" Elliot asked, setting down their sketchbook with interest.

"Sure, just be—"

Finn's words caught in his throat as he moved to hand the Remembrall to Elliot. His fingers, still slightly damp from the condensation on his mug of pumpkin juice, slipped on the smooth glass surface.

Everything seemed to happen in slow motion. The Remembrall tumbled through the air, catching the firelight as it fell. Finn lunged forward, his hand grasping at empty space. Mira gasped. Zara started to her feet.

The Remembrall hit the corner of the wooden table with a sound like a breaking heart—a sharp, crystalline crack that seemed impossibly loud in the cozy room.

It bounced once, rolling across the table, and Finn caught it on the second bounce, his hands shaking. But the damage was done. A visible fracture ran across the surface of the glass, spider-webbing into smaller cracks. And inside, the smoke that had been so carefully contained for nearly eight decades began to dissipate, wisping out through the broken seal like a dying breath.

Within seconds, the interior of the Remembrall was empty. Clear glass with nothing inside but air.

"No." Finn's voice was barely a whisper. "No, no, no."

His hands were trembling so badly that Mira gently took the damaged Remembrall from him and set it on the table. The crack was unmistakable, running like a scar across the surface of something that had been whole and perfect just moments before.

"Finn—" Zara started, but he was already shaking his head.

"It was my great-grandmother's." His voice cracked. "She carried it every day of her seven years here. My grandmother used it to remember her way around Diagon Alley when she first got her wand. My dad..." He pressed his hands to his face. "My dad treasures it. He talks about it all the time, about the family history, about how it's been passed down through three generations, and I—I had it for less than twelve hours and I broke it."

"It was an accident," Elliot said firmly, moving to sit beside him. "Accidents happen."

"That doesn't make it better!" Finn's eyes were bright with unshed tears. "This was *important*. It was supposed to be mine someday, and I ruined it."

The guilt in his voice was palpable, made worse by the way he hunched in on himself as if trying to physically make himself smaller. Mira's heart ached for him.

"We'll fix it," she said with determination, picking up the Remembrall and examining it closely. "There have to be repair charms. Glass mending spells. Something."

"She's right," Zara added, her usual competitive energy shifting into protective fierceness. "We're fourth years at Hogwarts School of Witchcraft and Wizardry. If we can't figure out how to fix a broken magical object, what are we even doing here?"

Finn looked up at them, hope and desperation warring in his expression. "You think we can?"

"We can definitely try," Elliot said. "And I know exactly where to start."

---

An hour later, they had commandeered a corner table in the library, surrounded by stacks of books with titles like *The Practical Guide to Mending Charms*, *Glass Magic: Theory and Application*, and *Heirloom Enchantments: Preservation and Restoration*.

Madame Oakwood had raised her eyebrows at the pile of books they'd requested but hadn't asked questions, simply pointing them toward the relevant sections and reminding them that the library closed at nine.

"Okay," Mira said, opening her third book and scanning the table of contents. "There are definitely repair charms for glass. *Reparo* is the basic one, but for magical objects, it gets more complicated."

"*Reparo* fixes physical damage," Elliot read from their own book, finger tracing the lines of text. "But it doesn't necessarily restore magical properties. The enchantment on a magical object is often tied to its physical integrity—if the object is broken, the magic can be disrupted or lost entirely."

Finn, who had been sitting quietly with the damaged Remembrall cradled in his hands, looked up. "So even if we fix the glass..."

"The enchantment might not work the same way," Mira finished gently. "But that doesn't mean we shouldn't try to repair it. At least then it would look whole again, even if the magic is different."

"There's also this," Zara said, turning her book around so they could see the page. "It talks about something called 'sympathetic restoration'—using a piece of the original object or a strong emotional connection to help reinforce repair spells. Since Finn has a family connection to the Remembrall, that might help."

They spent another twenty minutes researching, taking careful notes. The process seemed straightforward enough in theory: use *Reparo* to mend the physical glass, then attempt to reseal the enchantment using a combination of *Finite Incantatem* followed by a careful reapplication of the original spell. But the books were filled with warnings about how difficult it was to restore complex enchantments, especially old ones.

"We should ask Professor Flitwick," Mira said finally. "He's the Charms expert. If anyone would know how to do this properly, it's him."

Finn nodded, though he still looked worried. "What if he's already left for the evening?"

"Only one way to find out," Elliot said, already standing. "Come on."

They found Professor Flitwick's office still lit, and when they knocked, his cheerful voice called out, "Come in, come in!"

The tiny professor was standing on a stack of books at his desk, grading what appeared to be second-year essays. He beamed when he saw them, then his expression turned concerned as he noticed Finn's distress.

"Mr. MacLeod! And friends. What brings you all here at this hour? Nothing wrong, I hope?"

Finn stepped forward and, with shaking hands, showed Professor Flitwick the broken Remembrall. The story spilled out—the heirloom, the accident, their research about repair charms.

Professor Flitwick listened carefully, occasionally making small sounds of sympathy. When Finn finished, the professor hopped down from his stack of books and approached, gesturing for Finn to place the Remembrall on his desk.

He examined it closely, pulling out his wand and casting several diagnostic charms that made the crack glow faintly blue.

"Hmm," he said finally. "I see. Well, the good news is that the glass itself can certainly be repaired. *Reparo* will handle that quite nicely. The bad news..." He looked up at Finn with kind eyes. "The bad news is that the enchantment is indeed compromised. Remembralls are delicate magic, you see—they require a perfectly sealed environment to maintain the smoke's color-changing properties. Once that seal is broken and the original smoke escapes, well..."

"It won't work anymore," Finn said dully.

"It may not work *the same* anymore," Professor Flitwick corrected gently. "There are ways to re-enchant it, but the new enchantment would be just that—new. It wouldn't have the same magical signature as the original. Think of it like... replacing a painting's canvas. You could recreate the image, but it wouldn't be the same painting your great-grandmother saw."

Finn's shoulders slumped. "So I really did ruin it."

"You broke it," the professor said, not unkindly. "But objects break, Mr. MacLeod. That's the nature of physical things. What matters is what we do afterward." He drew his wand. "Would you like me to repair the glass, at least? The crack can be completely mended. You won't even be able to see where it broke."

Finn looked at his friends. Mira nodded encouragingly. Zara squeezed his shoulder. Elliot gave him a small, supportive smile.

"Yes, please," Finn said quietly.

Professor Flitwick pointed his wand at the Remembrall and said clearly, "*Reparo.*"

The crack glowed golden for a moment, then the pieces of glass seemed to flow together like water, merging seamlessly. Within seconds, the surface was smooth and whole again, with no visible sign of damage. The Remembrall looked exactly as it had before—except for the notable absence of the magical smoke inside.

"There," Professor Flitwick said. "The physical damage is mended. As for the enchantment, I can teach you the spell to create new Remembrall smoke if you'd like, though it would take some practice. It's sixth-year charm work, but you're clearly a dedicated group of students."

"Thank you, Professor," Finn said, carefully picking up the now-repaired but still empty Remembrall. "I'll... I'll think about it."

"Take your time," Professor Flitwick said kindly. "And Mr. MacLeod? For what it's worth, accidents happen to everyone. I once dropped a priceless enchanted pocket watch that had belonged to my grandfather. Smashed it to pieces on a stone floor. I was devastated. But my grandfather's response taught me something important about the value of objects versus the value of the people who use them."

He patted Finn's arm—or rather, reached up and patted his elbow, given the height difference.

"Talk to your father," he advised. "I suspect he'll surprise you."

---

Writing the letter was one of the hardest things Finn had ever done.

He sat in the Hufflepuff common room late that night, long after his friends had reluctantly returned to their own dormitories. Parchment spread before him, quill in hand, he'd started and discarded four different versions.

*Dear Dad,*

*I have to tell you something, and I'm afraid you're going to be disappointed in me.*

No. Too dramatic.

*Dear Dad,*

*Something happened with the Remembrall.*

Too casual, like he was minimizing it.

*Dear Dad,*

*I'm so sorry.*

Just starting with an apology felt wrong too, like he was trying to deflect before even explaining.

Finally, on his fifth attempt, he just wrote honestly:

*Dear Dad,*

*Thank you for trusting me with Great-Grandmother's Remembrall. I was showing it to my friends tonight, and I accidentally dropped it. The glass cracked and the smoke escaped before I could catch it.*

*Professor Flitwick helped me repair the glass, so it looks whole again, but the enchantment is gone. He said it could be re-enchanted, but it wouldn't be the same as the original magic.*

*I know how much this heirloom means to our family. I know Great-Grandmother carried it every day, and Grandmother used it, and you used it when you were at school. I know it was supposed to be passed down to me someday, and I ruined it.*

*I'm so sorry. I understand if you're angry or disappointed. I was only trying to share something special with my friends, and I should have been more careful. I'll understand if you don't want to trust me with family heirlooms anymore.*

*I promise I'll take better care of it from now on. I'm sending it back with this letter so you can decide what you want to do with it.*

*Love,*
*Finn*

He read it over three times, each reading making his stomach twist tighter. Then, before he could second-guess himself, he carefully wrapped the mended Remembrall in its protective cloth, addressed the package to his father at the wandmaking shop in the Highlands, and tied everything together.

He'd send it with the morning post.

Sleep didn't come easily that night.

---

The response arrived two days later at breakfast.

Finn had been picking at his porridge, too anxious to eat properly despite Zara's attempts to distract him with stories from Quidditch practice. When the morning owls swept into the Great Hall, a familiar tawny owl landed directly in front of him, a letter tied to its leg and a small package clutched in its talons.

Finn's hands shook as he untied the letter. The package—he could tell from the weight and shape—was the Remembrall, being returned to him.

*Of course*, he thought miserably. *Dad doesn't even want me to keep it anymore.*

"Open it," Mira urged gently from across the table. She'd somehow materialized at the Hufflepuff table again, with Elliot beside her. All three of his friends were watching him with supportive, worried expressions.

Finn broke the seal and unfolded the parchment. His father's familiar handwriting sprawled across the page:

*Dear Finn,*

*First of all, breathe. I can practically feel your anxiety radiating from your letter, and I need you to know right away: I'm not angry, and I'm not disappointed.*

*Second, I'm sending the Remembrall back to you because it's YOURS now. Not "someday yours," not "on loan." Yours. The whole point of passing down family objects is for them to be used and lived with, not locked away in a drawer.*

*Now, let me tell you something I probably should have mentioned when I first sent it to you: that Remembrall has been dropped before. In fact, your great-grandmother dropped it at least twice that I know of from her journals—once on the Hogwarts Express and once in the Astronomy Tower. Your grandmother dropped it in Diagon Alley. I dropped it in the Great Hall during my fifth year. There's a reason wandmakers know good repair charms.*

*Each time it broke, whoever had it at the time was devastated, just like you. And each time, they learned the same lesson you're learning now: objects are precious, but they're not as precious as the people who use them. The memories attached to them matter more than their physical perfection.*

*That crack you're so worried about? Even though Professor Flitwick mended it beautifully, I'm sure—well, the thing is, son, those past breaks are part of the Remembrall's story now. My mother used to say that the value of an heirloom isn't in keeping it pristine. It's in the accumulated history of everyone who's held it, used it, maybe dropped it, and cared about it enough to fix it and keep going.*

*The Remembrall has been through four generations of our family now, including you. And yes, the original enchantment is gone, but so what? You can learn to re-enchant it, or you can keep it as is—a beautiful piece of glass that reminds you of your great-grandmother and all of us who came after. Either way, it's still ours. Still yours.*

*Your great-grandmother used to say that the best things in life are a little bit broken. They have history. They have stories. They've been loved and used and lived with, and that's what makes them valuable.*

*I love you, Finn. I'm proud of you. Not just for being careful with family heirlooms—though I know you are—but for being brave enough to share important things with your friends. For letting people in. That's harder and more important than keeping a glass sphere intact.*

*Whatever comes, whatever you need to tell me, whatever you're working through—I'm here. And I promise you, there's nothing you could break, drop, or damage that would change how much your mum and I love you.*

*Keep the Remembrall. Learn to re-enchant it if you want, or don't. Either way, it's part of your chapter in the family story now.*

*Love always,*
*Dad*

*P.S. Your mum says to tell you to eat properly and that she's sending more of those ginger biscuits you like.*

Finn read the letter twice, then a third time, his vision blurring with tears he didn't bother to hide. When he finally looked up, his friends were waiting patiently.

"He's not mad," Finn said, his voice thick. "He's not disappointed. He says—" He swallowed hard. "He says it's mine now. And that it's been broken before. And that..." He couldn't quite get the rest of the words out, but he didn't need to.

"That's good, right?" Zara asked, though she could clearly see the answer in his face.

"Yeah," Finn managed. "That's really good."

Elliot, who had been quiet through most of the ordeal, spoke up. "Your dad sounds like a good person."

"He is," Finn said. He looked down at the letter again, at the line that had hit him hardest: *Whatever comes, whatever you need to tell me, whatever you're working through—I'm here.*

It wasn't about the Remembrall, he realized. Or rather, it was about the Remembrall, but also about so much more. His dad was telling him, in that careful, loving way parents sometimes did, that mistakes were okay. That being imperfect was okay. That even if Finn broke something—or revealed something—that felt irreparable, there would still be love. Still acceptance.

*Whatever you need to tell me.*

Finn carefully refolded the letter and picked up the small package. He unwrapped the Remembrall, holding it up to the morning light streaming through the Great Hall's enchanted windows. The glass was perfect, seamlessly mended, beautiful even in its emptiness.

"Are you going to re-enchant it?" Mira asked.

Finn thought about it. "Maybe. Eventually. But I think..." He turned the Remembrall in his hands, watching the light catch and refract through the glass. "I think I like it like this for now. It reminds me of what Dad wrote. About things having history. About being a little bit broken and that being okay."

"That's very profound for before nine in the morning," Elliot said, but their tone was warm.

"I have my moments," Finn replied, managing a genuine smile.

Zara reached over and squeezed his hand. "You know what I think? I think your great-grandmother would be proud. Not because you kept it perfect, but because you cared enough to be devastated when it broke, and brave enough to tell your dad the truth, and smart enough to learn from it."

"And because you have good friends who'll sit in the library for hours researching repair charms," Mira added with a grin.

"Even when it's past dinnertime and we're all starving," Elliot said pointedly.

"Even then," Finn agreed, feeling warmth spread through his chest that had nothing to do with the porridge finally cooling in his bowl.

He slipped the Remembrall into his pocket, where it settled with a comfortable weight. Broken and mended. Empty but meaningful. Part of his story now.

And someday—maybe not today, maybe not even this year, but someday—when he was ready to share other truths that felt just as fragile and precious as an old glass sphere, he thought maybe it would be okay. Maybe his dad would respond with the same love and understanding he'd shown about a broken heirloom.

Maybe the best things in life really were a little bit broken. A little bit imperfect. But loved and accepted anyway.

"Come on," Zara said, standing and pulling him to his feet. "We're going to be late for Transfiguration, and McGonagall will give us that look if we run in after the bell."

"The look is terrifying," Mira agreed, gathering her books.

"Almost as terrifying as the lecture," Elliot added.

As they headed toward the doors of the Great Hall together—Finn flanked by his friends, the letter from his father tucked safely in his bag, the Remembrall a gentle weight in his pocket—he felt something ease in his chest. Something he hadn't even realized had been tight.

The fear of disappointing the people you loved was heavy. But the knowledge that they'd love you anyway, even when you made mistakes, even when you broke precious things, even when you were less than perfect—that was powerful.

That was magic of a different kind entirely.

Outside, the December morning was cold but bright, and Hogwarts gleamed under a fresh dusting of snow. The castle looked beautiful and ancient and enduring, and Finn thought about all the students who had walked these halls before him. All the people who had broken things and mended them, who had worried about disappointing their families, who had carried secrets and eventually found the courage to share them.

"Hey," Finn said suddenly, stopping his friends just before they reached the Transfiguration classroom. "Thank you. For helping with the Remembrall, and for... all of it. You didn't have to spend your whole evening researching repair charms."

"Of course we did," Mira said simply. "That's what friends do."

"We're a package deal at this point," Zara added with a grin. "Your problems are our problems."

"Terrifying thought," Elliot said, but they were smiling.

Professor McGonagall's voice drifted through the classroom door: "I'm waiting, and my patience has limits."

They exchanged quick glances and hurried inside, sliding into their seats just as the bell chimed. McGonagall gave them a look that suggested she knew exactly how close they'd cut it, but she simply turned to the blackboard and began the day's lesson.

Finn pulled out his notebook, but his hand drifted to his pocket, touching the smooth surface of the Remembrall through the fabric. Whole again. Changed, but whole.

Sometimes things broke. Sometimes you couldn't fix them exactly as they were. But that didn't mean they stopped being valuable or meaningful. It just meant their story had a new chapter.

And Finn was starting to think that maybe, just maybe, that was okay.

More than okay.

It was exactly how it was supposed to be.
