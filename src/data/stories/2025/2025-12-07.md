The Astronomy Tower was Mira's favorite place in the castle on clear nights, but tonight it felt more like a prison than a sanctuary.

She hunched over her star chart, quill scratching across parchment for what must have been the fourth time in as many hours. The brass telescope beside her was cold under her fingertips, and the December wind that whistled through the tower's open archways made her wish she'd brought a thicker cloak.

But she couldn't leave. Not yet. Not until she got this right.

"Cassiopeia, twenty-three degrees north-northwest," she muttered to herself, carefully marking the constellation's position. She checked her astronomy tables, cross-referenced the time, and then frowned. Was it twenty-three degrees, or twenty-four? The assignment only required accuracy within two degrees, but that wasn't the point. The point was to be precise. To be perfect.

She erased the mark and started again.

Behind her, the trapdoor opened with a soft creak. Mira didn't turn around—probably just a fifth-year coming to use one of the other telescopes. Most students had left hours ago, right after class ended, but there were always a few dedicated souls who stayed late to get better observations.

"Miss Patel?"

Mira jumped, her quill skittering across the parchment and leaving an ugly ink blot right across Andromeda. She spun around to find Professor Sinistra standing at the top of the ladder, a concerned expression on her sharp features.

"Professor! I didn't realize—I mean, I'm sorry, I know it's late—"

"It's past midnight," Professor Sinistra said mildly, stepping fully into the tower. She wore a thick traveling cloak over her robes, her dark hair pulled back in a practical bun. "Which means it's technically Sunday morning. How long have you been up here?"

Mira glanced at her chart, where she'd noted the time of her first observation. "Since... nine-thirty? Maybe ten?"

"Three hours, then." Professor Sinistra moved closer, looking over Mira's shoulder at the star chart. "And you've been working on this assignment the entire time?"

"I can't get it right," Mira admitted, her voice small. She gestured at the parchment with its multiple erasure marks and the fresh ink blot. "I keep plotting the positions, but something always feels off. The measurements should be more precise, and I think my angle calculations for Orion are wrong by half a degree, and now I've ruined the whole thing with this—" She pointed at the smudge.

Professor Sinistra was quiet for a moment, studying not the chart but Mira herself. Then she withdrew her wand and with a casual flick, conjured a comfortable-looking cushioned chair. Another flick produced a second chair and a steaming thermos that settled gently on the stone floor between them.

"Sit," the professor said, her tone leaving no room for argument.

Mira sat, confused but obedient. Professor Sinistra lowered herself into the other chair with a small sigh of relief, then poured two cups of what smelled like spiced tea.

"Drink," she said, handing one to Mira. "It's cold up here, and you look half-frozen."

The tea was perfect—hot and sweet with notes of cinnamon and something else Mira couldn't quite identify. She wrapped her hands around the cup gratefully, suddenly aware of how numb her fingers had become.

"Now," Professor Sinistra said, settling back in her chair and gazing out at the star-filled sky, "let me tell you a story."

---

"When I was in sixth year," the professor began, "I was determined to be the best Astronomy student Hogwarts had ever seen. Not just good—the absolute best. I wanted to discover a new constellation, publish a paper in _Celestial Studies_, and secure an apprenticeship with the most prestigious astronomers in Britain."

Mira found herself leaning forward, the forgotten star chart momentarily put aside.

"I stayed up here almost every night," Professor Sinistra continued. "I mapped the same sections of sky over and over, looking for the tiniest variations. I cross-referenced historical observations, calculated orbital mechanics by hand, and pushed myself to achieve a level of precision that was frankly... unnecessary."

She paused to sip her tea, her expression distant with memory.

"My grades were perfect. My observations were flawless. But I was exhausted. I stopped spending time with my friends. I barely ate. I would fall asleep during other classes because I'd been up all night tracking planetary movements. And the worst part?" She looked at Mira directly now. "I stopped enjoying it. The thing I loved most—studying the stars—became just another test to pass, another standard to meet."

Mira's throat felt tight. It was like Professor Sinistra was describing her own life, pulled forward twenty years.

"What happened?" she asked quietly.

"I collapsed during my Arithmancy N.E.W.T.," Professor Sinistra said bluntly. "Simply passed out from exhaustion. Madame Pomfrey kept me in the hospital wing for three days, and when I was released, Professor Dumbledore called me to his office."

The professor's lips curved into a slight smile at the memory.

"He didn't scold me. Instead, he asked me why I studied the stars. I told him I wanted to be the best. And he said something I've never forgotten: 'The stars don't judge us, Aurora. They simply are. There's wisdom in that acceptance.'"

She gestured up at the vast expanse of sky above them, where countless stars wheeled in their ancient patterns.

"The stars don't care if we map them perfectly or make small errors in our calculations. They continue their dance regardless. And the beauty of astronomy—the real beauty—isn't in perfect precision. It's in the wonder of understanding our place in something so vast and ancient that our tiny mistakes don't matter at all."

Mira looked down at her tea, her vision blurring slightly. "But if I don't do it perfectly, what's the point? If my work is just... adequate... then what does that say about me?"

"It says you're human," Professor Sinistra replied gently. "And that you're learning. Mira, do you know what your star chart from our first class this term looked like?"

Mira shook her head.

"It was lovely. Careful, thoughtful, accurate. You got every constellation within the required tolerance, and your notes were detailed and insightful." The professor paused. "You received an Outstanding. But you came to my office hours the next day to ask where you could have improved."

"I just wanted to do better," Mira said defensively.

"And that's admirable. Growth is important. But there's a difference between wanting to improve and believing that anything less than perfection means you've failed." Professor Sinistra leaned forward, her expression serious. "May I ask you something personal?"

Mira nodded, not trusting her voice.

"Are you afraid of failing, or are you afraid of not being enough?"

The question hit like a stunning spell. Mira's hands tightened around her teacup, and she found herself answering before she could think better of it.

"Both. Maybe. I don't know." She took a shaky breath. "I'm Muggle-born. My parents are proud of me, but they don't really understand this world. And sometimes I feel like I have to prove that I belong here. That I'm just as good as everyone else. Better, even. Because if I'm not... if I'm just average..."

"Then you're afraid people will think you don't deserve your place here," Professor Sinistra finished softly.

"Yes," Mira whispered.

The professor was quiet for a moment, then she stood and walked to the edge of the tower, looking out over the sleeping castle grounds. Snow covered the landscape in gentle undulating waves, and the Black Lake reflected starlight like a mirror.

"I was Muggle-born too," she said. "Did you know that?"

Mira's head snapped up. "No. I didn't."

"Not many people do. It doesn't come up in casual conversation." Professor Sinistra smiled over her shoulder. "But I remember that feeling. The constant need to prove myself. To show that I was just as talented, just as worthy as my pure-blood or half-blood classmates. That drive pushed me to excel, which wasn't all bad. But it also nearly destroyed me."

She returned to her chair, sitting down with a contemplative expression.

"Here's what I learned, though it took me years to truly understand it: Your worth isn't determined by your blood status or your grades or how perfectly you can plot a star chart. You belong at Hogwarts because you're magical, and you're curious, and you have a mind capable of grasping complex concepts. The fact that you care enough to stay up until midnight working on an assignment that isn't due for another week? That tells me you're already more than enough."

Mira felt something crack open in her chest—a pressure she'd been carrying for so long she'd forgotten it was there.

"But how do you stop?" she asked, her voice cracking slightly. "How do you stop needing to be perfect?"

Professor Sinistra's expression was kind but firm. "You practice. Every single day, you practice accepting 'good enough.' You catch yourself when you start spiraling into perfectionism, and you consciously choose to step back. It's hard. It goes against every instinct when you're someone who cares deeply about doing well. But it gets easier."

She reached over and gently took the ruined star chart from Mira's lap.

"Look at this," she said, studying it. "Your measurements are accurate. Your labeling is clear. Yes, there's an ink blot, but I can still read everything. This is good work, Mira. In fact, it's excellent work. The fact that it's not perfect doesn't diminish its value."

"But I could do better—"

"You could always do better. There's always another measurement to refine, another observation to make, another book to read. Excellence is a moving target, and perfection is impossible." Professor Sinistra handed the chart back. "The question is: at what point is the work good enough that continuing to refine it brings diminishing returns? At what point are you just exhausting yourself chasing an impossible standard?"

Mira looked down at her chart—at the careful notations, the precisely drawn constellation patterns, even the unfortunate smudge. She thought about the three hours she'd spent up here, the way her fingers had gone numb, how she'd missed dinner and evening socializing with her friends.

For what? For a half-degree of additional precision that her professor had just told her didn't matter?

"I think..." she said slowly, "I think maybe I've been up here long enough."

Professor Sinistra's smile was warm and proud. "I think you're right. And I think that chart is more than adequate for the assignment. In fact, I'll go ahead and grade it right now, if you'd like."

She pulled out her wand and a small grade book, making a quick notation.

"Outstanding," she said firmly. "For both the technical work and for the wisdom to know when to stop."

Mira felt tears prick at her eyes. "Thank you," she whispered.

"Don't thank me yet. I'm assigning you homework." Professor Sinistra's tone was light but serious. "Between now and our next class, I want you to do something imperfectly. Something where you consciously stop before it's as good as you could make it. And I want you to sit with the discomfort of that choice."

"That sounds terrible," Mira said, but she was almost smiling.

"It will be. But it's necessary practice." The professor stood, vanishing the chairs and thermos with a casual wave of her wand. "Now, let's get you back to Ravenclaw Tower before your friends send out a search party. It's well past curfew, but I think we can call this an extended lesson rather than breaking the rules."

---

They walked down the winding stairs together, the castle quiet and peaceful around them. When they reached the corridor where Professor Sinistra's office lay, the professor paused.

"Mira," she said, "I hope you know that you can come to me if you need to talk. Not just about Astronomy, but about... the rest of it. The pressure, the perfectionism, feeling like you have to prove yourself. I've been there. I understand."

"I will," Mira promised. "Thank you, Professor. For the tea, and the story, and... everything."

"You're welcome. Now go get some sleep. And remember—the stars will still be there tomorrow night, whether you map them perfectly or not."

Mira made her way back to Ravenclaw Tower, the eagle knocker's riddle barely registering as she answered it automatically. The common room was empty except for a few embers glowing in the fireplace. She climbed the spiral stairs to her dormitory, where her roommates were all fast asleep.

She changed into her pajamas quietly, then sat on her bed with her completed star chart in her lap. In the dim moonlight filtering through the window, the ink blot looked less like a disaster and more like... well, just an ink blot. A small imperfection in otherwise solid work.

Mira ran her finger along the edge of the parchment, thinking about Professor Sinistra's words. About the difference between excellence and perfection. About being enough.

She thought about the message in the frost from the beginning of the month: seek wonder in small things. Maybe that applied to her own work too. Maybe she could find wonder not just in the stars themselves but in her imperfect attempts to understand them.

It would take practice, like the professor had said. She couldn't just flip a switch and stop being a perfectionist. But she could try. She could catch herself when the spiraling started and make different choices.

Starting tomorrow. Or rather, today, since it was technically Sunday morning.

Mira carefully rolled up her star chart—ink blot and all—and set it aside. Then she lay down, pulling her blankets up to her chin, and looked at the ceiling where someone had charmed small points of light to mimic constellations.

They weren't perfectly accurate. She'd noticed that her first night in this dormitory. The angles were slightly off, and some of the stars were the wrong brightness. But they were still beautiful. Still magical.

Good enough, she thought, and felt something ease in her chest.

For the first time in longer than she could remember, Mira fell asleep without running through tomorrow's tasks or worrying about upcoming assignments. She just... slept.

And dreamed of stars that didn't judge, that simply were, wheeling through an infinite sky that had room for every kind of light—perfect and imperfect alike.

---

The next morning, Mira woke to sunlight streaming through the dormitory window and the sound of her roommates getting ready for breakfast. She felt surprisingly rested considering how late she'd been up.

When she made her way down to the Great Hall, she found her friends already gathered at their usual spot, this time at the Hufflepuff table near the doors.

"There she is!" Zara called out. "We missed you at dinner last night. Where were you?"

"Astronomy Tower," Mira said, sliding onto the bench beside Elliot. "Working on the star chart assignment."

"Until midnight?" Finn asked, passing her the jam without being asked. "That's dedication."

"Or obsession," Mira said wryly. "Professor Sinistra found me and gave me a very enlightening lecture on the difference."

"That sounds ominous," Elliot said, buttering their toast with precise strokes. "Everything okay?"

Mira considered the question as she prepared her own breakfast. Was everything okay? She'd had a minor crisis about her perfectionism, been told by a professor that she was exhausting herself for no good reason, and received an assignment to deliberately do something imperfectly.

But she'd also been seen. Understood. Given permission to be less than perfect.

"Yeah," she said, surprising herself with how much she meant it. "Everything's okay. Better than okay, actually."

"That's good," Finn said warmly. "You work too hard sometimes."

"Says the person who spent all of yesterday in Greenhouse Seven," Zara pointed out.

"That's different. That's fun."

"Your idea of fun is deeply concerning," Elliot said, but they were smiling.

"Says the person whose idea of fun is dueling," Finn shot back.

"Touché."

Mira listened to her friends banter, spreading jam on her toast with less precision than usual—deliberately letting it go slightly over the edge onto the plate. It felt wrong for about three seconds, then just felt... fine.

Good enough.

"So what are everyone's plans for today?" Zara asked, moving the conversation forward. "I've got Quidditch practice this afternoon, but I'm free this morning."

"Library," Mira said automatically, then paused. "Actually... maybe not. What else is there to do?"

Her friends stared at her.

"Did you just ask what else there is to do besides go to the library?" Elliot said slowly. "Are you feeling alright? Did Professor Sinistra hex you?"

"I'm serious," Mira said, ignoring the teasing. "I always go to the library on Sunday mornings. Maybe I should do something different."

"We could go for a walk around the lake," Finn suggested. "It's cold but clear. Nice day for it."

"Or we could have a snowball fight," Zara offered with a grin.

"Or we could do both," Elliot said. "Walk, snowball fight, and then hot chocolate in the village if we're ambitious."

Mira thought about her Transfiguration essay, which she'd planned to work on this morning. It wasn't due until Wednesday. She had time.

"Let's do it," she said, making the decision before she could second-guess herself. "All of it."

Zara's grin widened. "That's the spirit! Who are you and what have you done with our Mira?"

"I'm practicing," Mira said cryptically. "Professor Sinistra's homework."

"Homework that involves snowball fights and hot chocolate?" Finn looked impressed. "Best. Professor. Ever."

"It's more complicated than that," Mira said, but she was smiling. "I'll explain while we walk."

And she did. As the four of them bundled into their cloaks and scarves and made their way down to the lake, Mira told them about the conversation in the Astronomy Tower. About perfectionism and belonging and the weight of trying to prove yourself worthy.

Her friends listened—really listened—asking questions and sharing their own struggles. Finn talked about the pressure of coming from a wandmaking family, always feeling like he should have some special affinity for magical objects that he just didn't have. Zara mentioned the expectations that came with being from a pure-blood family with strong magical traditions. Elliot spoke quietly about their mother's accomplishments and the assumption that they'd follow the same path.

They were all carrying their own versions of "not enough," Mira realized. She wasn't alone in that feeling.

The snowball fight that followed was chaotic and joyful, with no clear winner and no one keeping score. Mira's throws were wildly inaccurate, and she didn't care. Elliot got snow down their collar and laughed about it. Finn constructed an elaborate snow fort that immediately collapsed, and Zara's competitive streak emerged but in a way that was playful rather than serious.

By the time they were too cold and wet to continue, they were all breathless with laughter.

"Hot chocolate?" Finn asked, snow still clinging to his hair.

"Definitely," Mira agreed.

As they trudged back toward the castle, leaving a trail of footprints in the snow, Mira felt something she hadn't felt in a long time: present. Not thinking about the next assignment or the next exam, but just here, in this moment, with her friends.

Good enough didn't mean giving up or not caring. It just meant knowing when to stop pushing and start living.

The stars would still be there tonight, whether she studied them or not. And her worth wasn't measured in perfect star charts or Outstanding grades, but in moments like this—messy, imperfect, and exactly right.

She looked up at the pale December sky, where stars were hiding behind the daylight, waiting for darkness to reveal them again. And she smiled.

Professor Sinistra was right. There was wisdom in acceptance.

And maybe, just maybe, Mira was starting to learn it.
