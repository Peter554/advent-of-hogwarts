The owl was two days late and very put out about it.

It came in over the Slytherin table at ten past eight on Monday morning, an eagle owl the colour of a wet slate roof, with a wingspan that cleared the candles by an inch and a parcel in its talons the size of a small suitcase. It circled once, like someone who had flown through three snowstorms and a customs inspection and wished it known. Then it landed on the end of the bench, sent a dish of kippers into a first-year's lap, and glared at Elliot as if the whole thing had been their idea.

"Bohumil," said Elliot. "You've put on weight."

The owl clicked its beak. In two years of acquaintance, this was the closest it had come to a laugh.

The parcel was wrapped in brown paper and tied with red string, the way only one person in Prague wrapped parcels: very tightly, with the knot in a particular place and a stub of sealing wax at each corner, as though the contents might make a break for it. The label said _For Elliot Novak, fifth year, Slytherin, Hogwarts. Please deliver by St Nicholas._

St Nicholas had been on Saturday. The snow over the North Sea, apparently, had other plans.

A good many people at the table had stopped eating to look. Elliot cut the string.

---

You have to understand about Mikuláš.

On the evening of the fifth of December, in Prague, every house with a child in it gets a knock on the door. It's three people. There is a tall grave bishop in a mitre and a white beard, who is St Nicholas. There is an angel in a cardboard halo, who carries the basket. And there is a devil, with horns, a chain and a sack, whose job is to look at the children as though he has read their files.

You stand in the hall and say your poem. If you've been good, you get an orange. If you've been middling, a walnut. If you've been dreadful, the devil lets you know about it, with great ceremony, in front of the neighbours.

In the Novak household the devil was Elliot's father. He had played the part for the first ten of Elliot's fifteen years, in a huge false nose, a fur coat from a theatre sale and a sack that clanked, and every year, very gravely, he had handed the same present to the same child, who had earned it by answering the bishop back. Since Hogwarts, the devil had been obliged to work by owl.

It was a lump of coal.

It was the size of a hen's egg, wrapped in gold foil, with a label in a dramatic hand: _For the sharpest tongue in Prague_. At six, Elliot had been furious. At nine they had understood it was a joke, and started leaving a cup of tea out for the devil, which he accepted with dignity, and which they had never mentioned to anyone at school and never would.

It lay at the top of the box, in its foil. Under it were three oranges in tissue, a bag of walnuts, and a tin of the little iced gingerbread hearts called _perníčky_, with a picture of a cat on the lid. A chocolate St Nicholas, slightly bent, wore a mitre of white marzipan. And at the very bottom, in a nest of straw, was a small hard parcel the size of a book, wrapped in plain paper, with a letter laid flat on top.

Elliot knew the writing: quick, upright and impatient, with a long tail on every _y_, the handwriting of someone who had never had to ask a question twice.

_Elliot,_

_Professor Slughorn has written to ask whether I might give a masterclass to your year on Saturday the 12th. I have said yes. I shall arrive on Friday evening. I am told there is a party which I cannot decently avoid._

_I hope this is convenient. I did not wish to arrive without notice. Your father has sent what he calls the usual. I did not stop him._

_Mama_

Elliot read it. Then they read it again, and a third time, elbows on the table, jaw set. It was an ordinary note. It had no unkind word in it. It had, if you were looking, a carefully worded _I hope this is convenient_, which in their mother's vocabulary was roughly a bunch of flowers. But there were things it didn't say, and they stood around the sentences like furniture in a dark room.

They turned it over. Nothing on the back.

The small hard parcel, when they unwrapped it, was a drawing.

---

It was in pencil, on cheap paper, in a plain wooden frame, done in the quick, slightly crooked line of an architect who drew all day for money and in the evenings for fun. It showed the kitchen at home: the table with the scorch mark at one end, the window over the sink with the geranium that never died, the dresser with its row of cups. A fat orange cat was asleep on the radiator.

It was so exactly right that for a moment Elliot could smell it, coffee and wet wool and whatever their mother had left on the back burner to cool.

On the dresser, a little way in from the edge, stood a photograph in a frame. It was only a small rectangle with a few pencil lines inside it. But Elliot had stood in front of it, in their head, often enough to know what it was.

They turned the frame over. On the back, in a thick, blunt, cheerful hand, someone had written:

_Your mother took the Yule Ball photograph out of the drawer last Monday and put it on the dresser. She has not said anything. She has not stopped looking at it._

_Tata_

_P.S. The cat misses you. The cat is a liar._

Elliot sat very still.

Around them the table had begun to notice. A fourth-year across the bench was eyeing the gingerbread with open longing. Two sixth years had stopped arguing about Quidditch. At the far end, a small second-year with a stack of books beside his plate, who had been trying hard not to look, glanced up and then, in confusion, down.

Elliot, who could not at that moment have said a sentence on any subject, did the only thing they could think of.

"Take some," they said.

---

They did it properly. They passed the tin of gingerbread down the bench. They peeled an orange and divided it, section by section, round the table. The chocolate St Nicholas was cut up with a butter knife and given out in splinters, and the walnuts were cracked on the underside of a plate. Somebody asked what it was all for and Elliot said "Saint's day" in a voice that didn't invite follow-up, and somebody else said "Cheers, Novak" with their mouth full, and a wave of cheerful, sugary noise rose up and covered everything over.

Ansel, the second-year, got a whole heart. He took it as though he had been handed a medal.

Elliot gave away all of it. They kept only the coal, in its gold foil, in the pocket of their robe, and did not eat anything at all.

Across the Hall, a girl with long braids had turned in her seat at the Gryffindor table. She raised her eyebrows and mimed, with both hands, the shape of a very large owl. Elliot rolled their eyes from habit, and did it so well that she grinned and turned back, and nothing more was said.

It was a small, ordinary kindness, and it was all they could have borne.

---

They didn't write the reply until the evening.

They did a double period of Charms. They spent an hour in the library on a Transfiguration essay that came out fluent and polished and completely empty. They had dinner. They did the rounds of the common room like someone checking the house was locked. At a quarter to ten, with the dormitory quiet and the other curtains drawn, they sat on their bed, pulled the trunk out from underneath, and reached down through the socks and spare ink to the bottom, where a flat envelope lay in the dark.

It was addressed, in a younger, rounder hand, to _Mama_.

They hadn't opened it for a year. It was two pages, both sides, written in the week after the Yule Ball in one furious night, read over four times, and judged impossible to send. It began: _I have to tell you something and I do not know how to make it sound like the right sort of news_. It went on: _I don't think I want to be a potioneer. I think I might never have wanted to_.

They read the first page. It was awful. It was honest and polite and full of the over-reasonable language you use when you're bracing for a blow, with a line about _a sense of duty_ and another about _the family reputation_.

They didn't read the second.

It wasn't the letter's fault. It had been the right letter, a year ago, to the person Mama had been then, or that Elliot had thought she was. But now there was a photograph on a dresser in Prague. There was a drawing propped on their bed, with a cat on a radiator. And a stiff, short, careful note, in a hand that never asked anything twice, had asked.

_I hope this is convenient._

They folded the letter along its old creases, put it back in its envelope, and put the envelope back in the trunk. They didn't throw it away. It was a true thing. It was only the wrong one for now.

Then they took a clean sheet of parchment from the desk, propped the drawing on the windowsill where the light would catch it, and sat for a long time with the pen held an inch above the page.

---

The Owlery at half past ten was cold and black and full of the soft rustle of several hundred birds asleep.

Bohumil had been given a stall in the far corner to himself, where he'd been spoilt. Somebody had left him a dish of mice and a bit of sausage, and he sat on his perch with his feathers puffed out like a pillow, heavier than ever and quite satisfied.

"You'll fly back tonight," said Elliot.

The owl blinked, slowly, one eye at a time.

"I know. Sorry. It's only a short one."

It was a single line on a single scrap, folded small. They had written it, torn it up, written it again, torn that up as well, and then, at the bottom of the third attempt, simply done it. They tied it to Bohumil's leg with a bit of red string from the parcel, in the knot their mother always used, tight, with the end tucked under.

As they did, a small warmth pressed against their hip. The grey cloth bag at their side was glowing through the weave, a low amber light that threw a gold shadow on the straw, and on the owl's great round feet, and on the knot.

They stared at it.

"You're not _watching_," they said to the owl.

Bohumil looked at them, unblinking.

"No. You're an owl. It doesn't count owls."

The lantern burned on, bright enough to read by. They read the note once more in its light before pulling the knot tight. It said:

_Yes. Come. —E._

They lifted their arm. Bohumil gave a grunting hoot, shifted his weight and stepped on, and Elliot carried him across the straw to the open window, where the night wind came in clean and cold over the snowy fields with the stars thick in it.

"Go on."

The owl went with a hard clap of wings. For a moment it hung against the stars, a huge round shadow, and then it banked and turned south-east, towards the sea and a kitchen with a geranium, and was gone.

Elliot watched until they couldn't see it any more. Then they went back to the dormitory, put the drawing on the wall by their bed, and went to sleep.
