The owl was two days late and very put out about it.

It came in over the Slytherin table at ten past eight on Monday morning, a vast, bad-tempered eagle owl the colour of a wet slate roof, with a wingspan that cleared the candles by an inch and a parcel in its talons the size of a small suitcase. It circled once, with the air of someone who had flown through three snowstorms and a customs inspection and wished it known. Then it came down on the end of the bench, sending a dish of kippers into a first-year's lap, and glared at Elliot as if the whole of it had been their idea.

"Bohumil," said Elliot. "You've put on weight."

The owl clicked its beak. This was, in two years of acquaintance, the closest it had ever come to a laugh.

The parcel had been wrapped in brown paper and tied with red string, in the way that only one person in Prague wrapped parcels, which was to say very tightly, with the knot in a particular place and a stub of sealing wax at each corner, as though the contents might make a break for it. There was a label. _For Elliot Novak, fifth year, Slytherin, Hogwarts. Please deliver by St Nicholas._

St Nicholas had been on Saturday. The snow over the North Sea, apparently, had other plans.

A good many people at the table had stopped eating to look. Elliot cut the string.

---

You have to understand about Mikuláš.

On the evening of the fifth of December, in Prague, in every house that has a child in it, there is a knock on the door. It's three people. There is a tall, grave bishop in a mitre and a white beard, who is St Nicholas. There is an angel, in a cardboard halo, who carries the basket. And there is a devil, with horns, a chain and a sack, whose job it is to look at the children as though he has read their files.

You stand in the hall and say your poem. If you're good, you get an orange. If you're middling, you get a walnut. If you've been dreadful, the devil lets you know it, with great ceremony, in front of the neighbours.

In the Novak household, the devil had been Elliot's father. He had played the part for every one of Elliot's fifteen years, with an enormous false nose, a fur coat from a theatre sale and a sack that clanked, and he had every year, very gravely, handed over the same present to the same child, who had every year earned it by answering the bishop back.

It was a lump of coal.

It was always the same. It was the size of a hen's egg and wrapped in gold foil, and there was always a label, in a dramatic hand: _For the sharpest tongue in Prague_. At six, Elliot had been furious. At nine they had understood it was a joke. At twelve they'd started leaving a cup of tea out for the devil, which he accepted with great dignity, and which they had never mentioned to anyone at school, and never would.

It was there now, at the top of the box, in its foil. Under it lay oranges, three, wrapped in tissue. A bag of walnuts. A great quantity of gingerbread, the little iced hearts called _perníčky_, in a tin with a picture of a cat. A chocolate St Nicholas, slightly bent, with a mitre made of white marzipan. And at the very bottom, in a nest of straw, a small hard parcel the size of a book, wrapped in plain paper, with a letter laid flat on top.

Elliot knew the writing. Everybody at Hogwarts, in the end, would have known it: a quick, upright, impatient script with a long tail on every _y_, the writing of someone who had never in her life had to ask a question twice.

_Elliot,_

_Professor Slughorn has written to ask whether I might give a masterclass to your year on Saturday the 12th. I have said yes. I shall arrive on Friday evening. I am told there is a party which I cannot decently avoid._

_I hope this is convenient. I did not wish to arrive without notice. Your father has sent what he calls the usual. I did not stop him._

_Mama_

Elliot read it.

Then they read it again, and a third time, with their elbows on the table and their jaw set. It was, on the face of it, a perfectly ordinary note. It had no unkind word in it. It had, if you were looking for it, a very small, carefully worded _I hope this is convenient_, which in their mother's vocabulary was roughly the equivalent of a bunch of flowers. But there were certain things it did not say, and they stood around the sentences like furniture in a dark room.

They read it a fourth time, and turned it over, and there was nothing on the back.

The small hard parcel, when they unwrapped it, was a drawing.

---

It was a pencil drawing, on cheap paper, in a plain wooden frame, done in the quick, light, slightly crooked line of an architect who drew all day for money and in the evenings for fun. It showed a kitchen. It was the kitchen at home. The table with the scorch mark at one end; the window over the sink with the geranium that never died; the dresser, with its row of cups; the stove. A cat, fat and orange, was asleep on the radiator.

It was exactly right. It was so exactly right that for a moment Elliot could smell it, the coffee and wet wool and the faint sweet smell of whatever their mother had left on the back burner to cool.

On the dresser, in the corner of the drawing, a little way in from the edge, there was a photograph in a frame. It wasn't clearly drawn. It was only a small rectangle, with a few suggestive pencil lines inside it. But Elliot had stood in front of it, in their head, often enough to know what it was.

They turned the frame over. On the back of the paper, in a thick, blunt, cheerful hand, someone had written:

_Your mother took the Yule Ball photograph out of the drawer last Monday and put it on the dresser. She has not said anything. She has not stopped looking at it._

_Tata_

_P.S. The cat misses you. The cat is a liar._

Elliot sat very still.

Around them, the table had begun to notice. A fourth-year across the bench was looking at the gingerbread with undisguised longing. A pair of sixth years had stopped arguing about Quidditch. At the far end, a small second-year with a stack of books beside his plate, who had been trying with great concentration not to look, glanced up and then, in confusion, down.

And Elliot, who at that moment could not have said a single sentence on any subject, did the only thing they could think of.

"Take some," they said.

---

They did it properly. They opened the tin of gingerbread and passed it down the bench, and they peeled one of the oranges and divided it, section by section, round the whole of the table. The chocolate St Nicholas was cut up with a butter knife and given out in splinters. The walnuts were cracked on the underside of a plate. Somebody asked what it was all for and Elliot said "Saint's day," in a voice that didn't invite follow-up, and somebody else said "Cheers, Novak," with their mouth full, and a wave of cheerful, sugary noise rose up round them and covered everything over.

Ansel, the second-year, got a whole heart. He took it as though he had been handed a medal.

Elliot gave away all of it. They kept the coal, in its gold foil, in the pocket of their robe; and they did not eat anything at all.

Across the Hall, at the Gryffindor table, a girl with long braids had turned in her seat to look. She raised her eyebrows, and mimed, with both hands, the shape of a very large owl. Elliot, from habit, rolled their eyes. They did it so well that she grinned, and turned back, and nothing more was said.

It was a small, ordinary kindness, and it was all they could have borne.

---

They didn't write the reply until the evening.

They did a double period of Charms. They did an hour in the library on a Transfiguration essay that came out as fluent, polished and completely empty. They had dinner. They did the rounds of the common room in the way of a person making sure the house is locked. And at a quarter to ten, when the dormitory was quiet and the other curtains were drawn, they sat on their bed and took the trunk out from underneath, and lifted the lid, and reached down through the layers of socks and spare ink to the bottom, where a flat envelope lay in the dark.

It was addressed, in a younger, rounder hand, to _Mama_.

They hadn't opened it for a year. It was two pages, both sides. They had written it in the week after the Yule Ball, one night, in a sort of white-hot state, and had read it over four times, and decided that they couldn't. It began: _I have to tell you something and I do not know how to make it sound like the right sort of news_. It went on: _I don't think I want to be a potioneer. I think I might never have wanted to_.

They read the first page. It was awful. It was very honest, and very polite, and full of the sort of defensive, over-reasonable language you use when you're bracing for a blow. It had a line in it about _a sense of duty_. It had another about _the family reputation_.

They didn't read the second.

It wasn't the letter. That was what they understood, sitting there with the paper on their knee and the lamplight on the quilt. It had been the right letter to write, a year ago, to the person that Mama had been then, or that Elliot had thought she was. But there was a photograph on a dresser in Prague. There was a drawing on their bed, in a plain wooden frame, with a cat on a radiator. And a note, a stiff, short, careful note, in a hand that never asked anything twice, had asked.

_I hope this is convenient._

They folded the letter along its old creases, and put it back in the envelope, and put the envelope back in the trunk. They didn't throw it away. It was a true thing. It was only that it was the wrong one for now.

Then they took a clean sheet of parchment from the pile on the desk, and propped the drawing on the windowsill where the light would catch it, and sat for a long time with the pen held an inch above the page.

---

The Owlery, at half past ten, was cold, and black, and full of the soft, wet, rustling sound of several hundred birds asleep.

Bohumil had been put up in the far corner, in a stall by himself, where he'd been spoilt: somebody had left him a dish of mice, and a bit of sausage, and he was sitting on his perch with his feathers puffed out like a pillow, looking weightier than ever, and quite satisfied.

"You'll fly back tonight," said Elliot.

The owl blinked, very slowly, one eye at a time.

"I know. Sorry. It's only a short one."

It was. It was a single line, on a single scrap, folded small. They had written it, and torn it up, and written it again, and torn that up as well, and then, at the bottom of the third attempt, had simply done it. They tied it to Bohumil's leg with a bit of red string from the parcel, in a knot of the sort their mother always did, tight, with the end tucked under.

And as they did, there was a small warmth against their hip. They glanced down. The grey cloth bag at their side was glowing, through the weave, with a low, even, amber light, and it threw a gold shadow on the straw, and on the owl's great round feet, and on the knot.

They stared at it.

"You're not _watching_," they said to the owl.

Bohumil looked at them, unblinking.

"No. You're an owl. It doesn't count owls."

The lantern burned on, a low gold flame in the dark Owlery, bright enough to read by. They read the note once more, in its light, before they tied it off. It said:

_Yes. Come. —E._

They lifted their arm. Bohumil gave a sort of grunting hoot, shifted his weight, and stepped on, and Elliot carried him across the straw to the open window, where the night wind was coming in clean and cold over the snowy fields, with the stars thick in it and not a cloud to be seen.

"Go on."

The owl took off with a great hard clap of wings. For a moment it hung there, a huge round shadow against the stars, and then it banked, and turned to the south-east, towards the sea and a kitchen with a geranium, and was gone.

Elliot watched until they couldn't see it any more. Then they went back to the dormitory, and put the drawing on the wall by their bed, and went to sleep.
