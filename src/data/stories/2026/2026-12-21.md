The owl landed in the porridge.

It was a tawny with a bent left feather, and it arrived at eight o'clock on Monday morning, in the middle of the breakfast rush, with a letter in its beak and a look of grim, personal triumph. It skidded down the length of the Hufflepuff table, scattering a dish of kippers and half a rack of toast, and came to a halt in front of Finn with its wings spread like a man who has been told he's late and means to make a point of it.

"Don't," said Oliver. "Don't look at it. If you look at it, it'll change."

"It's not going to change."

"You don't know that."

"It's a letter, Ollie."

"It's a letter that's been in an owl for two days," said Oliver, who had gone slightly white around the nose and was holding his own spoon in a grip like a dagger. "I think we should wait. I think we should have a, a cup of tea, and some toast, and think about whether we're ready."

Finn looked at him. Ever since Friday morning, when he had watched a small brown shape grow smaller across the grey lawn, he had felt like a man at the edge of a pond in the dark who has let go of a stone and is waiting for the splash. He had been, on the whole, remarkably calm: a thin, bright, brittle calm that he'd kept very still all weekend, for fear it would break.

He picked up the letter. It was his father's hand: the quick, tilting scrawl, like a boy late for a bus, with a smear of sawdust on the corner of the envelope. He broke the seal.

_Dear Finn,_

_I read your letter at the bench, standing up, with a plane in my other hand, and I'd like you to know that I put the plane down first._

_I am not going to answer slowly. You were right about wands and wrong about this: I knew what I wanted to say before I got to the bottom of the first page._

_The shop will keep._

_I want you to hear that in my voice, so I've written it on a line by itself. The bench will keep. The rowan will keep, it's been drying eleven years and it can dry a few more. The first-years will keep coming, because they always do, and I've managed thirty Julys without a second pair of hands and I dare say I could manage a thirty-first. I'd be a poor sort of wandmaker if I hadn't learned that a thing doesn't have to be used at once to be worth having._

_What I'd have been sorry for is you standing at that bench next summer because you didn't know how to tell me you'd rather be in a fen. I'd have found out by about August. I'd never have forgiven myself._

_Go and see the Gurdyroots. Tell me what they say. I want to hear everything, and so does your mother, who has already drawn a map of Cambridgeshire on the back of the shopping list and put a cross on Ely._

_Bring Oliver. Bring warm socks, both of you. The heating has gone again._

_Love, Dad._

_P.S. Your mum says: wish on the first star tonight, but not for yourself. She says you'll know why._

He read it to the end.

Then he put it down, very carefully, beside his plate, and looked up at the enchanted ceiling, which was a pale, bright, fretful blue with a thin sliver of moon in it, and found, to his enormous embarrassment, that he couldn't see it clearly.

"Finn?" said Oliver.

"It's fine." His voice had gone peculiar. "It's good. He says the shop will keep. He's put it on a line by itself."

"Oh," said Oliver, and went limp. He put his head down on the table among the kippers. "Oh, thank _goodness_."

"He wants us to bring warm socks."

"I've got three pairs. I got them in October."

"Why?"

"I had a feeling," said Oliver, into the toast.

---

He grinned all through breakfast. He couldn't help it. It sat on his face as though somebody had pinned it there, and when, at twenty past eight, three people he'd rather expected arrived from three different directions at once, he was too far gone to be dignified about it.

"You've got a face on," said Zara, at a jog.

"He says the shop will keep."

"_Finn_." She had her arms round him before he could stand, and lifted him an inch off the bench, and he heard her say into his shoulder, "I _knew_. I did the voice and everything."

"It was a terrible voice," said Elliot, on his other side, with their head tilted and an expression very like pride. They gripped his arm above the elbow, quite hard. "It did the job, though. Good."

Mira read the letter standing at his shoulder, with her lips moving on the longer words. At the end she said nothing at all. She handed it back, and then, in a gesture none of them had expected, least of all herself, she leaned down and put her arms round his neck and held on.

It was the first time in nearly three months that all four of them had been at the Hufflepuff table together. They'd come across the Hall without a word, from three different houses, as if the way had always been known, and Zara had begun to eat Oliver's toast, and Elliot had put their feet on the bench.

He glanced, once, along the table. The chair at the end of the bench was still pulled out, a hand's breadth from the board. Nobody had sat in it. Nobody, he thought, had quite known they could.

---

The Lantern Walk began at four.

It was dusk already. In the north of Scotland, on the twenty-first of December, there are about six hours between the sun's coming up and its going down, and they are not generous ones: a long, low, level, yellow light, that never gets properly overhead, and a short, grey, glimmering afternoon that slides into evening almost before you've noticed. By half past three, the sky over the grounds had turned the deep tender colour of a dove's breast. By four, the first stars were out.

They had been told to assemble in the Entrance Hall. They stood in it, in the great, gold, echoing, torch-lit space, under the banner, in a long and rather subdued line: eighty-one fifth years in their winter cloaks, in rows of three, each holding, in front of them, in both hands, a brass lantern with a flame in it.

It was the oddest thing he had ever seen.

No two were alike. That was what struck him, standing there with his own warm gold burning in his hands. There were reds and blues and greens, pale lilacs, a startling white; a vivid, uncompromising purple held well away from its owner, a very small Gryffindor, with an expression of faint alarm; a Slytherin boy's flame as black as ink and rimmed with silver; Isla Brennan's deep rose; Callum Reyes's dazzling silver, at which he was still staring as if it had spoken to him.

And in the midst of them, side by side, four he knew: orange-red, amber, gold, and, at the end, small and clear and pale, a quiet flame the colour of a winter morning.

"Don't look at me," said Mira, from beside him. She was looking straight ahead. "I haven't said anything."

"I didn't say a thing."

"You were going to."

"I was going to say it's the nicest blue I've seen," said Finn.

She did not answer. But her chin went up, a little, and she was smiling.

"They're lined up," said Zara, on his other side, in a stage whisper. "Look. We're the only ones who match."

"We don't match."

"We match _differently_."

At the top of the stairs, in her long green cloak, with her hat pinned down against the wind and a lantern of her own held in her left hand, a small, plain, steady yellow, Professor McGonagall lifted her right.

"Fifth years," she said. "Forward."

---

It was nearly a mile from the castle to the lake, and the whole of it was lit.

They went out by the great doors in the long file, in silence, and as they came down the steps into the cold, a hush came over the grounds that Finn felt on the back of his neck. The air was crystal. The snow lay in great pale drifts on either side of the path, and the sky above was a vast dark bowl of blue, shot through with stars, beginning at its northern edge to flush a deep and gentle green.

Elliot's pennants stood along the path on tall poles of pale ash, six of them, curving down the long white slope towards the water. They were long and swallow-tailed, and each had been painted, quickly, with a single lantern: red, gold, apple-green, violet, white, and, at the very turn of the path, where the lake lay open like a sheet of pewter, a thin, clear, pale blue.

He heard Elliot, behind him, draw in a breath. They had not said a word all the way down. But Finn, who had watched them for five years, knew precisely where their eyes had gone. He didn't look round. He only moved his shoulder an inch, until it touched Elliot's arm, and kept walking.

Behind them, the school came down the hill: the whole of Hogwarts, three hundred and forty of them, in cloaks and scarves, each holding a plain wax candle in a jam jar, in a long, wide, slow river of warm gold. He saw Amara and Wren together in a bunch of first years, with their heads together, and Wren looking not at her candle but at the coloured flames ahead, with her eyes enormous.

He saw Priya, among the Slytherins, with a candle in each hand, one for her, one for somebody who was walking in front. He saw Professor Sprout, in her patched hat, quite alone, at the back, holding her candle in the crook of her arm and looking up at the stars. He saw a great dark shape, enormous in the middle of the procession, with a lantern the size of a bucket, and a very large dog.

And, beside him, in the starlight, with his sandy hair standing up and his nose pink and a small flame of a very clear, very ordinary, very lovely yellow held in front of him, like something he'd been given and had not yet dared believe in, was Oliver.

"What's yours?" said Finn, quietly.

"I told you. It's a sandwich."

"It's a very nice colour."

"I think," said Oliver, "it might be the shop. I mean, the idea of it. I've been wondering for weeks what I'd say, and it turns out—" He stopped. He glanced sideways, and went a little pinker. "It turns out I just want to go."

"You can go."

"I know. I'm going." He nodded, to himself, with great seriousness. "That's what it's for, I think."

---

They stopped at the water's edge.

It was a low bank, with a few black reeds sticking up, frozen into a sheet of silver, and beyond it the lake lay, wide and white and utterly silent, with the long dark line of the Forest on the far shore and the stars lying in it, reflected, like scattered coins. A faint green glow was coming up in the north. The cold had a clean sharp edge to it, like the edge of a knife, and his breath went out in front of him, in a slow pale cloud, and was gone.

McGonagall stood at the very edge, with her lantern held out in front of her. She did not speak for a long time. When she did, it was quietly, in a voice that carried perfectly to the back, and she did not, for once, use any of the words that anyone had expected.

"This is the longest night," she said. "That is all. It is not an occasion of any great mystery. It is simply the night when the dark is longest. And it has been the custom in this school, from time to time, for those who are about to be examined to carry a light to the lake and set it down, and to remember that after tonight the days begin to grow." She paused. "I have never found it necessary to add to that."

She bent. She set her lantern, a small plain yellow flame, on the ice.

It sat there, on the frozen surface, perfectly steady, throwing a round pool of warm light on the silver.

"Fifth years," said McGonagall.

They went down one at a time.

It was a very quiet thing. There was no music. Each stepped out onto the ice, a yard, or two, in the dark, and set down their lantern, and stepped back, and a small new fire stood on the lake. A red. A green. A violet. Callum Reyes's silver. A black one, with a rim. They went on and on, in a long line, going out across the ice, each one carefully spaced from the last, until the whole frozen water in front of the bank was strewn with small brilliant islands of colour, quivering and glowing and reflecting upward on the faces of the people who watched.

He saw the four of them go at once.

It wasn't arranged. They had somehow come to the edge together, in a line, with their lanterns held in front of them, and Zara had put out her hand, and found his, and they stepped out onto the ice together, in step, into the dark.

They set them down in a row.

Orange-red. Gold. Amber. Blue.

And in the great, cold, silent night, under the blazing stars, with the long ranks of Hogwarts behind them and the coloured fires in front, they straightened, and stood, shoulder to shoulder, looking out.

---

"Mira," said Finn. "Have you got it?"

She had. She took it out of her inside pocket, the old green-covered book from last year, with its frayed corners and its blotted spine, and she held it a moment, in both gloved hands, as she would have held a bird. She had not brought a pen. She had brought, instead, a small bottle of dark green ink, and a quill, and she knelt, on the cold ice, among the lanterns, in the glow, and turned to the last of the written pages.

It was the one with the pledge. They had all signed it, the year before, in a different ink. There were four names, and a date, and a sentence in her tidy, rounded, eager hand, which Finn could read from where he stood:

_On the longest night of every year, we will light a candle, share a meal, and wish for each other. Never for ourselves._

"Do we say it?" said Zara.

"We said it last year."

"We should say it again."

They did. They said it standing, in the dark, in a ragged, uneven half-whisper, with their breath going up, and Finn, as he said it, was aware, in some deep warm place, that he had never meant a sentence more. He looked at the others. They were not looking at him. They were looking, each of them, at the small steady light of the one beside them.

"Right," said Mira. "I'll go first. If that's all right."

"Go on."

She straightened. She looked at Finn. She had got, he saw, rather pink round the eyes, in the cold, and she put a hand up to her glasses, and let it fall.

"I wish for Finn," said Mira, "a July that is exactly as good as he's afraid it won't be."

It went through him like a spoon of hot soup. He opened his mouth, and found that he'd nothing at all to say. Zara's hand tightened on his.

"I wish for Mira," said Zara, in her low, clear, ringing voice, "one whole day with nothing in it. Not a list. Not a plan. Just a day." She gave a crooked grin. "I'll be watching."

"You'll be—"

"I'll _know_."

Elliot had stepped forward. They stood very upright in the starlight, with their hands deep in their pockets and their chin up, and the dry, composed, defended look of a person delivering an ironic toast. "I wish for Zara," they said, "a Snitch that behaves. And a captain who listens, and a team that tells her when she's wrong." A pause. The corner of their mouth moved. "And ten more points, on a Sunday."

"Ten _more_—"

"It's a very particular wish."

Finn laughed. It came out of him all of a piece, and he saw it go down the line, from face to face, in the dark.

It was his turn.

He had known what he would say. He had known it for days. It had been waiting in him, quiet, like a seed in a cold frame, ready for the day, and he looked across at the tall figure with the amber light in front of them, and the paint-stained cuff, and the face that was so carefully, so painfully held, and said it, plainly, as he said everything.

"I wish for Elliot," said Finn, "that they'd sign it in the middle."

There was a very long silence.

"The banner," he said. "The pennants. It's—you always put it in the corner. I'd like you to put it in the middle, one day. In big letters. I'd like you to be the sort of person who just does."

Elliot did not say anything. They stood quite still. And then, quite slowly, in the light of four lanterns, they turned their head away, and put a gloved hand to their face.

"That's a good one," they said, at last, rather thickly. "That's a very—that's rather a good one."

"Do you want—" said Mira, anxiously.

"I'm fine," said Elliot. "It's cold. It's the cold."

"It's the cold," said Zara, gravely.

---

It was Finn who said it.

He hadn't meant to. They'd got to the end. The book lay open on Mira's knee, in the glow of the lanterns, with the green ink drying on the page, and she was holding the quill above the line, and for a minute nobody spoke. There was a hush in the air. A small, soft, faint, clear sound was drifting up the hill, from somewhere among the first years, so quiet that it might have been the wind: a thin, high, wavering voice, singing, without words.

"There's a thing," said Finn.

They looked at him.

"It's silly. It's not for the pact, exactly. It's only that, on the first of December, I came in to breakfast, and there was a chair." He swallowed. "At the end of the bench. At the Hufflepuff table. It used to be yours, Zara. You'd turn up halfway through and sit in it, and take my toast. And it was pushed in. It was just—sitting there. And I thought: it's wrong. Somebody should sit in that."

Nobody said anything.

"So I pulled it out," said Finn. "A bit. About this far. And nobody sat in it. For three weeks. Nobody's sat in it, even this morning, and you were all there." He rubbed the back of his neck. "I thought, I don't know. I thought if I left it out, somebody would. And I think I've been looking at it for so long that I've stopped being able to tell whether it's—whether it was for you, or for someone else, or just a chair. But I'd like it to be for somebody."

He stopped. His face was hot, in the cold.

Zara had got very still. She was looking at him with her mouth a little open, and an expression on her face that he'd seen only once, on a Sunday morning, in a doorway.

"I thought it was Oliver's," she said, in a small voice.

"It isn't Oliver's."

"I thought it was reserved. I walked past it three times. I thought you'd saved it for somebody _else_."

"I'd saved it for—" He began to laugh, and stopped, and it turned, halfway through, into something else. "I'd saved it for whoever needed it."

"One chair," said Mira, quietly.

"What?"

"One chair. Always." She had the quill between her fingers. She wasn't looking at the page. She was looking at him, with her whole dark, steady, shining face, and she said it as she said all her best things, slowly, as though she'd been turning it over for a long time. "Left empty. For whoever needs it."

She bent. In the glow of the four coloured fires, on the ice, in the middle of the longest night, she wrote it, in green ink, in a clear small hand, at the bottom of the pledge:

_One chair always empty, for whoever needs it._

She signed. She passed the quill. They signed, in order, one at a time, beneath the date, on their knees on the lake, with the lights burning round them and the stars gone green behind: _M.P. F.M. Z.O. E.N._

Then, because they did not seem to be able to do anything else, they stayed there, close, in the shining dark, with their shoulders touching and their breath going up, and looked out at the water.

---

It was quite dark by the time they went back.

The procession had broken up. They went up the hill in twos and threes, in the starlight, with their jam-jar candles, laughing and murmuring, and the lanterns they had left, a long, faint, jewelled line of them, strung out across the ice, were quite alone on the lake. Hagrid would fetch them in the morning, with a sledge. They'd said so. They would lie there all night, in the cold, shining, in the black, burning on until the first thin light, in a long, bright, silent trail from the shore to the middle of the water.

Finn was the last to leave.

He stood at the edge of the bank for a moment, in the snow, with his hands in his pockets and his breath going up. Oliver was waiting for him, further up the path, with a candle, stamping his feet. The others had gone ahead. Their voices drifted back, small and bright, down the hill.

He looked at the lake.

It was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen. It lay in front of him, wide and still and silver, with the ice gleaming under the stars and the long line of lights going out across it: red and violet and green, silver and rose and black, and in the middle, side by side, a small steady row of four. They did not waver. They did not flicker. They burned on, in the great cold dark, on the longest night of the year, like something that had never in its life been afraid.

And above them, in the north, quite suddenly, as he stood there, a single star came out. It was the first. It was low and clear and very bright, above the black line of the trees, and he knew exactly what to do.

He didn't say it aloud. He only shut his eyes, for a moment, and wished, hard, for each of them, one after the other, in order, the whole of what he had.

Then he turned, and went up the hill to where the candle was.
