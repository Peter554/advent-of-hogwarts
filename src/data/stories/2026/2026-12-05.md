The itinerary was on the back of an Arithmancy problem set, folded into four and tucked inside Mira's left glove, where it could be reached in a single movement and consulted without looking as though she was consulting it.

It was not, she wanted to say in advance, an _excessive_ itinerary. It had four items. It ran from eleven in the morning until four in the afternoon, with a margin for error at either end. Every item had a fallback, in brackets, in case the first-choice venue was unexpectedly full or closed or (this had happened once, in October, to Finn) taken over by a wedding. And the whole thing was designed so that nobody would ever, at any point, have to ask "What shall we do now?" in the particular tone of voice that people used when they had run out of things to say.

1. _Tea, Madam Puddifoot's (fallback: Three Broomsticks)._
2. _Tomes and Scrolls: 30 min. (fallback: Dervish and Banges, 15 min.)._
3. _Honeydukes: new seasonal fudge._
4. _Walk by the lake (wind-dependent)._

"Is that a flowchart?" said Priya.

"It's a list."

"There's a bracket."

"It's a list with contingencies."

"Mm," said Priya Desai, and tucked her arm through Mira's, and did not say any of the other things that Mira could see her not saying.

---

It had started to snow on the road down. It wasn't heavy, only a fine, dry, glittering fall that lay on shoulders and scarves and the tops of wool hats and did not stay long enough to turn to water. Hogsmeade had put out its decorations in the night: green garlands on every doorframe, a string of tiny lanterns across the High Street, and outside Zonko's a snowman with a Quaffle for a head and a sign that said _PLEASE DO NOT FEED_.

Priya walked beside her with her hands in her pockets and her chin in her scarf, a green one, Slytherin issue, with a hole in the end that Mira had once offered to darn. She was in the same year as Mira and a good deal cleverer, and she moved with a kind of economy that Mira had admired for most of a year: she never wasted a gesture. She had thick dark wavy hair, which had come out of its clip on the way down the hill, and which she didn't appear to have noticed.

She also looked, Mira thought, a little grey.

It was a small thing. She was probably imagining it. It might have been the light, which had that flat, pale, shadowless quality of a snowy December morning, and which made everyone look vaguely like a drawing of themselves. But Priya's eyes had a bruised, careful look, and she had yawned twice in the lane and covered it both times with her glove, in a way that suggested she had been covering things for some time.

"Are you all right?" said Mira.

"Fine. Why?"

"I only mean—I don't want to presume—but you seem slightly—"

"I'm fine. Tea?" said Priya, and pushed open the door of Madam Puddifoot's.

It was exactly as bad as they had both been told. It was small, steamy and pink, with frilled curtains and a ceiling hung with golden cherubs that fluttered and cooed, and a great many couples were sitting at tiny tables in the attitude of people who have been told to enjoy themselves. A waitress was carrying a three-tiered cake stand shaped like a heart. A cherub landed on the sugar bowl and sneezed.

"I would rather die," said Priya, in a very low voice, "than eat a scone under a baby with wings."

"That's why there's a fallback."

"Oh," said Priya, and for the first time that morning her whole face changed, and she looked at Mira with an expression that Mira was beginning to recognise, and was not prepared for even now. "You really did think of everything."

"I tried to," said Mira, and went slightly pink, and led the way out.

---

It was at the third stop that she first noticed them.

She had noticed _something_ at the second. The bookshop had been warm and dim and full of the particular quiet of other people looking at books, and Mrs Catchpole had come out from behind the counter to show them a first edition of a rune lexicon that Mira had been looking at, wistfully, since the third year. She had held it. She had put it back. And in the corner of her eye, in the aisle by the poetry, a tall figure in a dark hood had been reading a volume of verse upside down.

She had thought: _That's probably a coincidence_. Then she'd thought: _It isn't_.

The next one was harder to place. In the lane outside Honeydukes, in the middle of the swirling snow, a girl in a very large hat had been looking in the window of the post office with the fixed interest of someone who has never seen a post office before. The hat was a Gryffindor scarlet pom-pom hat with a brim pulled down over the eyes. A pair of long braids showed underneath it. From the post office, in the way of things, a very large hat is a curious choice for a person who does not wish to be recognised.

"I think," said Mira, "and I want to say this is only a preliminary hypothesis, that we're being followed."

"We are," said Priya.

"You've noticed."

"There's somebody in the bookshop who was reading a poem upside down. I think that's meant to be your friend Elliot." Priya's mouth was twitching. "And I'm fairly sure that's Zara Okonkwo in a hat."

"I could kill them."

"I wouldn't. I think it's sweet."

"It is _not_ sweet. It's an invasion of privacy. I made an itinerary—"

"I know you did."

"—specifically so that—" She stopped. There wasn't an end to the sentence that she could say aloud. She tugged her scarf up. "Zara said they were doing their own Hogsmeade."

"They are," said Priya. "Theirs is just also yours."

A short way down the lane, a scarlet pom-pom turned very rapidly in the direction of a snowdrift and stood to attention.

---

Honeydukes was warm and loud and smelt of burnt sugar. There was a queue at the back, snaking round three pillars of sherbet lemons and a pyramid of Fizzing Whizzbees, and in the middle of the room, on a polished brass stand, a sign stood propped against a mountain of pale yellow squares.

_NEW FOR WINTER!_ it said, in a looping, glittering script. _LONGING FUDGE. Tastes of what you most want in the world. (Honeydukes accepts no liability for revelations.)_

"That's ridiculous," said Mira.

"Brilliant," said Priya.

They each took a piece. It was small and square, the colour of butter, with a faint sheen of sugar on the top. The woman behind the counter, who had a plait down to her knees and a badge saying _MAVIS_, wrapped them in waxed paper and said in a kindly, practised voice, "Eat it slowly. Don't try to guess. It doesn't like being guessed at."

They stood against a pillar and unwrapped them.

Mira had assumed, in so far as she had assumed anything, that it would taste of a perfect score. She had sometimes had the horrible suspicion that this was what she most wanted in the world, and she'd readied herself for it, the sharp, dry, clean taste of a gold star.

She bit into the fudge.

It tasted of cardamom.

It tasted of cardamom and hot milk and the sweet, scorched edge of toast spread thick with ghee. It tasted of the back kitchen in her grandmother's flat in Harrow, with the little brass tea-strainer and the radio on low, and the smell of steam on a cold day, and a very small hand being given a cup that was far too hot and told, _Blow, beta. Blow, and then wait_.

She stood very still.

She was aware, after a while, that Priya was speaking to her. "Mira? Mira. Are you—"

"I'm fine," said Mira. "It's, ah. It's a bit like—" She stopped. She turned her head and found that the corner of her eye had gone suddenly hot. "It's cardamom."

"Oh," said Priya, very softly.

She didn't say anything else. She put her free hand through Mira's arm, and held on.

After a minute, Mira cleared her throat. "What about yours?"

"Mine?"

"It's meant to be—what do you want most in the world?"

Priya looked at her piece. Then, with the air of someone getting something over with, she ate it.

There was a short, ominous pause.

"Mint," she said.

"Mint?"

"Toothpaste. It's—" She held her hand up. "It's the first thing in the morning. After you've slept, properly slept, eight hours, and woken up on your own, and you've got your teeth done and the window's open and it's just _quiet_." She swallowed. Her eyes, which were sharp and dark and usually knew exactly what they were doing, had filled with water. "Oh. That's annoying."

"Priya."

"I'm fine."

"You haven't slept, have you?"

"I've slept."

"How much?"

Priya was silent.

"It's the Arithmancy Challenge," she said at last. "The December set came out at midnight on the first, and it's—" She waved a hand. "They've deliberately made it impossible. There are three problems. There's a bit where you've got to prove something about the ratio of a spiral to its own reflection, and I think I've got it, and then I look at it again and I haven't. I've been up till three every night since Tuesday."

"Why didn't you say?"

"Because you'd made an _itinerary_."

It wasn't said unkindly. It was said, in fact, in something very like a laugh, with her head dropped sideways against Mira's shoulder. But it landed, in the warm, loud, sugary room, with a small cold weight, and Mira stood there, with the cardamom still in her mouth, and looked at what she had done.

She had made a list. She had put in it everything that she thought might help. She had put in tea and books and sweets and snow, and she had put them in order, with brackets, and she had not, once, in the whole of it, put in _a place to sit down_.

She took the folded paper out of her left glove, and looked at the fourth item.

_Walk by the lake (wind-dependent)._

She crossed it out with her thumbnail. It didn't make a good line, but it was enough.

"We're going to the Three Broomsticks," said Mira.

"We've got the lake—"

"There's wind," said Mira. "It's very dependent."

---

The Three Broomsticks was the sort of place that, on a snowy Saturday in December, you could feel from the far end of the street: a low, golden, noisy glow behind steamed-up windows. There was a fire in the big hearth at the back. There was a nook beside it, a round booth with a bench seat of worn red velvet, and a small round table on which somebody had left a candle, and Madam Rosmerta, with one look at the two of them, brought two mugs of hot butterbeer without being asked and a plate of ginger biscuits, and said, "Quietest corner, loves. Nobody'll bother you."

Priya sat down on the velvet and said, "I'm only going to close my eyes for one minute."

"Of course."

"I'm not going to sleep."

"No."

"Don't let me—" said Priya, and put her head on Mira's shoulder, and was gone.

It was as simple as that. One moment her breathing was shallow and quick and careful, and the next it was long and slow and deep, with a very faint catch at the bottom of each breath, like someone walking down a staircase in the dark. Her hair, which had come out of its clip entirely, tickled Mira's neck. Her hand, which was lying in her lap, had turned over, palm up, in the way that only a hand that has stopped holding anything can.

Mira didn't move. She was holding the paper wrapper of her fudge, and in it, still, half a piece, uneaten, smelling of tea and toast and being seven years old. The fire popped. Somewhere in the pub a man was telling a very long story about a goat.

She didn't read the itinerary again. She didn't need to. She thought of all the things that might have gone on it, if she had thought to ask; and she thought that, probably, none of them would have been half as useful as the thing she was doing, which was nothing, as carefully as she could.

---

They came at about half past two.

She felt them before she saw them, the way you feel a draught: a rustle by the curtain, a scuffle, a muffled thump. A pom-pom hat appeared over the edge of the booth. It was followed by a long, grave, hooded face, and the two of them stood side by side with the awkward dignity of people carrying four mugs of butterbeer and the knowledge that they had been caught.

"We weren't following you," said Zara, in a whisper.

"You were wearing a hat," Mira whispered back.

"It's a very good hat."

"I'd like it noted," said Elliot, "that the poem was a deliberate artistic choice."

"It was upside down."

"That was the artistic choice."

Mira looked at them. They looked at her. Zara's pom-pom wobbled. A long way off, the man with the goat reached the end of his story and a ragged cheer went up.

"We'd planned to leave," Zara whispered. "Then you went into Honeydukes, and we thought we'd just see how the fudge was—"

"We got some," said Elliot.

"And—"

"And it was fine."

"What did you get?" said Mira, in spite of herself.

Zara opened her mouth. Shut it. "Wet grass," she said, a bit stiffly. "And, like, mud. It was a good sort of mud."

"And you?"

Elliot examined the ceiling. "Paper," they said.

"Paper?"

"It's a very nice paper. I'd rather not discuss it."

There was a short silence. It had, Mira realised, something in it she hadn't expected, which was neither embarrassment nor irritation but a kind of shy, shared, startled feeling, as though they had all been caught looking at the same thing through different windows. Zara's tall hat had gone slightly askew. Elliot's hood had slid back. Neither of them seemed to know what to do with their hands.

"We brought you these," said Zara, putting down two mugs. "As an apology. And one for—" She looked down at the head on Mira's shoulder, and her voice dropped even lower. "Is she—"

"Asleep. She's been up for days."

"Oh," said Zara.

"We'll go," said Elliot.

"No," said Mira. She hadn't known she was going to say it. "You could—if you wanted—it's quiet. You could sit down."

They looked at each other. It was a brief glance, the sort that two people give each other when they are checking something. And then Zara, with enormous care, lowered herself onto the end of the bench like a person sitting on a sleeping bird, and Elliot hooked a chair out from the next table with their foot.

Nobody said anything. The fire burned. After a while Zara took off her hat and set it, gently, on the table, as though it might wake somebody. Elliot picked up a ginger biscuit and examined it with great attention. The snow came down against the window, and the man in the corner started on another story, this time about a duck.

Mira sat with a girl asleep against her shoulder and half a piece of fudge in her hand, still smelling faintly of tea and toast, and watched the snow come down past the window and the candle burn slowly lower.
