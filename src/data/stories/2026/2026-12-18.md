The parcel came on the last day of term, in the last post, by the last owl, in brown paper so thin that the owl had very nearly dropped it into the lake.

"I'd been told," said Elliot, holding it at arm's length in the Entrance Hall, "that it would arrive by the end of November."

"It's the eighteenth of December," said Mira.

"I _know_ it's the eighteenth of December."

"I'm only saying. If it's late, there's nothing to be gained by—"

"Mira."

"Yes?"

"If you say 'schedule', I'll send it back."

Mira closed her mouth. She looked at the parcel. She looked, with visible effort, at the ceiling. Elliot, who could count on one hand the times in five years they had seen her bite her tongue, watched her do it with something very close to happiness, and tore open the paper.

Inside, in a nest of tissue, were eight shirts.

They were soft, grey and slightly too big, with short sleeves and a faded look, as if they'd been washed a hundred times already. Across the front of each, in strong, hopeful, off-centre yellow, were two words.

_WONDER SEEKERS_.

Underneath, smaller, in black, was a quick loose line drawing of four figures from behind, standing side by side at the rail of a very high tower, shoulders touching, with a great deal of sky in front of them.

"Oh," said Mira.

Elliot didn't say anything. They had half forgotten what it looked like. They had drawn it in November, one grey evening in the library, in a defensive, ironic rush, and sent it to a small Muggle printing firm in Kent that had taken the money and said nothing since. They had half hoped it would never come. They had drawn it from the back so that nobody would have to look at the faces.

"It's nice," said Mira, in a small voice.

"It's a joke."

"It's _very_ nice."

"I said in January I'd do it, and I didn't want to be someone who didn't." They shook the shirt out and looked at it critically. "The yellow's wrong. It's meant to be gold."

"It's perfect." Mira had taken one. She was turning it over with the frown of a person looking for a hidden clause, and in about four seconds she found one. "Elliot. There's writing."

"There isn't."

"On the hem. It's very small." She held it to the light. "'Seek wonder in small things. Terms and conditions apply. Satisfaction not guaranteed. Wonder is non-refundable. The designer accepts no responsibility for fit.'"

"That's the Muggle printers," said Elliot. "They insisted on a disclaimer."

"They did not."

"They might have."

Mira looked at them over her glasses, and her mouth was shaking.

"I'm going to put it on," she said, "right now."

---

By six the Great Hall had turned into a theatre.

It was the oldest tradition at Hogwarts, or so Zara maintained, who had it from her cousin Tobe, who had it from an uncle. On the last night of the autumn term, with the exams marked and the trunks not yet packed and the whole castle flushed and restless, the staff gave a pantomime. It was always a fairy tale, and always performed with a joyous and thoroughly misguided sincerity, with costumes and songs and several jokes nobody would be allowed to repeat in class. It had been going for a hundred and forty years. In all that time, said Zara, only one performance had finished the way it was meant to.

The long tables had been pushed back and benches laid across the floor facing the high table, which had been turned into a stage with a good deal of paint. It had a backdrop, a pair of wobbly pillars, and at one end a flat cardboard clock with a round white face, two painted hands and a hole at the bottom through which, a first-year had been heard to remark, a person's feet were plainly visible.

Elliot found a seat at the end of the fourth row, with a wall to lean on and nobody beyond it. They sat with their long legs crossed in the light of three hundred floating candles, in a grey shirt with yellow lettering over their dress shirt and under a green jacket, and waited with a dry, wary contentment.

"Is that—" said Zara.

She'd dropped onto the bench beside them. She was already in hers, pulled on over her Quidditch jersey and then, on reflection, over her cloak, so that it sat on her like a tabard with the letters stretched across her chest. She was grinning from ear to ear.

"I'm never taking it off," she said.

"It's a shirt."

"It's the _best_ shirt. Look, there's us. That's me. I'm the tall one."

"You're all the same height in the drawing."

"I'm the tall one _in spirit_." She held out a hand, palm up. "Are there any more?"

There had been. They had been given out in the corridor before the doors opened. Finn put his on straight away over his jumper and hugged it, in the middle of the Entrance Hall, with both arms crossed over his chest, like a man embracing a large friendly dog. Oliver said, "Oh, is it for me too?" and had to be told that it was. Priya held hers at arm's length and said, "I haven't earned this," and Mira told her, with perfect gentleness, that nobody was asking her to. And two first-years, a small round girl and a small narrow one, had appeared at Elliot's elbow and gone completely silent, and put theirs on over their robes, and walked in side by side with the sleeves hanging past their knuckles.

Eight shirts. That left none.

"I ordered ten," said Elliot. "Two got lost."

"Which two?"

"They're for whoever we've not met yet."

Zara gave them a quick, sharp, unreadable look. Then she grinned, leaned sideways, put her head on their shoulder for a second, sat up, and said in a perfectly ordinary voice, "Here come the curtains."

---

It would be impossible, afterwards, to convey the whole of it.

It began with Professor Sinistra, who came out from the wings in a long black gown and enormous spectacles, carrying a leather book, and stood quite still in the middle of the stage until the Hall had gone silent. She did not smile.

"Good evening," said Professor Sinistra, in a voice of pure and unshakeable tedium. "I am your narrator. I have been asked to tell you the story of Cinderella. I have been told that it is a happy one. I have not been shown any evidence for this." She opened the book. "Once upon a time there was a girl called Cinderella, who lived with her two stepsisters, who were, in a word, awful. I shall now introduce them."

A roar went up.

They came on from either side in ball gowns of astonishing size, magenta and lime-green, with a great many frills and wigs the height of a hatstand. The taller one crossed the stage in four-inch silver heels with the grim, dogged expression of a man crossing a bog.

"It's Vance," breathed Finn, from three seats down.

"It can't be."

"It's Vance. He's in _sequins_."

He was. Professor Vance, who taught Advanced Defence Against the Dark Arts to the seventh years and was known to remove points for the improper tying of a shoelace, was dressed head to foot in a gown of such shimmering vulgarity that it threw fans of candlelight across the ceiling. He glared at the audience with the flat stare of someone who had been told this would be good for morale.

"I am," said Professor Vance, in his weather-report voice, "the elder sister. My name is Griselda. I do not like the prince. I do not like the ball. I do not like, in general, any of you."

The audience went up like a firework.

Beside him, in a gown of smaller dimensions and rather more lace, with gold spectacles on a chain and a stiff white cap on top of her wig, a thin grey upright figure stepped into the light, put one finger to her lips, and said, in a very quiet and terrible voice, "_Shh_."

"That's Madam Oakwood," said Mira, with reverence.

"She's been waiting thirty years," said Elliot.

---

It went rapidly downhill. Elliot wouldn't have had it any other way.

Cinderella was Professor Babbling, in a ragged apron with a mop and bucket, and she turned out to have the timing of a seasoned stage actress. She slipped on the bucket seven times. By the third the audience had begun to chant. By the sixth Thorne, at the front, with a pencil in her hair and both arms in the air, had got the whole Hall roaring "OH NO SHE DIDN'T!" and the first-years were falling off their benches.

There was a Prince, who was Madam Hooch, in a plumed hat and tights, riding a Nimbus with a horse's head tied to the front. There was a Clock, who was McGonagall, standing stock-still at the end of the stage in a cardboard cylinder, and who struck midnight at the proper moment with a deep, grave, expressionless _bong_ that brought down the house. There was a ball, in which seven professors waltzed with the utmost seriousness, and one of them was a Hippogriff.

And there was the Fairy Godmother.

She came on in a cloud of glitter, in pale pink net, with paper wings and a star-tipped wand made from a coat-hanger. She was about three feet high. She was standing on a stack of books piled four deep for the occasion, wobbling at the top like a very small, very proud Christmas fairy.

"_Bibbidi_," squeaked Professor Flitwick.

The Hall lost its mind.

---

It was the coach that did it.

It was meant to be the high point: the transformation scene. Cinderella in tears at the bottom of the garden; the Fairy Godmother with a flourish; the pumpkin rolling in from stage left on wheels, as big as a cottage and as orange as a sunset; and in front of it, drawing it with a slow, thundering tread, the coach-horse.

It was a pantomime horse, shaggy and dark brown, with a long face, a paper mane and two soulful, slightly crossed eyes. The front half was Hagrid. There was no question of that; nobody else in Scotland had legs of that diameter. The back half was smaller, rounder and much more determined, and a patched grey hat stuck out of the rear of the costume like the tail of a surprised bird.

"That's _Sprout_," said Finn, in rapture.

"It can't be."

"It's the _hat_."

It was the hat. Professor Sprout, with the face of a woman who had agreed to this in a weak moment in October, was bent double inside the back of a pantomime horse with her hands on Hagrid's belt, doing her best to keep in step.

They did very well. They got halfway across the stage and halted in front of the pumpkin to a roll of applause. The horse pawed the ground with one front hoof and gave a long, ringing, wonderfully realistic whinny, so that the front row, who had been drinking butterbeer, got a fine spray over their robes.

And Sprout, at the rear, in the dark, put her foot on the hem of the pumpkin's cloth.

It was only a small thing. Afterwards nobody could agree what: a tangled rope, a fold in the curtain, a stubborn tuft of moss she had probably put there herself. She took a step back. She wobbled. She put out a hand to grab something, and it found, with splendid inevitability, the painted pillar at the left of the stage.

The pillar swayed.

It went slowly, like a tree being felled, leaning its pasteboard length across the stage until it caught the corner of the backdrop, and both of them came down together with a soft, dignified crash like a very large book being closed. The castle fell. The ballroom fell. A cloud of dust and glitter rose from the boards and hung in the light.

A second's silence.

Then Hagrid, at the front of the horse, who had not looked round, who had not heard a thing, who was entirely wrapped in the performance, took another three steady strides across the stage, towing a small, furious, flailing Herbologist behind him like the tail of a comet, drew himself up to his full and magnificent height, and in a loud, clear voice delivered his next line.

"AS I WAS SAYIN'," said Hagrid.

The Hall exploded.

---

Elliot had never laughed so hard. It wasn't a thing that happened to them. They had a good laugh in the ordinary way, dry and sudden and satisfied, like a good move at chess. This began as a gasp under the ribs and turned into something between a howl and a wheeze, and their whole body was shaking, and they couldn't breathe, and tears were running into the collar of a grey shirt with yellow lettering.

On their right, Zara was bent double with her head on her knees. On their left, past a small gap, Finn had both hands over his mouth and was making a noise like a kettle. Further down, Oliver had slid off the bench entirely and was lying on his back across the floor, kicking his heels. Mira sat bolt upright with her glasses off and her face wet, laughing in high, silent, shaking gasps with one hand clamped over her mouth, and every so often she took a breath and said "_Oh_, oh no, oh," and started again.

"I'm going to be sick," she gasped.

"You're not."

"I'm _going to_."

"He went on," said Elliot, in a weak voice. "He didn't even—"

"'As I was sayin'—'"

"_Don't_."

It was Priya, on Mira's other side, who finally broke. She had been sitting very still with her lips pressed together in heroic composure. Then she looked at the stage, where Sprout had at last got free and was sitting in the wreckage with her hat over one eye, saying something in a level, unrepeatable undertone to a horse's head, and Priya's whole face went at once, like a dam. She laughed like someone who hadn't slept for a month and had just been told she could. She put her forehead on Mira's shoulder and shook.

And somewhere along the row, a small thin voice in a Whitby accent was saying, quite clearly, "It's _brilliant_. It's the best thing that's ever happened," and a smaller, rounder, much louder one said, "I TOLD you."

---

It ended at nine in a blaze of paper snow.

There was no resolution. The Prince had been lost in the wreckage along with most of the second act. The glass slipper turned out to be a wellington. The Fairy Godmother, undaunted, conjured a second pumpkin and a conga line of mice, which led the whole cast and then half the audience three times round the Hall, out into the Entrance Hall and back again. Sinistra, at the end, stood alone in the middle of the stage in a drift of glitter, and closed her book.

"And they all lived," she said, "more or less." She considered. "I do not wish to be asked about it."

The applause went on for four minutes.

McGonagall came out last. She had taken off the Clock and was in her own robes and hat. She stood at the front with her hands folded and let the roar go on for exactly as long as she judged proper, and when it began to die she raised one hand.

"The staff," said Professor McGonagall, "wish to thank you for your attendance."

A cheer.

"We wish to remind you that nothing you have seen this evening is to be repeated in any classroom. Mr Vance, in particular, has asked me to say so." She did not look at him. "Term is concluded. The Express leaves on Tuesday. Those of you who have elected to remain will be informed of the arrangements in due course." A faint dryness touched the corner of her mouth. "I would, finally, like to say that it is the view of the Headmistress that it was by some margin the best performance in a decade, and that I do not expect it to be repeated."

She turned and went off in perfect silence, and left the Hall speechless with delight.

---

It was a sixth-year with a camera who took the photograph.

She went down the row collecting them in the noisy, half-lit aftermath: eight people in grey shirts in an uneven line, arms round one another's shoulders, in front of the wreck of the stage. She propped the camera on a stool, set the charm, ran back to join the end of the row and said, "Everyone look at the lens. Three, two—"

"Wait," said Zara. "Where's Amara?"

"Here!"

"Where's Wren?"

"_Here_."

They had somehow got to the front, crammed in at knee level, one on either side, in shirts that came down to their shins, faces turned up and smiles so wide they nearly met at the back.

"Everyone in?"

"Everyone in."

"One."

And as the flash went, at the exact moment of its going, a vast shape swept in from the right in a waistcoat the colour of an aubergine, with a walrus moustache and a glass of something in its hand. It was an elbow. It was, to be precise, a very large, pleased and unmistakable elbow, which had inserted itself, with the unerring instinct of a man who has never in his life failed to find a camera, into the extreme right-hand corner of the frame.

"Professor Slughorn," said the sixth-year, in faint despair.

"My dears!" cried Slughorn. "Don't mind me!"

The photograph came out crooked, a little blurred and badly lit, and slightly off the edge of the paper. Everyone in it was laughing. Zara had her head back and her mouth wide open. Mira had her glasses askew and her face streaked, and Priya, behind her, had both arms round her shoulders, and Finn was lifting Oliver clean off his feet. Wren and Amara, at the front, held their shirts out sideways like wings. And in the middle, with their hands in their pockets and their chin up, Elliot Novak stood in a row of eight people in grey shirts with yellow lettering, laughing at something just out of the picture, and did not look at the camera at all.

In the corner, large and shining and entirely unrepentant, was Slughorn's elbow.

They pinned it, afterwards, on the wall of the third-floor classroom beside the fireplace, where it stayed.
