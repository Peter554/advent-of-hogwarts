The parcel came on the last day of term, in the last post, by the last owl, in a wrapping of brown paper so thin that the owl had very nearly dropped it into the lake.

"I'd been told," said Elliot, holding it at arm's length in the Entrance Hall, "that it would arrive by the end of November."

"It's the eighteenth of December," said Mira.

"I _know_ it's the eighteenth of December."

"I'm only saying. If it's late, there's nothing to be gained by—"

"Mira."

"Yes?"

"If you say 'schedule', I'll send it back."

Mira closed her mouth. She looked at the parcel. She looked, with enormous and visible effort, at the ceiling, and Elliot, who could count on one hand the number of times in five years that they had seen her bite her tongue, watched her do it, with a glow of something that was very close to happiness, and tore open the paper.

Inside, in a nest of tissue, there were eight shirts.

They were soft and grey and slightly too big, with short sleeves and a faded look, like something that had been washed a hundred times already, and across the front of each, in letters of a strong, hopeful, off-centre yellow, were two words.

_WONDER SEEKERS_.

Underneath, smaller, in black, a drawing. It was a line drawing, quick and loose, of four figures from behind, standing side by side at the rail of a very high tower, with their shoulders touching and a great deal of sky in front of them.

"Oh," said Mira.

Elliot didn't say anything. They were holding up the top shirt by the shoulders. They'd forgotten, a little, what it looked like. It had been done in November, one grey evening in the library, in a sort of defensive, ironic, half-embarrassed rush, and sent off to a small Muggle printing firm in Kent that had taken money, and said nothing, and forgotten them. They had half hoped it would never come. They'd drawn it from the back so that nobody would be looking at the faces.

"It's nice," said Mira, in a small voice.

"It's a joke."

"It's _very_ nice."

"I said in January I'd do it, and I didn't want to be the sort of person who didn't." They shook it out, and held it up, and looked at it with a critical eye. "The yellow's wrong. It's meant to be gold."

"It's perfect," said Mira. She'd taken one. She had gone very pink. She was turning it over in her hands, with the absorbed, slightly frowning look of a person looking for a hidden clause, and she had, in about four seconds, found one. "Elliot. There's writing."

"There isn't."

"On the back. On the hem. It's very small." She held it to the light. "'Seek wonder in small things. Terms and conditions apply.'" She read on. "'Satisfaction not guaranteed. Wonder is non-refundable. The designer accepts no responsibility for fit.'"

"That's the Muggle printers," said Elliot. "They insisted on a disclaimer."

"They did not."

"They might have."

Mira looked at them over the top of her glasses, and her mouth was shaking.

"I'm going to put it on," she said, "right now."

---

By six o'clock the Great Hall had turned into a theatre.

It was the oldest tradition at Hogwarts, or so Zara maintained, who'd had it from her sister, who'd had it from a cousin. On the last night of the autumn term, when the exams were marked and the trunks were not yet packed and the whole castle hung in a loose, flushed, restless sort of holiday, the staff gave a pantomime. It was always the same. It was always a fairy tale, performed with a terrible, joyous, thoroughly misguided sincerity, with costumes, and songs, and a number of jokes that nobody would be allowed to repeat in class. It had been going for a hundred and forty years. In all that time, said Zara, only one performance had finished the way it was meant to.

The long tables had been pushed back. Rows of benches had been laid across the floor in their place, facing the high table, which had been transformed, with a good deal of paint and ingenuity, into a stage. It had a painted backdrop. It had a pair of rather wobbly pillars. It had, at one end, a tall, flat cardboard clock, with a round white face and two painted hands, and a hole at the bottom, through which, a small first-year had been heard to remark, a person's feet were plainly visible.

Elliot found a seat at the end of the fourth row. It was a good one. It had a decent view, and a wall to lean on, and nobody beyond it. They sat, with their long legs crossed, in the dim golden light of three hundred floating candles, wearing a grey shirt with yellow lettering over their dress shirt and under a green jacket, and a sort of dry, wary contentment, and waited.

"Is that—" said Zara.

She'd dropped on the bench beside them. She was already in her shirt. She had put it on over her Quidditch jersey and then, apparently on reflection, over her cloak, so that it sat on her like a tabard, with the letters stretched across her chest, and a broad, helpless, ear-to-ear grin on her face.

"I'm never taking it off," she said.

"It's a shirt."

"It's the _best_ shirt. Look. There's us. That's me. I'm the tall one."

"You're all the same height in the drawing."

"I'm the tall one _in spirit_." She held out a hand, palm up, with the air of someone about to be given something. "Are there any more?"

There were. They had been distributed already, in the corridor, in the thin, crowded light of the interval before the doors: one to Finn, who had put it on straight away, over his jumper, and then hugged it, in the middle of the Entrance Hall, with both arms crossed over his chest, like a man embracing a large and friendly dog. One to Oliver, who'd said, "Oh, are we—is it for me too?" and had had to be told that it was. One to Priya, who had held it, rather gravely, at arm's length, and said, "I haven't earned this," and been told by Mira, with perfect gentleness, that she wasn't being asked to earn it. And one for each of the two first-years, who had arrived out of nowhere at Elliot's elbow, a small round girl and a small narrow one, and had been so overwhelmed that they'd both gone completely silent, and put them on over their robes, and walked in, side by side, with the sleeves hanging past their knuckles.

Eight shirts. That left none.

"I ordered ten," said Elliot. "Two of them got lost."

"Which two?"

"They're meant to be for whoever we've not met yet."

Zara looked at them. It was a quick, sharp, unreadable look. Then she grinned, and leaned sideways, and put her head briefly on their shoulder, and sat up again, and said, in a perfectly ordinary voice, "Here come the curtains."

---

It would be impossible, afterwards, to convey the whole of it.

It began with Professor Sinistra. She came out from the wings in a long black gown and a pair of enormous spectacles, carrying a large leather book, and stood quite still in the middle of the stage until the entire Hall had gone silent. Her face was the colour of old paper. She did not smile.

"Good evening," said Professor Sinistra, in a voice of the purest and most unshakeable tedium. "I am your narrator. I have been asked to tell you the story of Cinderella. I have been told that it is a happy one. I have not been shown any evidence for this." She opened the book. "Once upon a time there was a girl called Cinderella, who lived with her two stepsisters, who were, in a word, awful. I shall now introduce them."

A great roar went up from the hall.

They had come on from either side. They were enormous. They were in ball gowns of astonishing size, in magenta and lime-green, with a great many frills, and wigs the height of a hatstand, and one of them, the taller one, was making his way across the stage in a pair of four-inch silver heels with the grim, dogged, deeply unamused expression of a man crossing a bog.

"It's Vance," breathed Finn, from three seats down.

"It can't be."

"It's Vance. He's in _sequins_."

He was. Professor Vance, who taught Advanced Defence Against the Dark Arts to the seventh years, who had been known to remove points for the improper tying of a shoelace, was dressed from head to foot in a gown of such colossal and shimmering vulgarity that it threw back the light of the candles in great fans across the ceiling, and he was glaring at the audience with the cold, flat, bleak stare of someone who had been told that this would be good for morale.

"I am," said Professor Vance, in his deadpan weather-report voice, "the elder sister. My name is Griselda. I do not like the prince. I do not like the ball. I do not like, in general, any of you."

The audience went up like a firework.

And beside him, in a gown of slightly smaller dimensions and a good deal more lace, with a pair of gold spectacles on a chain and a stiff white cap on top of her wig, a thin, grey, upright figure stepped into the light, put one finger to her lips, and said, in a very quiet and terrible voice, "_Shh_."

"That's Madam Oakwood," said Mira, with a sort of reverence.

"She's been waiting thirty years," said Elliot.

---

It went rapidly downhill. Elliot wouldn't have had it any other way.

Cinderella was Professor Babbling, who came out in a ragged apron with a mop and a bucket, and who, to the stunned delight of the whole Hall, turned out to have the comic timing of a seasoned stage actress. She slipped on the bucket. She did it seven times. By the third, the audience had begun to chant. By the sixth, Thorne, at the front, with a pencil in her hair and her arms in the air, had got them all to roar, with perfect and sublime unanimity: "OH NO SHE DIDN'T!" and the first-years were falling off their benches.

There was a Prince, who was Madam Hooch, in a plumed hat and a pair of tights, riding a Nimbus with a horse's head tied to the front. There was a Clock, which was McGonagall, who stood quite still, at the end of the stage, in a cardboard cylinder with a face painted on it, and who struck midnight, at the proper moment, with a deep, grave, expressionless _bong_ that brought down the house. There was a ball, in which seven professors danced a waltz in a state of the utmost seriousness, and one of them was a Hippogriff.

And there was the Fairy Godmother.

She came on in a cloud of glitter, in a floating gown of pale pink net, with a pair of paper wings, and a wand with a star on the end that had been made from a coat-hanger. She was about three feet high. She was standing on a stack of books. They had been piled four deep, for the occasion, and she was wobbling at the top of them like a very small, very proud, immensely satisfied Christmas fairy.

"_Bibbidi_," squeaked Professor Flitwick.

The Hall lost its mind.

---

It was the coach that did it.

It had been, by all accounts, the high point of the production. It was the great transformation scene. Cinderella, in tears, in her rags, at the bottom of the garden; the Fairy Godmother, with a flourish; the pumpkin, rolling in from stage left on a pair of wheels, as large as a cottage and as orange as a sunset; and, in front of it, drawing it with a slow, majestic, thundering tread, the coach-horse itself.

It was a pantomime horse. It had two people in it. It was dark brown, and shaggy, and enormously large, with a long face, and a flowing paper mane, and two large, soulful, slightly crossed eyes. The front half was Hagrid. There was no question of that. There was nobody else in Scotland with legs of that diameter. The back half was a smaller, rounder, much more determined creature, in a grey patched hat that stuck out of the rear end of the costume, like the tail of a very surprised bird.

"That's _Sprout_," said Finn, in a voice of rapture.

"It can't be."

"It's the _hat_."

It was the hat. Professor Sprout, with the face of a woman who had agreed to this in a weak moment in October, was bent double inside the back end of a pantomime horse, with her hands on Hagrid's belt, and was doing her best to keep in step.

They did very well. They got halfway across the stage. They came to a halt in front of the pumpkin, to a roll of applause, and the horse, with a great and elegant gesture, pawed the ground with one front hoof, and gave a long, ringing, wonderfully realistic whinny, so that the whole of the front row, who had been drinking butterbeer, got a fine spray all over their robes.

And Sprout, at the rear, in the dark, put her foot on the hem of the pumpkin's cloth.

It was only a small thing. Afterwards, nobody could agree on what it was. A tangled rope; a fold in the curtain; a very small, very stubborn tuft of moss on the floor, which she had probably put there herself. She took one step back. She wobbled. She put out a hand, to grab something, and it found, with a terrible and splendid inevitability, the painted pillar at the left of the stage.

The pillar swayed.

It was a slow, stately thing, like a tree being felled. It leaned, with its long pasteboard column, across the stage, and caught the painted backdrop at the corner, and both of them went down together, with a long, soft, dignified, majestic crash, like a very large book being closed. The castle fell. The ballroom fell. A cloud of dust and glitter rose up from the boards and hung, shimmering, in the light.

A second's silence.

Then Hagrid, at the front of the horse, who had not looked round, who had not heard a thing, who was entirely wrapped in the performance, and who had taken another three steady strides across the stage and was now, quite alone, well beyond the point of recovery, towing a small, furious, flailing Herbologist behind him like the tail of a comet, drew himself up to his full and magnificent height, and in a loud, clear, ringing voice, delivered his next line.

"AS I WAS SAYIN'," said Hagrid.

The hall exploded.

---

Elliot had never in their life laughed so hard. It was not a thing that happened to them. They had a very good laugh, in the ordinary way, a dry, sudden, satisfied one, with a sort of pleasure in it, like a good shot at chess. But this was something else. It began as a gasp, somewhere under the ribs, and it turned, unstoppably, into something between a howl and a wheeze, and their whole body was shaking, and they couldn't breathe, and tears were running down their face into the collar of a grey shirt with yellow lettering, and they could not have stopped to save their life.

On their right, Zara was bent double with her head on her knees. On their left, past a small gap, Finn had both hands over his mouth, and was making a noise like a kettle. Further down, Oliver had slid off the bench entirely and was lying across the floor, on his back, kicking his heels. Mira was sitting bolt upright, with her glasses off, and her face wet, and her shoulders heaving, and she was laughing in a high, thin, uncontrollable way, in huge, silent, shaking gasps, with tears streaming down both cheeks, and her hand clamped over her mouth, and every so often she took a breath and said, "_Oh_, oh no, oh," and began again.

"I'm going to be sick," she gasped.

"You're not."

"I'm _going to_."

"He went on," said Elliot, in a weak voice. "He didn't—he didn't even—"

"'As I was sayin'—'"

"_Don't_."

It was Priya, from the other side of Mira, who finally broke. She had been sitting very still, in her grey shirt, with her lips tightly pressed, with an expression of the most heroic composure; and then she looked at the stage, where Sprout had at last got herself free and was sitting on the boards, in the wreckage, with her hat over one eye, saying something in a level, appalling, quite unrepeatable undertone to a horse's head, and her whole face went, all at once, like a dam. She laughed. She laughed like a person who has not slept for a month and has just been told she can. She put her forehead down on Mira's shoulder, and shook.

And somewhere along the row, in the middle of it all, a very small, thin voice, in a Whitby accent, was saying, quite clearly, "It's _brilliant_. It's the best thing that's ever happened," and a smaller, rounder, much louder one said, "I TOLD you."

---

It ended at nine, in a blaze of paper snow.

There was, in the end, no resolution. The Prince had been lost in the wreckage, along with most of the second act. The glass slipper had turned out to be a wellington. The Fairy Godmother, undaunted, had conjured a second pumpkin from nowhere and a conga line of mice, which had led the whole cast, and then half the audience, in a long, ragged, joyful, entirely unplanned chain, three times round the Hall, and out into the Entrance Hall, and back again. Sinistra, at the very end, stood alone in the centre of the stage, in the ruins, in a drift of glitter, and closed her book.

"And they all lived," she said, "more or less." She considered. "I do not wish to be asked about it."

The applause went on for four minutes.

McGonagall came out last. She had taken off the Clock. She was in her own robes, with her hat on, and she stood at the front of the stage with her hands folded and let the roar go on for exactly as long as she judged proper. When it had begun to die, she raised one hand.

"The staff," said Professor McGonagall, "wish to thank you for your attendance."

A cheer.

"We wish to remind you that nothing you have seen this evening is to be repeated in any classroom. Mr Vance, in particular, has asked me to say so." She did not look at him. "Term is concluded. The Express leaves on Tuesday. Those of you who have elected to remain will be informed of the arrangements in due course." She paused, with a very faint dryness at the corner of her mouth. "I would, finally, like to say that it is the view of the Headmistress that it was by some margin the best performance in a decade, and that I do not expect it to be repeated."

She turned, and went off, in complete and perfect silence, and left the whole of the Hall, for a moment, speechless with delight.

---

It was a sixth-year with a camera who took the photograph.

She'd gone down the row, collecting them, in the milling, noisy, half-lit aftermath: eight people in grey shirts, in an uneven line, with their arms round one another's shoulders, in front of the wreck of the stage. She'd propped the camera on a stool, and set the charm, and run round to the end of the row to join them, and said, "Everyone look at the lens. Three, two—"

"Wait," said Zara. "Where's Amara?"

"Here!"

"Where's Wren?"

"_Here_."

They had somehow got to the front. They were crammed in together at the knee level, one on either side, in shirts that came down to their shins, with their faces turned up and their eyes enormous and their smiles so broad that they very nearly met at the back.

"Everyone in?"

"Everyone in."

"One."

And as the flash went, at the exact moment of its going, an enormous shape swept in from the right, in a waistcoat the colour of an aubergine, with a walrus moustache and a glass of something in its hand. It was an elbow. It was, to be precise, a very large, rather pleased, entirely unmistakable elbow, which had inserted itself, with the unerring instinct of a man who has never in his life failed to find a camera, into the extreme right-hand corner of the frame.

"Professor Slughorn," said the sixth-year, with a sort of faint despair.

"My dears!" cried Slughorn. "Don't mind me!"

The photograph came out crooked.

It was a little blurred, and badly lit, and slightly off the edge of the paper. Everyone in it was laughing. Zara had her head back and her mouth wide open. Mira had her glasses crooked and her face streaked, and Priya, behind her, had both arms round her shoulders, and Finn was lifting Oliver clean off his feet. Wren and Amara, at the front, were holding their shirts out sideways, like wings. And in the middle, with their hands in their pockets and their chin up and their whole face, for once, wide open, Elliot Novak stood in a row of eight people in grey shirts with yellow lettering, laughing at something just out of the picture, and did not look at the camera at all.

In the corner, large and shining and entirely unrepentant, was Slughorn's elbow.

They pinned it, afterwards, on the wall of the third-floor classroom, beside the fireplace, where it stayed.
