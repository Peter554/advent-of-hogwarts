Mira had gone to the alcove, on Tuesday afternoon, for an hour of perfectly ordinary peace and quiet, and instead found herself in possession of a mystery.

It was, she admitted afterwards, a very small mystery. It was about the size of a thumbprint. But there were two ways of looking at a thing like that: you could regard it as a minor anomaly, to be noted and set aside, or you could regard it as the first clear piece of evidence in a case. She had spent the whole of her fourth year deciding which sort of person she was, and the answer had turned out to be the second.

The alcove was exactly as they'd left it. The armchair, flattened by decades. The little table with the melted candle. The twenty or so battered books on their three crooked shelves, bristling with other people's handwriting. And on the back wall, at eye height, held up by a sticking charm that Elliot had cast with the air of someone laying a foundation stone, a single sheet of paper, pinned flat.

_To whoever finds this place: You're not alone._

It was a year old. It had gone faintly yellow at the corners, and it had been written, line by line, by four people who had thought themselves rather clever. Mira had read it perhaps three times since. She'd come, in part, to see whether it was still there.

It was. But at the bottom of it, below the four sets of initials, where the paper had been blank, somebody had written something new.

It was a single line. It was in a small, careful, very slightly slanting hand, and in ink of a colour Mira had never seen on a library table: a deep, vivid, unmistakable green, the green of a conifer in the rain.

_Thank you. I come here after lights-out. Is that all right? —W._

---

She read it standing up. Then she sat down in the armchair, took her glasses off, rubbed her eyes, and read it again, and a smile started to come over her face that she did not quite know what to do with.

_Is that all right?_

Somebody had read their note. Somebody had stood in this little windowless room in the dark, with whatever small light they'd carried in, and read _You're part of it now_, and believed it enough to ask.

She got out a piece of parchment.

It was not that she meant to find them, she told herself, firmly. It was only that, as someone who had in some sense started the conversation, she had a responsibility to know with whom she was having it. She wrote down the facts. _One: green ink._ She'd seen green ink once, a long time ago, at the bottom of a bottle in the window of a stationer's in Hogsmeade. It was called Evergreen. It was sold only in tiny, expensive, one-ounce bottles, and she remembered it because she'd thought it very pretty, and extremely impractical.

_Two: handwriting._ Slanting to the left, which meant the writer was left-handed, or else had been taught by someone who was. There was a smudge at the end of the _W_ where a hand had dragged through the wet ink, and it was a very small hand.

_Three: the date._ The ink was still glossy. It had been written, at the most, in the last two days.

_Four: after lights-out._

That was the interesting one. After lights-out, the library was closed. The doors were locked at nine, and the only people who had keys were Madam Oakwood and, in the way of things, Mira, who had been given a pass in her fourth year for extended research and had not, strictly speaking, ever given it back. A student couldn't be in the library at night. She could think of only one explanation, which was that this student was in the library when it closed, and had stayed.

She wrote that down too. Then, because it seemed important, she underlined it.

---

Madam Oakwood was at the front desk, stamping a stack of returns with a ferocity that suggested they'd done something to deserve it. She had a pair of half-moon glasses on a chain and a cardigan the colour of porridge, and she did not look up.

"Miss Patel."

"Madam Oakwood. I wondered if I might ask about the late-pass ledger."

"You may ask."

"I'm trying to find out whether there's anyone at all, in the school, who's got permission to be in the library after nine."

"No," said Madam Oakwood, stamping. "Only you."

"Right. Right, that's—" Mira's pen hovered. "And has anyone, to your knowledge, been in here, not _with_ permission, after the doors were locked? Hypothetically."

The stamp came down. It came down very precisely, in the exact centre of the page.

"I wouldn't know," said Madam Oakwood. "I never see anything after nine. It's a failing of mine. Terrible eyesight in the evenings." She turned a page. "Dreadful. I have to rely entirely on my other senses. I'll say this, though, Miss Patel, my hearing is excellent, and in the last month I have not heard a single book being dropped, a single page being torn, or a single thing, of any description, being done that I'd have to write down."

She looked up, finally, over her half-moon glasses, and her eyes were quite mild, and quite bright.

"A first-year Ravenclaw, I'd have thought," she said, "if you were asking. They tend to be the ones who need it. Now, that's quite enough hypothetical. Do mind the stairs."

---

It didn't take long, once she knew where to look.

Dinner was in the Great Hall at six, and the Ravenclaw first years sat at the end of the long blue-draped table in a bunch of eight, with their elbows in each other's way and their conversation going up in a bright, jumbled cloud of Quidditch and homework and who had let off a firework in the dormitory. Mira stopped at the end of the next row and looked.

She almost missed her. She was at the very end of the bench, a little apart, with an open book propped against the water jug and a plate that had hardly been touched. She was small and pale and thin, with long, straight, light-brown hair and a fringe that fell into her eyes, and she was eating her dinner in the manner of someone who has been told to, in a room full of people whose noise she was trying very hard to turn into scenery.

Her left hand, as she turned a page, had ink on the side of it. The ink was green.

Mira stood quite still. She had practised, on the stairs, three different opening lines. All three had gone.

"Hello," she said.

The girl looked up, and then, with a sort of dreadful speed, whipped the book shut and sat very straight. She had large, hazel-grey, watchful eyes, and they went to the blue of Mira's tie, and from there to Mira's face, and what happened in them was not at all what Mira had expected. It was something much closer to horror.

"I'll stop," said the girl, very fast, in a flat northern accent. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I didn't know it was anyone's, I just thought, I thought it was—and then I saw the note, and it said you could add, so I did, and I shouldn't have, I'll never go back, I'll take it off, I'll—"

"No," said Mira. "No, please, I—that's not why I'm—" She stopped. She breathed. She sat down on the bench, a respectful foot away, so that they were more or less level. "I'm Mira," she said. "I wrote the note. I mean, I wrote part of it. I'm one of the four. And I came to say—I'd like to say—"

She heard herself. She had a long sentence queued up, with three clauses and a caveat.

She put it down.

"It's all right," said Mira. "That's all. It's all right. You're allowed to be there."

There was a pause. The girl stared at her.

"Really?"

"It was meant for whoever found it. That's what the note says. It's the whole point."

"I know it's what it says," said the girl, in a rush, going red from the neck up. "But people say things. You never really know if they mean it. It's like when someone says 'pop round whenever you like', and you do, and they're in their dressing gown."

Mira laughed. She hadn't known she was going to. It came out of her in a short, delighted bark, and a couple of the first years glanced round.

"I mean it," she said. "I'm in my dressing gown all the time, and I mean it."

The girl didn't smile. But something in the set of her shoulders came down, about an inch.

"Wren," she said. "I'm Wren Calloway."

---

They went together, afterwards, in the long blue evening, and Madam Oakwood, as they passed the desk, looked intently at a stack of returns.

It was Wren's first time in the alcove with another person. Mira could tell. She went into it the way you go into a church, bareheaded, on tiptoe, with her hands behind her back, and looked around at the shelves and the chair and the candle with the shy, tight, shining look of someone who has been carrying something heavy and has just been told she can put it down.

"It's like somebody's left the light on," she said.

"I think that's exactly what it is."

"I didn't know where else to go." She had turned to look at the wall of books. "It's all—it's very big, here. Everything's very big. At home, you can see the whole of the harbour from the top of our road, and you know everybody, and you know what everyone's having for their tea. And here there's all these corridors, and it's all so old, and nobody laughs at the same bits." She stopped. "I didn't mean to say that."

"It's all right."

"I didn't know about any of it. The magic. We've got a fish shop. Mum thought the letter was a joke."

"Mine thought it was a hoax," said Mira. "My dad wrote to the _Daily Telegraph_."

Wren looked round, startled, and this time she did smile. It was a small, sudden, entirely unguarded smile. It changed the whole of her face.

"Did he?"

"He thought it was a scam. He wanted to report it."

"Mum made me go and show the postman."

"Did it work?"

"He fainted," said Wren, with a dignity that did not quite hold, and they both began to laugh.

---

She had written a poem. She showed it to Mira, after a while, with her face turned away, in the book that she'd turned to first, because it was the one that had found her: the battered copy of _Alice_, open at the Cheshire Cat. Underneath the three old notes, in the margin, where the page was still blank, there was a verse in green ink. It was four lines. It was in the same small, slanting hand.

_Come back, said the cat, to the grin on the bough,
I'm learning to vanish. I'm not good at it now.
But a smile left behind is a kind of a light:
you left yours for me. Thank you. Good night. —W._

Mira read it twice. Her eyes had gone suspiciously warm.

"That's good," she said.

"It's not."

"It is. It scans. The 'bough' and 'now' is a bit of a stretch but I think you get away with it, and the last line is really—" She stopped. "I'm not being nice. I'm being accurate. It's very good."

Wren said nothing. She looked at her feet. But the tips of her ears had gone pink.

"May I—" she began. She turned over the corner of a page. "I wondered if I could ask you things. Not all the time. It's only that I've got so many questions, and I don't want to bother the professors, they've all got such a lot to do. About the castle. And about runes. There are carvings all over the third-floor corridor and nobody will tell me what they say. And I thought—"

"Yes," said Mira.

"You don't have to—"

"I'd like to. Of course. Look, we could—" She was already reaching for her bag. "I'll put you in for Thursdays. At four. No, Friday's better, I've got Arithmancy on Thursdays, but Friday I'm in the library anyway, so—" She stopped. She'd got half of a parchment out of her bag and a pencil between her teeth. She took the pencil out. "Sorry. I do that. I make a timetable."

"I don't mind."

"I mind a bit, I think." She put the parchment back. "Come whenever you like. If I'm there, ask. If I'm not, ask someone else. That's the whole system."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. And then she nodded, once, as if something had been settled, and unscrewed the little bottle of Evergreen she'd taken out of her pocket.

"Would you like to?" she said. "Write something? It's a better colour than black."

---

It was nearly nine by the time they'd gone. Wren went first, up the stairs, in the direction of the Ravenclaw tower, with a spring in her step that hadn't been there at dinner. Mira stayed for a minute in the doorway of the alcove, looking back.

The candle had burned low. She'd put a book on the armchair. It wasn't one of the twenty. It was her own copy of _A Wizard of Earthsea_, which she'd read for the first time when she was eleven, in her bedroom in London, a month after the letter came, in the particular state of mind of someone who is being told that the impossible is real and is not at all sure how to feel. It was dog-eared, and annotated, and held together with an elastic band, and she'd turned it open at the page she remembered best.

Under the margin, in the green ink, in her own much squarer hand, she had written:

_Yes. It's all right. You're always allowed. —M._

She looked at it for a moment. Then she put out the lamp, and stepped out into the corridor, and left the book in the dark, waiting, for whoever came next.
