Mira went to the alcove on Tuesday afternoon for an hour of perfectly ordinary peace and quiet, and found herself in possession of a mystery.

It was a small one, about the size of a thumbprint. There were two ways to treat a thing like that: you could note it and set it aside, or you could regard it as the first clear piece of evidence in a case. She had spent the whole of her fourth year finding out which sort of person she was, and the answer had turned out to be the second.

The alcove was as they had left it. The armchair, flattened by decades. The little table with its melted candle. Twenty-odd battered books on three crooked shelves, bristling with other people's handwriting. And on the back wall, at eye height, held up by a sticking charm that Elliot had cast with the air of someone laying a foundation stone, a single sheet of paper.

_To whoever finds this place: You're not alone._

It was a year old now and had gone yellow at the corners. Mira had come, if she was honest, to see whether it was still there.

It was. But under the four sets of initials, where the page had been blank, somebody had written something new, in a small, careful, slightly slanting hand, in ink of a colour she had never seen on a library table. It was the green of a conifer in rain.

_Thank you. I come here after lights-out. Is that all right? —W._

---

She read it standing up. Then she sat down in the armchair, took off her glasses, rubbed her eyes, put them back on, and read it again. A smile arrived on her face that she didn't know what to do with.

_Is that all right?_

Somebody had stood in this little windowless room in the dark, with whatever light they'd brought, and read _You're part of it now_, and believed it enough to ask.

She got out a piece of parchment. It was not that she meant to find them, she told herself. It was only that, having started the conversation, she had a responsibility to know with whom she was having it.

_One: green ink._ She had seen it once, in a stationer's window in Hogsmeade. It was called Evergreen, it came in one-ounce bottles, and she remembered it because it was very pretty and extremely impractical.

_Two: handwriting._ The slant ran to the left, so the writer was left-handed, or had been taught by someone who was. There was a smudge at the end of the _W_ where a hand had dragged through the wet ink, and it was a very small hand.

_Three: the ink was still glossy._ Two days old at most.

_Four: after lights-out._

That was the interesting one. The library locked at nine. The only people with keys were Madam Oakwood and Mira, who had been given a pass in fourth year for extended research and had not, strictly, given it back. A student couldn't be in the library at night. So this student was in it when it closed, and had stayed.

She underlined that twice.

---

Madam Oakwood was at the front desk, stamping a stack of returns with a ferocity that suggested they'd done something to deserve it. She wore half-moon glasses on a chain and a cardigan the colour of porridge, and did not look up.

"Miss Patel."

"Madam Oakwood. I wondered whether I might consult the late-pass ledger."

"You may ask."

"I'm trying to find out whether anyone in the school has permission to be in the library after nine."

"No," said Madam Oakwood, stamping. "Only you."

"Right. Yes. And has anyone, to your knowledge, been in here _without_ permission? Hypothetically."

The stamp came down very precisely in the middle of the page.

"I wouldn't know," said Madam Oakwood. "I never see anything after nine. A failing of mine. My eyesight in the evenings is dreadful." She turned a page. "I rely entirely on my other senses. I will say that my hearing is excellent, Miss Patel, and in the last month I have not heard a single book dropped, a single page torn, or a single thing done that I should have to write down."

She looked up over the half-moons. Her eyes were quite mild and quite bright.

"A first-year Ravenclaw, I'd have thought, if you were asking. They tend to be the ones who need it. Now that's quite enough hypothetical. Mind the stairs."

---

It should have been simple after that. Mira would later admit that it could have been, if she had walked into the Great Hall at six and sat down next to a first-year and said hello.

What she did was prepare.

She prepared on the stairs, on the walk to the Hall, and in the queue for soup. She had opening lines. She had a question about homework, which she would ask all eight Ravenclaw first-years in turn, so that nobody would feel singled out, and which would cause the culprit to give herself away by some tiny involuntary sign. She had a quill. She had decided against the clipboard, with some regret.

"Hello," she said, to the first boy on the bench. "I'm Mira. I'm a fifth-year. I'm just going round, informally, to ask whether you use green ink."

He looked at her. "Why?"

"No reason. It's a, it's more of a general survey. Do you?"

"I use the school one."

"Excellent. Thank you. And do you happen to be left-handed?"

"No," said the boy, edging along the bench. "Are you from the prefects?"

"I'm not a prefect."

"Then why—"

She moved on. The next girl said she had once spilled some green ink, in September, on a cat. The one after that asked whether she was allowed to go to the toilet. By the fifth, the whole end of the bench had gone silent, and Mira had the clear, cold sensation of a person who has walked into a lamp-post in front of an audience.

_This is not an informal survey_, she thought. _This is an interrogation, with a quill._

At the very end of the bench, apart from the others, a small girl with a fringe had been eating her dinner in the manner of someone who has been told to, with a book propped against the water jug. She had gone completely still. As Mira looked, she whipped the book shut and sat bolt upright, and her left hand, which lay flat on the table, had green on the side of it.

Mira stood quite still. She had practised three opening lines on the stairs. All three had gone.

"Hello," she said.

"I'll stop," said the girl, very fast, in a flat northern voice. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I didn't know it was anyone's, and then I saw the note, and it said you could add, so I did, and I shouldn't have, I'll never go back, I'll take it off, I'll—"

"No," said Mira. "No, please, I—that's not why—" She stopped. She breathed. She sat down on the bench, a respectful foot away, so that they were more or less level. "I'm Mira. I wrote the note. Some of it. And I came to say—"

She heard herself. A long sentence was queued up with three clauses and a caveat.

She put it down.

"It's all right," she said. "That's all. You're allowed to be there."

There was a long pause. The girl stared at her.

"Really?"

"It was meant for whoever found it. It says so. It's the whole point."

"I know it says," said the girl, going red from the neck up. "But people say things. You can't tell if they mean it. It's like when somebody says 'pop round whenever you like,' and you do, and they're in their dressing gown."

Mira laughed. She hadn't known she was going to. It came out as a short delighted bark, and several first-years glanced round.

"I mean it," she said. "I'm in my dressing gown all the time and I mean it."

The girl didn't smile. But her shoulders came down about an inch.

"Wren," she said. "Wren Calloway."

---

They went together afterwards, in the long blue evening, and Madam Oakwood, as they passed the desk, became deeply interested in a stack of returns.

It was Wren's first time in the alcove with another person. Mira could tell. She went in the way you go into a church, on tiptoe, hands behind her back, and looked round at the shelves and the chair and the candle with the shy, bright look of someone who has been carrying something heavy and has just been told she can put it down.

"It's like somebody's left the light on," she said.

"I think that's exactly what it is."

"I didn't know where else to go." She had turned to the books. "Everything's very big here. At home you can see the whole harbour from the top of our road, and you know everybody, and you know what they're having for tea. Here it's all corridors, and it's so old, and nobody laughs at the same bits." She stopped. "I didn't mean to say that."

"It's all right."

"I didn't know about any of it. The magic. We've got a fish shop. Mum thought the letter was a joke."

"Mine thought it was a hoax," said Mira. "My dad wrote to the _Daily Telegraph_."

Wren looked round, startled, and this time she did smile, small and sudden and not guarded at all. "Did he?"

"He wanted to report it."

"Mum made me go and show the postman."

"Did it work?"

"He fainted," said Wren, with a dignity that didn't quite hold, and they both began to laugh.

---

She had written a poem. She showed Mira after a while, with her face turned away, in the book that had found her first: the battered _Alice_, open at the Cheshire Cat. Under three old notes, in the margin where the page was still blank, in green, were four lines.

_Come back, said the cat, to the grin on the bough,
I'm learning to vanish. I'm not good at it now.
But a smile left behind is a kind of a light:
you left yours for me. Thank you. Good night. —W._

Mira read it twice.

"That's good," she said.

"It's not."

"It is. It scans. The 'bough' and 'now' is a bit of a stretch but I think you get away with it, and the last line is really—" She stopped. "I'm not being nice. I'm being accurate. It's very good."

Wren said nothing. She looked at her feet. The tips of her ears had gone pink.

"May I," she began, and turned over the corner of a page. "I wondered whether I could ask you things. Not all the time. Only I've got so many questions, and I don't like to bother the professors, they've all got so much on. About the castle. And runes. There are carvings all along the third-floor corridor and nobody will tell me what they say."

"Yes," said Mira.

"You don't have to—"

"I'd like to. Of course. We could—" She was already reaching for her bag. "I'll put you in for Thursdays at four. No, Friday's better, I've Arithmancy on Thursdays, and Friday I'm in the library anyway, so—" She had got half a parchment out of her bag and a pencil between her teeth. She took the pencil out. "Sorry. I do that. I make a timetable."

"I don't mind."

"I mind a bit, I think." She put the parchment back. "Come whenever you like. If I'm there, ask. If I'm not, ask somebody else. That's the whole system."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, as if something had been settled, and unscrewed a small bottle of Evergreen from her pocket.

"Would you like to write something?" she said. "It's a better colour than black."

---

It was nearly nine by the time they left. Wren went first, up towards the Ravenclaw tower, with a spring in her step that hadn't been there at dinner. Mira stayed a minute in the doorway of the alcove, looking back.

The candle had burned low. She had put a book on the armchair. It wasn't one of the twenty. It was her own copy of _A Wizard of Earthsea_, which she had read for the first time at eleven, in her bedroom in London, a month after the letter came, in the state of mind of someone who has been told the impossible is real and isn't sure how to feel. It was dog-eared, annotated and held together with an elastic band, and she had opened it at the page she remembered best.

Under the margin, in green (Wren had left the bottle on the table with its cork out, which was either an accident or not), in her own much squarer hand, she had written:

_Yes. It's all right. You're always allowed. —M._

She looked at it for a moment. Then she put out the lamp and stepped into the corridor, and left the book in the dark, waiting for whoever came next.
