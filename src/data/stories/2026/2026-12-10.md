"I've been practising," said Oliver, "in the mirror."

He said it in the tone of a man confessing to a crime, with his back to the greenhouse bench and his hands, which he had no idea what to do with, wrapped round a watering can he'd been holding for several minutes. It was a quarter past four on Thursday afternoon. Outside, the light was already going, a thin watery blue leaking out of the sky over the lake, and in Greenhouse One the warm lamps had come on, one by one, over the seed trays, and the glass had steamed up so thoroughly that the whole of the world beyond it had turned to soft grey wool.

"Practising what?" said Finn.

"Shaking hands."

"You can shake hands."

"I _can_," said Oliver. "In general. In theory. But there's a moment, you see, in the middle of it, where you've got to decide how hard to grip, and I get to that bit and I think, _is this too firm_? And then I think, _is it too limp_? And then I look down and I'm still holding on, and it's been about eleven seconds." He put the watering can down. He picked it up again. "I did it forty times last night. I've a feeling my hand's got worse."

Finn had a good many things he could have said. He had a speech, which he'd been building, in his head, for most of a week, on the subject of how his dad was the gentlest man in the Highlands. He'd tried it out on the Mimbulus. It had listened politely and done nothing.

He put down his trowel instead.

"Right," he said. "We need to practise properly."

---

Asking Zara had been the easy part.

That was the odd thing, and he'd noticed it at the time. It had taken him about ninety seconds, at lunch, at the Gryffindor table, where he'd stood on the end of the bench with his hands in his pockets and said, "Zara, I need a favour," and she'd put down her fork and looked at him with her whole face. It was a thing she did. When you asked her for something, she gave you all of herself, at once, without a second thought, like opening a door.

"It's going to sound daft," he'd said.

"Good."

"I need you to play my dad."

"Do I get a moustache?"

"I hadn't—"

"I get a moustache," said Zara, with a gleam, and was already standing up. "I've got a coat. Where? When?"

He'd walked back to the Hufflepuff table with his ears pink, and a feeling in his chest that he'd hardly known the name of. It was a curious, loose, grateful sort of feeling, the feeling of reaching out and finding the hand exactly where it had always been. He'd been afraid to ask for weeks. He wasn't sure now why.

The chair at the end of his own bench was still pulled out, a hand's breadth from the table. He'd walked straight past it.

---

She arrived at twenty past four, and the greenhouse went quiet.

It was the coat. It was an enormous, brown, heavy tweed overcoat, of the sort that belonged on a gamekeeper, with leather patches at the elbows and pockets you could have hidden a goat in, and it came right down to her ankles. She'd turned the sleeves up four times and they were still too long. She'd tied a belt round her middle. She had a flat tweed cap pulled low over her braids, and, centred under her nose, with an unwavering and terrible dignity, a very large, very bristly, very red moustache.

"It's from Zonko's," she said, in a deep, hoarse voice. "It was sold as the Distinguished Gentleman. It's not."

"I'd say it's slightly Hagrid," said Oliver, faintly.

"It's _Scottish_."

"It's got a bit of the Isle of Man—"

"Sit down, lad," said Zara, "and tell me about yerself."

It was, without question, the worst Scottish accent that Finn had ever heard. It began somewhere near Glasgow, wandered south through Yorkshire, took a wrong turn in County Cork, and arrived, somewhere around the word _yerself_, at something that sounded very much like a man with a mouthful of toffee. He turned round quickly and pretended to inspect a tray of seedlings.

"It's a good start," he said, into a lettuce.

---

It went wrong in the first minute.

They'd arranged it as an interview. Oliver sat on an upturned crate. Zara sat on a stool, with her knees apart and her thumbs hooked in her belt, the way she thought a father sat, and she looked at him down the length of her moustache with the steady, searching, slightly menacing attention of a man about to buy a horse.

"So," she said. "Fenwick."

"Yes, sir."

"Ye're a Hufflepuff."

"Yes, sir."

"Fifth year."

"Yes, sir."

"And what'll yer wand be?"

"Alder," said Oliver. He'd gone, by this point, a delicate shade of green. "Alder, eleven inches, springy. With a, ah." He swallowed. "A dragon heartstring core."

"Is that right," said Zara. She'd put her chin in her hand. "A heartstring. Which kind of dragon?"

"I don't—I mean, I never asked. Hungarian? A Hebridean?" He floundered. "It might be unicorn."

"Unicorn."

"It's—no. It's dragon. Definitely dragon heartstring." He clapped his hands over his face. "I don't know. I don't know what's in my own wand."

"Take yer time," said Zara, in her cracked, kindly, completely implausible voice. "Ye've plenty of it."

"Dragon heartstring," said Oliver, into his fingers. "Dragon heartstring. Sir."

Finn had put down the seedling tray. He had put it down because he could no longer be trusted with it. He had begun, without any warning, to shake. It started in his stomach and worked up through his chest, and by the time it reached his face it had turned into a long, silent, helpless gasping that bent him over double against the bench, with tears running down his cheeks.

"What's funny?" said Oliver, through his hands. "Why is he laughing? I haven't done anything."

"You've said it _twice_," said Finn.

"I know I've said it twice."

"And then you said unicorn. And then you went back."

"I _know_—"

"It's—you looked—" Finn gave up. He slid down the bench and sat on the floor among the pots, with his hands over his mouth, and laughed, and laughed, until his ribs hurt and a trowel fell off the shelf and rang. Over on the stool the tall figure in the tweed coat was having much the same trouble. The moustache had begun to peel, at one corner, and she'd clamped a hand over it to hold it in place, and made a noise like a leaking tyre.

And in the middle of it all, Oliver took his hands from his face and looked at the pair of them, with his hair standing up and his ears scarlet, and a very slow smile began to spread.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, it's quite funny."

"It's _so_ funny," said Zara, wheezing.

"It was the 'sir' that did it," said Oliver. "I think. I said 'sir' about nine times."

"Twelve."

"I'm going to say it to your actual dad. I'm going to meet him at the front door and say, 'Good morning, sir. It's dragon heartstring, sir.'"

"Don't you dare," said Finn.

They were all, by now, in pieces. It was some time before anybody could say anything useful.

---

It was after Zara had gone to find some tea that Oliver said it.

She had stood up, in the end, shaking the coat down, holding her moustache to her lip with one finger. "Give me ten minutes," she'd said. "I'm going to ask Dibby for a flask, and a proper stool. A man can't give advice from a crate." And she'd gone, with great dignity, round the end of the bench, trailing about two feet of tweed behind her. The door had banged. The greenhouse had gone quiet. In the lamplight, somewhere in the dim, steamy green distance, a drip fell into a tray.

They sat side by side on the floor.

"You're as nervous as I am," said Oliver.

Finn looked at him.

"Not about this. About something else." He wasn't looking at Finn. He was looking at the floor between his shoes, in the manner of someone choosing the words with great care. "You've been carrying a letter about for a week. You get it out at dinner, and you read it, and then you put it away. And you keep touching your pocket. And then there's the other thing."

"What other thing?"

"The face you do. When you think nobody's looking." He did a very small, accurate imitation. It was an absent, stricken, faraway expression, as if somebody had just reminded him of a deadline. "I don't mind. I only wondered if it was anything to do with me."

"It's not you."

"I thought it probably wasn't."

"It's—" said Finn. He stopped.

He had not, until that moment, any intention of saying it. He'd gone through it in his head a dozen times, in the dark, in the dormitory, in the long hours at the back of the Herbology lecture. In each of them he'd said it to somebody else: to Sprout, to Mum, to a pillow. He had never once thought of it as a thing you could just tell the person sitting next to you on a greenhouse floor.

It was in his pocket. The letter. The one from his dad, with its _second bench_ and its length of rowan.

He took it out, and held it, and found that he could not look at it.

"Professor Sprout's put me up for something," he said. "A placement. At Fenmoor. It's a garden, in the fens, a proper one, with Gurdyroots, and a Mimbulus that's older than the Ministry. It's six weeks, in July. They take six students." He heard the words coming out in a rush. "She put my name forward in October. She didn't tell me. I have to say yes or no by the middle of January."

There was a pause.

"Finn," said Oliver. "That's _brilliant_."

"I know."

"That's—you didn't say. Why didn't you—"

"It's July," said Finn. "It's the wand shop. It's the first-years' orders, and the shop gets—it gets mad. Dad does everything himself, and I've always been there, every year, I do the sweeping, and the cores. He's cleared the second bench. He's saving some rowan. He wrote and told me, last week, and I didn't—" He swallowed. "I don't know how to say it. I don't know how you tell someone you love that you want something that isn't the thing they've kept for you."

Oliver didn't say anything for a while.

Behind them, on its hook, where Finn had hung it at the start of the lesson, his lantern was burning very low. He had hardly noticed it. It was a small, gentle, steady gold, a good deal dimmer than it had been all week, as if something in it had been lifted off.

"He'll want to know," said Oliver, quietly.

"I know he will."

"Even if it's difficult. _Especially_ if it's difficult. Finn, I've met—" He stopped. "I haven't met your dad. But I've met you. And if you came from him, I'd say he's the sort of person who'd rather hear it from you."

"I'll write," said Finn.

He'd put his hand in his pocket, without meaning to. It had found the Remembrall. It was a small, smooth, cold, empty sphere, no heavier than an egg. He turned it over once between his fingers.

"Not yet," he said. "But I'll write."

"Good," said Oliver. He moved an inch along the floor, until their shoulders touched. "You don't have to do it today."

---

She was back in ten minutes with a flask, a three-legged stool, and a plate of ginger biscuits that she'd negotiated out of the kitchen under cover of the moustache. She'd re-stuck it. It was a good deal more crooked.

"Right," said Zara, setting everything down. "Where were we? Ah." She cleared her throat. She planted herself on the stool, and put one hand on each knee, and fixed the pair of them with a long, careful look. "We'd got to the part where the lad's said _dragon heartstring_ a great deal."

"We have," said Oliver.

"And he's a nice lad."

"I'd like to think so."

"And he's frightened out of his wits."

"Largely."

"So I'll tell him this," said Zara. She'd dropped the accent. That was the strange thing. She'd stopped doing a voice, somewhere between one sentence and the next, and what she was doing now was much quieter, and plainer, and sat in her own mouth as though it belonged there. "Now then. I don't know what they've told yer, but I'll tell yer what I know. I've made wands for thirty years, and the thing I've learned is that no wand does a thing it doesn't want to. Yer can't force a core. Yer can't hurry it. Yer put the best you have in the room, and yer wait, and it finds its own way."

She stopped. She looked at Finn, and then at Oliver, from under the brim of the cap, with the large, bristling moustache slightly askew.

"Whatever lad ye've brought home, son," she said, "bring him in. Put the kettle on. I'll not be asking about cores." She nodded. "The shop will keep."

Nobody said anything.

It was not, Finn thought, a speech that anyone had written. It had no business being as good as it was. And it landed in the warm green silence of the greenhouse, among the steaming pots and the dripping glass, with the soft, particular weight of something that has come in at exactly the right time and at exactly the wrong angle, and he sat on the floor with his arms round his knees and could not, at all, think of a thing to say.

"Too much?" said Zara, in her own voice.

"No," said Finn. His throat was tight. "No. Just right."

"Really? I thought the moustache was doing a lot of the work."

"It was a good moustache," said Oliver.

She looked at him, narrowly, and then at Finn. She didn't ask. She had a kind of tact, Zara, that she very rarely showed, that came into the room like a cat and didn't make a noise.

"Right," she said. "Well. Stand up, Fenwick. Let's have the handshake."

Oliver got up. He wiped his palm on the front of his robe. He was, Finn saw, still very slightly shaking, and also, very slightly, grinning. He crossed the greenhouse floor and held out his hand to the tall figure on the stool, who had risen, and put out her own, with its sleeve rolled up four times, across the gap.

They shook. It lasted exactly three seconds. It was firm, and it was brief, and it was without any doubt the best handshake in the history of Hufflepuff House.

And then, because neither of them seemed to know quite how to finish it, they both gave a very small, formal, ridiculous bow.
