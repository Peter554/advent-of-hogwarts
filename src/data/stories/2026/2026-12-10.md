"I've been practising," said Oliver, "in the mirror."

He said it like a man confessing to a crime, with his back to the greenhouse bench and his hands wrapped round a watering can he'd been holding for several minutes. It was a quarter past four on Thursday afternoon. Outside, the light was going, a watery blue leaking out of the sky over the lake, and in Greenhouse One the warm lamps had come on over the seed trays and the glass had steamed up so thoroughly that the world beyond it had turned to grey wool.

"Practising what?" said Finn.

"Shaking hands."

"You can shake hands."

"I _can_," said Oliver. "In general. In theory. But there's a moment in the middle where you've got to decide how hard to grip, and I get to that bit and think, _is this too firm_? And then, _is it too limp_? And then I look down and I'm still holding on, and it's been about eleven seconds." He put the watering can down. He picked it up again. "I did it forty times last night. I think my hand's got worse."

Finn had a good many things he could have said. He had a speech, which he'd been building for most of a week, on how his dad was the gentlest man in the Highlands. He'd tried it on the Mimbulus. It had listened politely and done nothing.

He put down his trowel instead.

"Right," he said. "We need to practise properly."

---

Asking Zara had been the easy part.

That was the odd thing. It had taken about ninety seconds, at lunch, at the Gryffindor table, where he'd stood at the end of the bench with his hands in his pockets and said, "Zara, I need a favour," and she'd put down her fork and given him her full attention. It was a thing she did. When you asked her for something, she opened like a door.

"It's going to sound daft," he'd said.

"Good."

"I need you to play my dad."

"Do I get a moustache?"

"I hadn't—"

"I get a moustache," said Zara, already standing up. "I've got a coat. Where? When?"

He'd walked back to the Hufflepuff table with his ears pink and a loose, grateful feeling, the feeling of reaching out and finding a hand exactly where it had always been. He'd been afraid to ask for weeks. He wasn't sure now why.

---

She arrived at twenty past four, and the greenhouse went quiet.

It was the coat. It was a vast brown tweed overcoat, the sort that belonged on a gamekeeper, with leather patches at the elbows and pockets you could have hidden a goat in, and it came down to her ankles. She'd turned the sleeves up four times and they were still too long. She had tied a belt round her middle and pulled a flat cap low over her braids, and centred under her nose, with unwavering dignity, sat a very large, very bristly, very red moustache.

"It's from Zonko's," she said, in a deep hoarse voice. "It was sold as the Distinguished Gentleman. It's not."

"I'd say it's slightly Hagrid," said Oliver, faintly.

"It's _Scottish_."

"It's got a bit of the Isle of Man—"

"Sit down, lad," said Zara, "and tell me about yerself."

It was the worst Scottish accent Finn had ever heard. It began somewhere near Glasgow, wandered south through Yorkshire, took a wrong turn in County Cork, and arrived, around the word _yerself_, at a man with a mouthful of toffee. He turned round quickly and pretended to inspect a tray of seedlings.

"It's a good start," he said, into a lettuce.

---

It went wrong in the first minute.

They'd arranged it as an interview. Oliver sat on an upturned crate. Zara sat on a stool with her knees apart and her thumbs hooked in her belt, the way she thought a father sat, and looked at him down the length of her moustache like a man about to buy a horse.

"So," she said. "Fenwick."

"Yes, sir."

"Ye're a Hufflepuff."

"Yes, sir."

"Fifth year."

"Yes, sir."

"And what'll yer wand be?"

"Alder," said Oliver, who had gone a delicate shade of green. "Alder, eleven inches, springy. With a, ah." He swallowed. "A dragon heartstring core."

"Is that right," said Zara, chin in hand. "A heartstring. Which kind of dragon?"

"I don't—I mean, I never asked. Hungarian? A Hebridean?" He floundered. "It might be unicorn."

"Unicorn."

"No. It's dragon. Definitely dragon heartstring." He clapped his hands over his face. "I don't know. I don't know what's in my own wand."

"Take yer time," said Zara, in her cracked, kindly, wholly implausible voice. "Ye've plenty of it."

"Dragon heartstring," said Oliver, into his fingers. "Dragon heartstring. Sir."

Finn had put down the seedling tray because he could no longer be trusted with it. The shaking started in his stomach and worked up through his chest, and by the time it reached his face it had become a long silent gasp that bent him double against the bench, tears running down his cheeks.

"What's funny?" said Oliver, through his hands. "Why is he laughing? I haven't done anything."

"You've said it _twice_," said Finn.

"I know I've said it twice."

"And then you said unicorn. And then you went back."

"I _know_—"

"It's—you looked—" Finn gave up. He slid down the bench and sat on the floor among the pots with his hands over his mouth and laughed until his ribs hurt and a trowel fell off the shelf and rang. On the stool, the tall figure in the tweed coat was having much the same trouble. The moustache had begun to peel at one corner, and she'd clamped a hand over it to hold it on, and was making a noise like a leaking tyre.

In the middle of it, Oliver took his hands from his face and looked at the pair of them, hair on end and ears scarlet, and a slow smile began to spread.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, it's quite funny."

"It's _so_ funny," said Zara, wheezing.

"It was the 'sir' that did it," said Oliver. "I said 'sir' about nine times."

"Twelve."

"I'm going to say it to your actual dad. I'm going to meet him at the front door and say, 'Good morning, sir. It's dragon heartstring, sir.'"

"Don't you dare," said Finn.

It was some time before anybody could say anything useful.

---

It was after Zara had gone to find tea that Oliver said it.

She had stood up at last, shaking the coat down, holding her moustache to her lip with one finger. "Give me ten minutes," she'd said. "I'm going to ask Dibby for a flask and a proper stool. A man can't give advice from a crate." She'd swept out round the end of the bench, trailing two feet of tweed. The door banged. Somewhere in the steamy green distance, a drip fell into a tray.

They sat side by side on the floor.

"You're as nervous as I am," said Oliver.

Finn looked at him.

"Not about this. About something else." He was looking at the floor between his shoes, choosing his words. "You've been carrying a letter about for a week. You get it out at dinner and read it and put it away. You keep touching your pocket. And then there's the face you do when you think nobody's looking." He did a small, accurate imitation: absent and stricken, as if someone had just mentioned a deadline. "I don't mind. I only wondered if it was anything to do with me."

"It's not you."

"I thought it probably wasn't."

"It's—" said Finn. He stopped.

He hadn't meant to say it. He'd gone through it a dozen times in the dark of the dormitory and at the back of the Herbology lecture, and each time he'd said it to somebody else: to Sprout, to Mum, to a pillow. It had never occurred to him that you could just tell the person next to you on a greenhouse floor.

The letter was in his pocket. The one from his dad, with its _second bench_ and its length of rowan. He took it out and found he couldn't look at it.

"Professor Sprout's put me up for something," he said. "A placement. At Fenmoor. It's a garden, in the fens, a proper one, with Gurdyroots, and a Mimbulus that's older than the Ministry. Six weeks, in July. They take six students." The words came out in a rush. "She put my name forward in October and didn't tell me. I have to say yes or no by the middle of January."

There was a pause.

"Finn," said Oliver. "That's _brilliant_."

"I know."

"You didn't say. Why didn't you—"

"It's July," said Finn. "It's the wand shop. It's the first-years' orders and the shop goes mad. Dad does everything himself, and I've always been there, every year, sweeping and sorting cores. He's cleared the second bench. He's saving some rowan. He wrote and told me last week, and I didn't—" He swallowed. "I don't know how you tell someone you want something that isn't the thing they've kept for you."

Oliver said nothing for a while.

Behind them, on its hook, Finn's lantern was burning very low, a steady gold and a good deal dimmer than it had been all week. He hadn't noticed.

"He'll want to know," said Oliver, quietly.

"I know he will."

"Even if it's difficult. _Especially_ if it's difficult. I haven't met your dad. But I've met you, and if you came from him, I'd say he's the sort who'd rather hear it from you."

"I'll write," said Finn.

His hand had gone to his other pocket without his meaning it to and found the Remembrall, small and smooth and cold, no heavier than an egg. He turned it over once between his fingers.

"Not yet," he said. "But I'll write."

"Good," said Oliver. He moved an inch along the floor until their shoulders touched. "You don't have to do it today."

---

She was back in ten minutes with a flask, a three-legged stool, and a plate of ginger biscuits negotiated out of the kitchens under cover of the moustache. She'd re-stuck it. It was a good deal more crooked.

"Right," said Zara, setting everything down. "Where were we? Ah." She planted herself on the stool, a hand on each knee, and fixed them with a long look. "We'd got to the part where the lad's said _dragon heartstring_ a great deal."

"We have," said Oliver.

"And he's a nice lad."

"I'd like to think so."

"And he's frightened out of his wits."

"Largely."

"So I'll tell him this," said Zara. She'd dropped the accent, which was the strange thing. Somewhere between one sentence and the next she'd stopped doing a voice, and what she did now was quieter and plainer and sat in her own mouth as if it belonged there. "Now then. I've made wands for thirty years, and the thing I've learned is that no wand does what it doesn't want to. Yer can't force a core. Yer put the best you have in the room, and yer wait, and it finds its own way."

She looked at Finn, and then at Oliver, from under the brim of the cap, moustache slightly askew.

"Whatever lad ye've brought home, son, bring him in. Put the kettle on. I'll not be asking about cores." She nodded. "The shop will keep."

Nobody said anything.

It was not, Finn thought, a speech anyone had written. It had no business being as good as it was. It landed in the warm green quiet among the steaming pots, and he sat on the floor with his arms round his knees and couldn't think of a thing to say.

"Too much?" said Zara, in her own voice.

"No," said Finn. His throat was tight. "No. Just right."

"Really? I thought the moustache was doing a lot of the work."

"It was a good moustache," said Oliver.

She looked at him narrowly, and then at Finn. She didn't ask. Zara had a tact she rarely showed, which came into the room like a cat and made no noise.

"Right," she said. "Well. Stand up, Fenwick. Let's have the handshake."

Oliver got up. He wiped his palm on the front of his robe. He was, Finn saw, still very slightly shaking, and also very slightly grinning. He crossed the greenhouse floor and held out his hand to the tall figure on the stool, who had risen, and put out her own, sleeve rolled up four times, across the gap.

They shook. It lasted exactly three seconds. It was firm, and brief, and without any doubt the best handshake in the history of Hufflepuff House.

Then, because neither of them seemed to know how to finish it, they both gave a small, formal, ridiculous bow.
