"Forty," said Professor McGonagall.

She said it at breakfast, from the staff table, in the voice she kept for the really serious numbers, and let it hang over the porridge until the whole Great Hall had stopped talking and turned to look at the trees.

There were twelve of them. Hagrid had brought them in on the first of the month, one at a time, on a sledge he'd rigged to his shoulders: twelve towering Scottish firs in great tubs of earth along the walls, tips brushing the enchanted ceiling, the whole Hall smelling of resin and cold. Every year they were hung with old blown-glass baubles in reds and golds and ocean blues, each with a tiny scene inside that moved if you looked long enough: a skating rink, a Hippogriff in flight, a very small house with smoke coming out of the chimney.

The trees were bare. Not quite bare, but from where Zara sat, with her toast halfway to her mouth, they looked like somebody who has been mugged. Whole sections, from the floor to about five feet up, had been picked clean as bones.

"Forty baubles in three nights," said McGonagall. "They are hung each evening. They are gone each morning. I have had the house-elves in. I have had Mr Filch in, who is of no use whatsoever. And I am told by Hagrid that it is pixies."

"It's the very look of pixies!" rumbled a voice from the end of the table.

"It is _not_ pixies," said McGonagall, "since pixies do not, as a rule, take things neatly. I should like someone to find out what it is. The Hall will be watched tonight from midnight until two, from a place of concealment. I am looking for a volunteer."

Somebody coughed. Jamie, next to Zara, studied her egg with every appearance of interest in the shell.

"I'll do it," said Zara.

She was on her feet before she knew it. McGonagall looked at her over the top of her square glasses, and one eyebrow went up a quarter of an inch.

"Miss Okonkwo. I should have thought you had your hands full."

"I haven't, Professor." It was true, in a way that surprised her. Since Sunday she had a great deal of spare energy, the sort that builds up in a person who's lost something and has nowhere to put it. "I'd like to. If that's all right."

"Very well. Twelve till two, in the Hall, and not a sound. You may take a flask. Do not, under any circumstances, fall asleep."

"I won't."

"Hm," said McGonagall, which in her case could mean almost anything.

Zara sat down. Jamie leaned across. "You're not doing it alone."

"I'm fine."

"You're fine at everything," said Jamie. "It's a symptom."

---

Zara's plan was in her bag. It was on two sheets of parchment, drawn in three colours of ink, and it had arrows. It had the Hall divided into zones. It had a tripwire made of tinsel, a bucket of water charmed to tip itself at a signal, and, in the bottom left corner, a note to self: _Pixies fast. Use Glacius?_

She'd been rather pleased with it all afternoon. By midnight, crouched under the high table with her knees under her chin, behind a white cloth that hung to the floor, it seemed a lot less of a plan and a lot more of a bucket.

The Hall at midnight was another place entirely. The ceiling was a deep clear black full of cold stars. The long tables had been pushed back against the walls. The floating candles had been put out, or nearly: a few still drifted by the high table, a dozen yards apart, like the last lights in a village. And the twelve trees stood in the dark like tall silent guests, hung with what baubles they had left, which glimmered and ticked.

At half past twelve she heard something. It wasn't what she'd been listening for. It was behind her: a small, careful shuffle, and an indrawn breath, as of somebody who has just caught her robe on a table leg.

"Don't turn round," said a voice. "It's only me."

"_Amara?_"

"Shh."

"What are you _doing_ here?"

"Same as you." A small round face slid in beside her under the cloth, with its two puffs of hair, over a dressing gown printed with hedgehogs. "Hagrid told me about the trees. He said pixies. I said it wasn't. He said how do you know, and I said, because pixies don't take things in a pattern."

"A pattern?"

"They've all been from the bottom. Never the top. Pixies fly." She tucked her feet in. "It's something that can't reach."

Zara looked at her.

"How did you get out?"

"Hufflepuff's right next to the kitchens. It's the easiest to get out of. Everybody knows." She produced a paper bag from her sleeve. "I brought biscuits. Tibby gave me them. A stakeout without snacks isn't a stakeout, it's a stand-around."

"You can't be here. You'll get detention. I'll get detention. McGonagall said not a sound—"

"I'm being _very_ quiet."

"You've been talking for a minute."

"That's only whispering." She unrolled the bag. "Do you want a biscuit or not?"

There was a moment, a short one, in the dark, with the trees ticking and her little sister settling into the cushion like a bird into a nest, when Zara had a clear and complete plan for what to do. It involved taking Amara by the hand, walking her straight back to the kitchens, and tucking her up in bed.

She opened her mouth to say so.

She thought of a shed, and a Knarl in a paper hat, and a girl with her chin up saying _I'm not your project_.

She shut her mouth, and took a biscuit.

---

"What's that?" whispered Amara, a little after one.

"My plan."

Amara read it by the light of the nearest candle. She read the zones and the arrows and the tinsel. She turned it over to see if there was a second page, and there was.

"Is the bucket to catch it?"

"It's to alarm it."

"With water?"

"It's a very _controlled_ bucket."

"I think," said Amara, in the kindest possible voice, "that it's a Niffler."

"It's not a Niffler. A Niffler is—" Zara stopped. "How do you know?"

"I don't. But look."

A little way along the wall, on the flagstones at the foot of the third tree, in a scatter of pine needles and dry dust, a line of tiny dents had appeared like stitches. Each was about the size of a thumb. They were paired, and wide at the toe, like a pair of small spades.

"It's coming from behind," breathed Amara. She was kneeling up with a hand on Zara's arm, gripping hard. "Oh. It's so _small_."

It was. Something was moving at the foot of the tree, in the shadow, so dark against the dark that it was almost invisible: a low round glossy creature with a long snout and a very round belly, reaching with two black delicate paws for the lowest of the baubles. A pouch on its stomach was open. It was already very full.

"It's a Niffler," whispered Amara. "Oh, it's a _Niffler_."

"It's Biscuit," said Zara.

She said it too loudly. The Niffler froze, one paw out, a small gold bauble in its grip, and turned its head. Its eyes were as black as pins. They met hers.

Then Biscuit tucked the bauble into his pouch and ran.

"Get him!" said Zara. She had got halfway out from under the cloth on her hands and knees when a small hand caught the back of her collar and hauled.

"_Don't_," said Amara, low and urgent. "You'll scare him. If you chase him he'll bolt and never come out again. Look where he's going."

Something glittered on the flagstones in a thin faint line: gold flecks from a bauble that had cracked in his pouch. It ran along the foot of the wall, past the fourth tree and the fifth, and turned at the end of the Hall through a small arched door.

"We just follow," said Amara. "Slowly."

"Slowly," said Zara, with the air of someone agreeing to a very large dentist.

---

She knew the place when she saw it. It was a long way down: through the arched door, down a steep stair, along a low passage lined with barrels, behind a tapestry of a sleeping dragon, and down a second stair that was mostly a ladder. She'd been here once before, the year before, with three friends and a stolen quill. It smelled as it had then, of damp stone and old metal and the particular bright smell of treasure.

The room at the bottom was low and round and about twenty feet across, and its whole floor was gold.

It was not gold. It was buttons. It was coins and spoons and old bottle-tops and thimbles, prefects' badges, watch-chains, bent tin whistles; a thousand small unconsidered shining things sorted with infinite care into spirals and arcs, the largest on the outside and the smallest in the middle, like the pattern of a shell. The light from Amara's wand ran over it, and a thousand points of brightness jumped back.

In the middle, on a heap of silver spoons, with forty glass baubles arranged around him in a perfect ring, sat Biscuit. He looked at them. He did not look in the least ashamed.

"Oh," said Amara, very softly. "He's _beautiful_."

It took Zara about forty seconds to ruin it.

"Right," she said, striding forward with her hands on her hips. "Biscuit. That's enough. Those aren't yours. They belong to the school, and they've got to go back, all of them, now, and if you hand them over I promise you'll get a, a fair—"

Biscuit hissed. It was a small, wet, surprisingly savage hiss, like a kettle with a grudge. He gathered the nearest bauble to his chest and glared at her out of eyes the size of peppercorns.

"I'm being reasonable," said Zara.

He hissed again.

"I am the captain of the Gryffindor Quidditch team, and I'm telling you—"

"Zara."

"What?"

"Sit down."

"I'm in the middle of—"

"_Sit down_," said Amara, in precisely the voice their mother used when she'd had enough of someone's nonsense. Zara sat. She hadn't decided to. It was the voice.

Amara was already on the floor, cross-legged, a good ten feet from the heap, with her hands in her lap and her head bowed so that she wasn't looking at the Niffler. After a minute, without hurrying, she took something from her pocket and set it on the floor in front of her. It was a large round gold-coloured button with an anchor on it.

"It's from my old school blazer," she said, quietly, to nobody. "From before. I kept it."

And then she did nothing at all.

It went on for a very long time. Zara counted, at first, and got to three hundred and lost her place. Biscuit's nose twitched. One foot came forward an inch and went back. Zara sat on her hands, which was a thing she had never in her life had to do, and looked at the ceiling, the stones, a spoon, and found that she was, to her surprise, completely absorbed.

At some point, without warning, Biscuit came across the floor. He did it in one rush, low and fast, like a spilled drink. He snatched the button and tucked it into his pouch. Then, with a quick shy look at Amara, he reached behind him, took a bauble from the ring, and rolled it gently across the floor to her feet.

She picked it up. She said "Thank you" as if he'd done her an enormous favour.

"Oh my goodness," said Zara.

"Shh."

"It _worked_."

"It's only the first one," said Amara, without moving her lips. "There's thirty-nine. Get the bucket."

---

It took a little under an hour. Every trade had to be done separately: a button for a bauble, a thimble for a bauble, a very good bent spoon. Amara had come with her pockets full and doled them out one at a time. Zara held the bucket. She was not a person who held buckets. But somewhere around the twentieth bauble, kneeling on the cold floor with the glass chiming quietly in the pail, it occurred to her that she was happy, in a surprised way, and had not been for several weeks.

"You're really good at this," she said, very low, so as not to frighten him.

"At what?"

"All of it. Him." She nodded at Biscuit, who sat in front of her sister now like an old friend, paws folded on his stomach. "I couldn't have done it. I'd have been hissed at all night."

"You were hissed at all night."

"That's what I'm saying."

Amara's face did something complicated in the lamplight. "I've been practising on Nettle," she said. "He's much harder. He doesn't even like buttons."

"What does he like?"

"Nothing. It's very difficult." She was trying not to smile and failing. "You're not so bad yourself. At sitting. For someone who's never done it."

"I've done loads."

"Name one."

"I waited for you to stop hating me."

It came out before she'd decided. It hung in the glittering dark between them, quite small.

"I didn't hate you," said Amara, after what felt like a long time. "I just wanted a bit of room."

"I know."

"You were _everywhere_."

"I know."

"I like it when you're somewhere," said Amara. "It's the everywhere I don't like."

"Right," said Zara. She breathed. "Somewhere. I can do somewhere."

"Good," said Amara, and held out the last of the buttons.

---

Hagrid found them in the middle of the last trade, with a lantern the size of a bucket and a very large dog.

"There yeh are," said his voice from the top of the ladder. "Been lookin' fer yeh two hours. Fang caught the scent o' biscuits an' wouldn't be—oh." A vast bearded face appeared in the hatch and went by degrees from alarm to astonishment to a kind of helpless joy. "Biscuit! Yeh little rascal. The _trees_!"

"He's given them back," said Amara. "All of them. We traded."

"Yeh traded?"

"With buttons."

Hagrid came down the ladder, which took some time, and stood among the heaps with his hat in his hands, looking at the Niffler and the bucket and the two girls, smiling so hard that his beard shook. "That's the best bit o' sense I've seen in a month. Pixies, I said. It's all very well bein' wrong, but yeh've ter be wrong in the right direction."

"Hagrid," said Amara, shyly. "Can he have one? To keep. Just one. He's been so good about it, and it's Christmas—"

"Which one?"

There was a small bent tin star on a string, tucked in with the glass ones, so cheap and so dented that Zara hadn't noticed it. It was the sort of thing you'd put on the top of a very small tree. Biscuit was looking at it with an expression that could only be called yearning.

"That one," said Amara.

"Seems fair," said Hagrid gravely, and held it out, and Biscuit took it in both paws with a small noise like a hiccup and carried it away to the exact centre of his spiral and set it down.

---

They went back up the long stairs a little after three, the two of them, with a bucket between them. The baubles chimed with every step. Hagrid had gone ahead with Fang and the lantern, murmuring about hot chocolate, and the corridor was dark and narrow, with a faint glimmer of starlight at the end of it.

"I'll take it," said Zara.

"It's all right."

"It's heavy."

"I know it's heavy." Amara had a hold of one handle. "I'll take this side."

"We could—"

"We could," said Amara, "go at the same speed."

It was, Zara thought, a perfectly sensible suggestion. She took the other handle. They set off shoulder to shoulder along the stone passage, the pail swinging between them and the glass chiming, left foot, right foot, not hurrying, not talking, in a rough uneven sort of time that was perfectly all right.

After a while Amara began to hum. It was a quiet, wandering tune. It was their grandmother's song.

After another while, Zara joined in.
