"Forty," said Professor McGonagall.

She said it at breakfast, from the staff table, in the voice she used for all the really serious numbers, and she let it hang there, among the porridge bowls, until the whole of the Great Hall had stopped talking and turned to look at the trees.

There were twelve of them. Hagrid had brought them in on the first of the month, one by one, on a sort of sledge that he'd rigged to his shoulders; twelve towering Scottish firs, planted in great tubs of earth along either wall, with their tips brushing the enchanted ceiling and the whole hall smelling of resin and cold. Every year they were hung with baubles. They were old, blown-glass things, hundreds of them, in reds and golds and deep ocean blues, each with a tiny scene inside that moved if you looked at it long enough: a skating rink, a Hippogriff in flight, a very small house with smoke coming out of the chimney.

The trees were bare.

Not quite bare. But from where Zara sat, at the Gryffindor table, with her toast halfway to her mouth, they had the thin, dishevelled look of a person who's been mugged. Great gaps showed in the branches. Whole sections, from the floor to about five feet up, had been picked as clean as bones.

"Forty baubles," said McGonagall, "in three nights. They are hung each evening. They are gone each morning. I have had the House-elves in. I have had Mr Filch in, who is of no use whatsoever. And I am told, by Hagrid, that it's pixies."

"Pixies!" came a rumbling voice from the end of the table. "It's the very look of it!"

"It is _not_ pixies," said McGonagall, "since pixies do not, as a rule, take things neatly. I should like someone to find out what it is. The Hall will be watched tonight, from midnight until two, from a place of concealment. I am looking for a volunteer."

A hush fell. Somebody coughed. Jamie, next to Zara, studied her egg with every appearance of an interest in the shell.

"I'll do it," said Zara.

She was on her feet before she knew it. A few heads turned. McGonagall looked at her for a moment over the top of her square glasses, and one eyebrow went up, a quarter of an inch.

"Miss Okonkwo. I'd have thought you'd have your hands full."

"I haven't, Professor." It was true, in a way that surprised her. Since Sunday she'd had a great deal of unused energy, the sort that builds up in a person who's lost something and has nowhere to put it. "I'd like to. If that's all right."

"Very well," said McGonagall. "Twelve till two, in the Hall, and not a sound. You may take a flask. Do not, under any circumstances, fall asleep."

"I won't."

"Hm," said McGonagall, which in her case could mean almost anything.

Zara sat down. Jamie leaned across.

"You're not doing it alone," she said.

"I'm fine."

"You're fine at everything," said Jamie. "It's a symptom."

---

The Great Hall at midnight was another place entirely.

She had never seen it so quiet. The ceiling was a deep, clear black that you could have fallen into, full of cold, hard stars. The four long tables had been pushed back against the walls. The candles that normally floated over them had been put out, or nearly: a few still drifted near the high table, one to a dozen yards, flickering faintly, like the last lights in a village. And the twelve trees stood in the dark like a row of tall, silent guests, hung with the few baubles they had left, which glimmered and ticked in the gloom.

Zara had found a hiding place. It was the space under the high table, behind the long white cloth that hung down to the floor, where you could sit with your knees up, with a clear view out under the hem, along the nearest wall. She had brought a flask and a cushion and a warm scarf. She had brought her lantern, which she had immediately put at the very bottom of her bag, as a precaution. It wasn't the sort of thing you wanted going off at a stakeout.

It was half past twelve before she heard it.

It wasn't the sound she'd been listening for. It was behind her. It was a very quiet, very careful, extremely deliberate shuffle, and a small, indrawn breath, as of someone who has just caught her robe on a table leg.

"Don't turn round," said a voice. "It's only me."

"Amara?"

"Shh."

"What are you _doing_ here?"

"Same as you." A small round face slid in beside her under the tablecloth, with its two puffs of hair, in a dressing gown printed with a pattern of hedgehogs. "Hagrid told me about the trees. He said they were pixies. And I said they weren't. And he said, how do you know? And I said, because pixies don't take things in a pattern."

"A pattern?"

"They've all been from the bottom. Never the top. Pixies fly." She tucked her feet in. "They're being taken by something that can't reach."

Zara stared at her.

"How did you get out?"

"Hufflepuff's right next to the kitchens. It's the easiest dormitory to get out of. Everybody knows that." She produced a paper bag from her sleeve. "I brought biscuits. Tibby gave me them. A stakeout without snacks isn't a stakeout, it's a stand-around."

"You can't be here. You'll get detention. I'll get detention. McGonagall said not a sound—"

"I'm being _very_ quiet."

"You've been talking for a minute."

"That's only whispering." She unrolled the bag. "Do you want a biscuit or not?"

And there was a moment, a very small one, in the dark under the high table, with the stars overhead and the trees ticking softly and her little sister settling into the cushion like a bird settling into a nest, when Zara had a perfectly clear and complete plan for what to do. It was a very good plan. It involved taking Amara by the hand and walking her, at once, directly back to the kitchens, and tucking her up in bed.

She opened her mouth to say so.

She thought of a shed, and a Knarl in a paper hat, and a girl with her chin up saying _I'm not your project_.

She shut her mouth again, and took a biscuit.

---

It was ten to one when the paw-prints came.

They didn't see the thing that made them. They only saw the marks. A little way along the nearest wall, on the flagstones at the foot of the third tree, in the dust of a dry autumn and the fine scatter of pine needles, a line of tiny dents had appeared, like stitches. They were each about the size of a thumb. They were paired, and spaced, and wide at the toe, like a pair of tiny spades.

"There," breathed Amara.

"Where?"

"There. Look. At the bottom of the tree." She was kneeling up, with a hand on Zara's arm, gripping it hard. "It's coming from behind. It's—oh. It's so small."

It was.

Something was moving at the foot of the tree, in the shadow, so dark that against the dark it was almost invisible: a low, round, glossy creature with a long, sensitive snout, and a very round belly, with two long, black, delicate paws, which it was using to reach, with enormous care, for the lowest of the baubles. There was a pouch on its stomach. It was open. It was already very full.

"It's a Niffler," whispered Amara. "Oh, it's a _Niffler_."

"It's Biscuit," said Zara.

She hadn't meant to say it so loud. The Niffler froze, one paw outstretched, with a small gold bauble in its grip, and turned its head.

Its eyes were as black as pins. They met hers. There was a moment of complete silence.

Then Biscuit tucked the bauble into his pouch, and ran.

"Get him!" said Zara, and she'd got halfway out from under the tablecloth, on her hands and knees, when a small hand caught the back of her collar and hauled.

"_Don't_," said Amara, in a low, urgent voice. "You'll scare him. He's already scared. If you chase him he'll bolt, and he'll never come out again. Look at where he's going."

"He's going to—"

"He's leaving a trail." She pointed. "Look."

It glittered on the flagstones, ahead of them, in a thin, faint line: a spatter of tiny gold flecks, from a bauble that had cracked in his pouch. It ran along the foot of the wall, past the fourth tree, and the fifth, and turned, at the end of the Hall, through a small arched door.

"We just follow," said Amara. "Slowly."

Zara looked at her.

"Slowly," she said.

It cost her something to say it. It was like holding her breath. But Amara had already got to her feet and brushed off her dressing gown, and was walking away down the Hall, in the dark, between the trees, with the quiet, steady, careful tread of a person who knew exactly what she was doing.

Zara followed.

---

She knew the place when she saw it.

It was a long way down. They went through the arched door and down a flight of steep stone steps and along a low passage lined with old barrels, and through a gap behind a tapestry of a sleeping dragon, and down a second stair, that was mostly a ladder. She'd been here once before, the year before, with three friends and a lantern and a half-formed plan. It had smelt then, as it did now, of damp stone and old metal and the particular, sharp, bright smell of treasure.

The room at the bottom was low and round and perhaps twenty feet across, and its whole floor was gold.

It wasn't gold, of course. It was buttons. It was coins and spoons and old bottle-tops and thimbles; it was prefects' badges, and watch-chains, and bent tin whistles; it was a thousand small, shining, unconsidered things, sorted with infinite care into spirals and arcs, with the largest pieces on the outside and the smallest in the middle, like the pattern of a shell. It glittered. It glowed. The light from Amara's wand fell across it, and a thousand points of brightness leapt back.

And in the middle, on a heap of silver spoons, with forty glass baubles arranged around him in a perfect ring, sat Biscuit.

He looked at them. He did not look in the least ashamed.

"Oh," said Amara, very softly. "Oh, he's _beautiful_."

---

It took Zara about forty seconds to ruin it.

"Right," she said, striding forward, with her hands on her hips. "Biscuit. That's enough. Those aren't yours. They belong to the school. They've got to go back, all of them, now, and if you give them to me, I promise you'll get a, a fair—"

Biscuit hissed.

It was a small, wet, surprisingly savage hiss, like a kettle with a grudge. He gathered the nearest bauble to his chest with both paws and glared at her out of a pair of eyes the size of peppercorns.

"I'm being reasonable," said Zara.

Biscuit hissed again.

"I'm captain of the Gryffindor Quidditch team," she said, with dignity, "and I'm telling you—"

"Zara."

"What?"

"Sit down."

"I'm in the middle of—"

"_Sit down_," said Amara, in precisely the voice their mother used when she'd had enough of someone's nonsense. Zara sat. She had not decided to. It was the voice.

Amara was already on the floor, cross-legged, a good ten feet from the heap, with her hands in her lap. She had her head very slightly bowed, so that she wasn't looking directly at the Niffler, and she wasn't moving. After a minute, without hurrying, she put one hand in her pocket, and drew something out, and set it down on the floor in front of her. It was a button. It was a large, round, gold-coloured button with an anchor on it. It lay in the lamplight, shining.

"It's from my old school blazer," she said, very quietly, to nobody. "From before. I kept it."

And then she did nothing at all.

Zara had never seen anyone do nothing so hard.

It went on for a very long time. She counted, at first. She got to three hundred and lost her place. Biscuit's nose twitched. It quivered, from the tip to the whiskers, and one foot came forward an inch, and went back. She could feel every muscle in her own legs singing with the wish to get up and do something, anything, to push, and carry, and fix. She sat on her hands. She thought of nothing. She looked at the ceiling, at the stones, at a spoon, at the curve of Amara's cheek, and slowly, very slowly, it came to her that the thing she was doing, which was waiting, wasn't the opposite of helping. It was, perhaps, the whole of it.

At some point, quite without warning, Biscuit came across the floor.

He did it all in one rush, low and fast, like a spilled drink. He snatched the button. He tucked it into his pouch. And then, with a quick, shy, appraising look at Amara, he reached behind him, and took one of the baubles from the ring, and rolled it, gently, across the floor to her feet.

She picked it up.

She said, "Thank you," as if he'd done her an enormous favour.

"Oh my goodness," said Zara.

"Shh."

"It _worked_."

"It's only the first one," said Amara, without moving her lips. "There's thirty-nine. Get the bucket."

---

It took a little under an hour.

She had never done anything so slowly, or so well. Every trade had to be done separately. A button for a bauble, a thimble for a bauble, a very good bent spoon. Amara had come with her pockets full, a pocketful of odds and ends, and she doled them out one at a time, with no hurry at all. Zara held the bucket. She was not a person who held buckets. But somewhere around the twentieth bauble, kneeling on the cold floor with the glass chiming quietly in the pail, it occurred to her that she was happy, in a dim, deep, surprised way, and that she hadn't been in quite this way for several weeks.

"You're really good at this," she said.

She said it very low, so as not to frighten him. Amara looked up.

"At what?"

"This. All of it. Him." She nodded at Biscuit, who was sitting in front of her sister, now, with the air of an old friend, with his paws folded on his stomach. "I couldn't have done it. I'd have been hissed at all night."

"You were hissed at all night."

"That's what I'm saying."

Amara's face, in the glimmer of the lamp, did something complicated.

"I've been practising on Nettle," she said, after a moment. "He's much harder."

"I bet."

"He doesn't even like buttons."

"What does he like?"

"Nothing. He likes nothing. It's very difficult." She was trying not to smile, and failing. "You're not so bad yourself. At waiting. For somebody who's never done it."

"I've done loads," said Zara.

"Name one."

"I waited for you to stop hating me."

It had come out before she'd decided. It hung in the glittering dark for a moment between them, quite small. Amara looked at her. She did not say anything for what felt like an extremely long time.

"I didn't hate you," she said. "I just wanted a bit of room."

"I know."

"You were _everywhere_."

"I know."

"I like it when you're somewhere," said Amara. "It's the everywhere I don't like."

"Right," said Zara. She took a breath. "Somewhere. I can do somewhere."

"Good," said Amara, and held out the last of the buttons.

---

It was Hagrid who found them, in the end, in the middle of the last trade, with a lantern the size of a bucket and a very large dog.

"There yeh are," said his voice, from the top of the ladder. "Been lookin' fer yeh two hours. Wondered what the pair o' yeh were up ter. Fang caught the scent o' biscuits an' wouldn't be—oh." The ladder creaked. A vast, bearded face appeared in the opening, and went, by degrees, from alarm to astonishment to a kind of helpless joy. "Biscuit! Yeh little rascal. Yeh never! The _trees_!"

"He's given them back," said Amara. "All of them. We traded."

"Yeh traded?"

"With buttons."

"Well, I'll be—" Hagrid came down the ladder, which took some time, and stood among the heaps of treasure with his hat in his hands, looking at the Niffler, and at the bucket, and at the two girls, and he was smiling so hard that his beard shook. "That's the best bit o' sense I've seen in a month. Pixies, I said. Pixies. It's all very well, bein' wrong, but yeh've ter be wrong in the right direction."

"Hagrid," said Amara, a little shyly. "Can he have one?"

"One what?"

"One of them. To keep. Just one." She pointed at the bucket. "It's only that he's been so good about it, and it's Christmas, and—"

"Which one?"

They looked. There was a small, bent, tin star, on a string, tucked in with the glass ones, so cheap and so dented that Zara hadn't noticed it. It was the sort of thing you'd have put on the very top of a very small tree. Biscuit was looking at it with an expression that could only be called yearning.

"That one," said Amara.

"Seems fair," said Hagrid gravely, and held it out, and Biscuit took it in both paws, with a small sound like a hiccup, and carried it away to the exact centre of his spiral, and set it down.

---

They went back up the long stairs, in the end, a little after three, the two of them, with a bucket between them.

It was heavy. The baubles chimed softly with every step. Hagrid had gone ahead, with Fang and the great lantern, murmuring about hot chocolate; and the corridor ahead of them was dark, and quiet, and narrow, with a faint glimmer of starlight at the end of it, from the high windows of the Hall.

"I'll take it," said Zara.

"It's all right."

"It's heavy."

"I know it's heavy." Amara had hold of one handle. "I'll take this side."

"We could—"

"We could," said Amara, "go at the same speed."

It was, Zara thought, an extremely sensible suggestion. She took the other handle. They set off, shoulder to shoulder, along the dark stone passage, with the pail swinging between them and the glass chiming at every step, left foot, right foot, left, right, not hurrying, not talking, in a sort of rough, uneven, perfectly good time.

After a while Amara began to hum. It was a very quiet, wandering sort of tune. It was their grandmother's song.

After another while, Zara joined in.
