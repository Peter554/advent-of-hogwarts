"Novak," said Professor McGonagall. "A word."

It was the end of Monday's Transfiguration, and the class was packing up with the clatter and shove of thirty people who had been conjuring spiders for fifty minutes and wanted to be somewhere without any. Elliot already had their bag on. They had one foot in the aisle. They stopped, turned, and put on a face that said _nothing, Professor, I'm nobody, I've been listening the whole time_.

She waited until the room was empty. It took a little over a minute. When the door had thudded behind the last of them, she came down from her desk with her hands folded and stopped at the end of the row. She was holding a sheet of paper.

"This," said McGonagall, "was on desk four on Friday afternoon, under a copy of _A Guide to Intermediate Transfiguration_. I assume it's yours. I don't know anyone else who draws with that line."

She held it up.

It was the lake. Elliot knew it at once, with a swooping horror, the way you know a face in a crowd you've been avoiding. They had done it on Thursday, in a spare half-hour, from memory, on the back of a worksheet: the flat pewter water, the ice at the margin, the black trees on the far shore, and over it all, small and grey, the castle, with every window picked out in a single stroke. In the corner a tentacle curled out of the water.

It wasn't one of their best. They were, in fact, a little ashamed of the tentacle. But it had something, and they knew it had something, which was why they had kept it out of any place a teacher might look.

"I'm sorry," said Elliot. "It must have fallen out of my—"

"Do not apologise. It is a very good drawing." McGonagall's voice was dry. "A line of that quality is not, as a rule, typical of an essay on conjuring spiders."

"No, Professor."

"I will be plain. The Lantern Walk is a week today. It is the first for some years, and the school has taken it up faster than I expected. There will be a procession." She set the drawing on the desk. "I wish for a banner. A large one, hung from the gallery over the stairs, where the whole school will pass beneath it. Professor Flitwick has designed three. They are, I regret to say, extremely tidy."

She looked at Elliot.

"I should like you to do it."

---

"No," said Elliot.

It came out before they could stop it, in the flat, polite voice they kept for emergencies, and left a small, awkward quiet in the room.

"No?"

"I don't do banners." They were looking at the desk. "It's a thing I do on my own. It's a hobby." They heard the word _hobby_ in the empty room and felt their ears go warm. "It's a very kind offer. I'd rather not."

"May I ask why?"

"A banner is _public_."

"It is."

"People would look at it."

"That, Novak, is the general idea."

She had folded her arms, and was regarding them over the top of her glasses. Elliot had spent four and a half years in her classroom and knew the look. It was the one she gave to work that wasn't finished.

"You are not obliged," she said. "I do not command people to be brave. In my experience it can't be done." She gathered up her papers. "But I know a good hand when I see one, and I know how rarely anyone your age takes such trouble to hide it."

"I'm not hiding it."

"Of course you aren't."

Elliot said nothing.

"Think about it. Mr Filch will bring the cloth to this room at half past six. If you decide it isn't for you, tell him to take it away again and I shall not mention the matter." She went to the door and paused with one hand on the frame. "The tentacle is excellent, by the way. A lot of people can't do suckers."

---

They drew it four times.

The library at four o'clock was warm and smelt of floor polish. Elliot took a table at the very back, behind the Charms stacks, and spread out a sheet of cartridge paper, a box of pencils and a rubber, and sat for a long time without drawing anything.

It wasn't that they didn't know what to do. They knew exactly. But the moment they thought _banner_, a fog came down, and the hand that was usually so quick went stiff, like a hand in a glove.

The first draft was a procession: a line of robed figures, each with a small lantern, wending out of the front door. It was neat. It was competent. It looked like a school photograph, and it went in the bin.

The second was heraldic, four animals and a ring of flames. It was dreadful. They tore it up before the third animal.

The third was the castle on its crag, with a snowy road and a great many stars. It was a very good picture of a place. It said nothing, and Elliot looked at it for ten minutes and knew that if they pinned it up, forty people would say _how pretty_ and walk past.

By the fourth it was almost five, and the light in the high windows had gone the colour of weak tea. They drew three lines, stopped, and put their head in their hands.

They had told the others on Sunday night, on the stairs, after the snowball fight, in four sentences and quite fast, with their eyes on the banister. _My mother came. She asked what to call me. She used the right word. I think it's going to be all right._ Zara had said, "Good," in a voice so thick she'd had to say it again. Finn had hugged them for a long time without a word. Mira had held their arm just above the elbow for a full minute, in a grip like being handed a coat. It had been an enormous relief. They hadn't expected to find, underneath it, a small new feeling, like a window opened in a stuffy room.

They had thought: _I could do anything_.

And now they were in a library with a blank sheet, four torn drafts and a pulse like a clock, and the thing they couldn't do was draw a lantern.

They screwed up the fourth with some savagery and flicked it over the back of the chair towards the wastepaper basket at the end of the stacks. It hit the rim, rolled off the pile, and lay on the floor.

Then they put on their bag and went out to the corridor for some air.

---

She was standing at the end of the corridor, under the window, with a crumpled ball of paper in her hands.

Elliot saw her from thirty feet away and stopped dead. It was the small Ravenclaw first-year, the one with the fringe and the green ink, who had been at the edge of everything all week with a book held to her chest. She hadn't seen them. She had her back to the window and was smoothing the paper flat against the wall, slowly, with both hands.

"That's mine," said Elliot.

It sounded harsher than they'd meant. She jumped, the paper slipped, and she caught it against her chest. For a moment they looked at each other down the corridor like two cats on a wall.

"I'm sorry," said Wren Calloway. "It fell. I thought it was rubbish. I only looked."

"It is rubbish."

"It isn't."

"It's the fourth draft of a thing I can't do, and I threw it away."

"Can I—" She stopped. Her face had gone very pink, and she looked, Elliot thought, like someone holding a door shut against a wardrobe. "Can I tell you what it looks like? To me?"

"I don't think—"

"It's the one with the lantern."

It was. Twelve lines, the start of something they'd abandoned: a lantern drawn from above, hanging in the air, with a thin line of light running down over a dark curve of water. A scribble. They'd lost interest after the glass.

"It looks," said Wren, setting each word down carefully, "like somebody holding their breath. It's the moment before. And I think it's the best thing I've seen in the whole castle." She went crimson. "Sorry. That's an awful thing to say, it isn't flattering, I don't know how to flatter, I just think it's true."

A long silence. Elliot stood in the draughty corridor with the dusk coming down the windows and had no idea what to say. Then a glow began to spread across the floor.

They glanced down.

The lantern was lit.

It was in the bag at their side, blazing through the weave, a clear amber light that fell across the flagstones and up the wall and over Wren's upturned face. It wasn't hiding. It wasn't going out. It had lit the moment she said _the best thing_, and it was lit now, in front of somebody, in plain view.

"Oh," said Wren, quite softly. "That's pretty."

"It doesn't usually do that," said Elliot, and found their voice had gone odd. "It goes out. When people look."

"Does it?"

"Always."

"I'm not looking at it very hard," said Wren. She thought. "Actually, I am."

"It hasn't gone out."

"No."

They stood in the light of it for a minute or more. Elliot had spent most of a year afraid of exactly this, the moment when somebody looked and the thing you'd made stopped being yours. What they felt now was a knot, tied very small and very tight, coming undone.

"Can I have that back?" they said, and held out a hand.

Wren gave it to them. She used both of hers.

---

The cloth arrived at half past six, as promised, while they were still at dinner being asked about a carp. It was a roll of heavy linen, nine feet long and four high, carried up to the empty Transfiguration classroom by Mr Filch, who dropped it on the floor and left without a word. McGonagall had also left a tin of paints and a note in her small upright hand: _A charm for the pigments is on the lid. Do not smell them. —M._

Elliot did not draw it again. They simply began. They opened the tin. They did smell the ultramarine, once, to see. It smelt of a wet dog that had eaten a violet. Wren, who had been told not to ask, did not ask.

The charm on the lid turned out to be generous. The pigments dried the moment they left the brush, so that a stroke of grey was already stone by the time the next one touched it, and the castle went up window by window at roughly the speed Elliot's hand could manage, which was a speed they had not used in front of another person since they were eleven.

It was the lake, seen from the castle steps, pale and dark and ringed in ice, and across it, from the foot of the steps to the far shore, a line of lanterns. They hung in the air, small and gold and evenly spaced, in a long curve that rose and dipped like a thread of beads. Each cast a little pool of light on the water beneath. They went on to the black trees at the end, and above them the sky ran from deep blue to a thin line of green, with one small exact star.

Wren held the end of the cloth. She did it without being asked, cross-legged on the floor with her chin on her knees, from eight until nearly eleven. Now and then she passed a brush or moved a pot. Once she said, "Is it all right if I'm here?" and Elliot, with a loaded brush in one hand and ultramarine on one cheek, said "Yes," and meant it, and was surprised how simple it was.

At a quarter to eleven they put in the last of it, in the lower right-hand corner where the water met the edge of the cloth. A very small mark, in black, in two quick strokes.

_E.N._

"It's small," said Wren.

"It's meant to be."

"It's _there_, though."

"Yes," said Elliot. "That's the point."

---

They hung it on Tuesday evening, at a quarter to seven.

It took Filch, two seventh-years, a ladder and a short sharp quarrel with the gallery rail, and it was done in the last of the dinner hour, with half the school gone in to eat and half drifting about the torch-lit Entrance Hall in twos and threes. Elliot stood at the foot of the stairs with their hands in their pockets, and had refused to be anywhere nearer. McGonagall, at the head of the stair in her hat and green cloak, said nothing, but looked at them once, down the whole length of the Hall, and gave a very small nod.

"Ready?" called a voice from the gallery.

"Go on," said McGonagall.

The cord was pulled. The banner, which had been rolled up with its hem tucked under, dropped.

It fell all at once, in a long silent sweep like a curtain going up on a stage, and hung there swaying, unfurled from gallery to floor. The torches lit it from beneath. The lake shone. The lanterns went away across it in their gold curve, on and on to the black far shore, and the stone hall seemed, for a moment, to take a breath.

People stopped.

Elliot watched it spread outward from the foot of the stair, a pause and a lifting of heads, and then another. A group of third years in the doorway fell silent in the middle of a sentence. A girl with a violin case stood on the bottom step with her mouth open. Somebody said, "Oh," and somebody else said, "Look at the _light_," and a murmur began and grew.

Nobody noticed Elliot. They were only another dark figure in the shadows, with a scarf up to their chin.

A step sounded at their shoulder, brisk and light, smelling faintly of ginger newts.

"Six pennants," said Professor McGonagall, without turning her head. She was looking up at the banner with her hands folded. "For the lake path. Poles at intervals, ready for Monday's procession. The same hand, if you've the time. Mr Filch has the cloth. I should not like to see the banner lonely."

"Six," said Elliot.

"Or four. Four would do."

"Six," said Elliot, and was surprised to find they meant it.

"I thought so," said McGonagall, and went up the stair with her cloak sweeping behind her, and did not look back.

And in the bag at Elliot's hip, where nobody could see, the lantern glowed amber, and stayed that way.
