"Novak," said Professor McGonagall. "A word."

It was the end of Transfiguration, and the class was packing up with the clatter and shove of thirty people who'd been conjuring spiders for fifty minutes and wanted to be somewhere that wasn't a room full of spiders. Elliot had already put on their bag. They had one foot in the aisle. They stopped, and turned, and put on the sort of face that they hoped said _nothing, Professor, I'm nobody, I've been listening the whole time_.

She waited until the room was empty. It took a little over a minute. When the last of them had gone, the door had thudded, and the long afternoon light had settled on the floor of the empty classroom like dust, she came down from her desk, with her hands folded in front of her, and stopped at the end of the row.

She had something in her fingers. It was a sheet of paper.

"This," said McGonagall, "was on desk four on Friday afternoon, under a copy of _A Guide to Intermediate Transfiguration_. I assume it's yours. I don't know anyone else who draws with that line."

She held it up.

It was a drawing of the lake. Elliot knew it at once, with a sort of swooping horror, the way you know a face in a crowd that you've been avoiding. It had been done in a spare half-hour, on the Thursday, from memory, on the back of a worksheet: the long flat pewter water, with the ice at the margin, and the black trees on the far shore, and, over it all, small and grey and faintly lit, the castle, in the distance, with every one of its windows picked out with a single quick stroke. In the corner, rising out of the water with a curl, was a single tentacle.

It wasn't one of their best. They had, in fact, been rather ashamed of the tentacle. But it had something, and they knew it had something, and it was a thing they'd been very careful to keep out of any place where a teacher might look.

"I'm sorry," said Elliot. "It was in my—it must have fallen out. I'll—"

"Do not apologise. It is a very good drawing." McGonagall's voice was dry. "I am bound to say, however, that a line of that quality is not, as a rule, typical of an essay on conjuring spiders."

"No, Professor."

"I will be plain. The Lantern Walk is on Monday. It is, I have been given to understand, the first for some years, and the school has been rather quick to take it up, which I confess I did not expect. There will be a procession." She set the drawing carefully on the desk. "I wish for a banner. A large one. It will hang in the Entrance Hall, from the gallery, over the stairs, where all of the school will pass beneath it. Professor Flitwick has designed three. They are all of them, I regret to say, extremely tidy."

She looked at Elliot.

"I should like you to do it."

---

"No," said Elliot.

They had not meant to say it so quickly. It came out before they could do anything about it, in the flat, dry, polite voice that they kept for emergencies, and it left a small, awkward quiet in the room.

"No?"

"I don't do banners." They were looking at the desk. "I don't do—anything, really. Not for other people. It's a thing I do on my own. It's just a hobby." They heard the word _hobby_, and the way it sounded in the empty room, and felt their ears go warm. "I'm sorry, Professor. It's a very kind offer. I'd rather not."

"May I ask why?"

"A banner is _public_."

"It is."

"People would look at it."

"That, Novak," said McGonagall, "is the general idea."

There was a pause. It was not an unfriendly one. She had folded her arms, and she was regarding them over the top of her glasses with an expression of the most exact and unhurried attention, which Elliot, who had spent four and a half years in her classroom, knew to be the look she gave to a piece of work that was not quite finished.

"You are not obliged," she said. "I should like that understood. I do not command people to be brave. It is not, in my experience, a thing that can be commanded." She gathered up her papers. "But I should be failing in my duty if I did not tell you that I have taught a great many students in this room. I know a good hand when I see one. And I know how very rare it is that anyone, at your age, with such a gift, takes the trouble to hide it."

"I'm not hiding it."

"Of course you aren't."

Elliot said nothing.

"Think about it," said Professor McGonagall. "I will need an answer by tomorrow. The cloth will be here. Mr Filch will bring it. If you decide that it is not for you, you may tell me, and I shall not mention the matter again." She walked to the door, and paused on the threshold, with one hand on the frame. "The tentacle is excellent, by the way. Don't be ashamed of it. A lot of people can't do suckers."

---

They drew it four times.

The library, at four in the afternoon, was warm, and quiet, and smelt of old paper and floor polish. Elliot had taken a table at the very back, behind the Charms stacks, where there was nobody. They had spread out a large sheet of cartridge paper, and a box of pencils, and a rubber, and sat, for a long time, without drawing anything at all.

It was not that they didn't know what to do. That was the odd part. They knew exactly what to do. It was just that the moment they thought, _banner_, a sort of fog came down, and their hand, which was usually so quick, went stiff and strange, like a hand in a glove.

The first draft was a procession. It was a long line of figures in robes, each holding a small lantern, wending their way out of the castle's front door. It was neat. It was competent. It had a great many people in it, all in rather formal positions, and they looked, Elliot thought, with a sinking in their stomach, like a school photograph. It went in the bin.

The second was a heraldic thing, with four animals and a ring of flames. It was dreadful. They tore it up before they had finished the third animal.

The third was a landscape, a good one, the castle on its crag with a long, curving, snowy road, and the trees, and a great many stars. It was beautiful. It was a very good picture of a place. It said nothing at all, and Elliot sat and looked at it for ten minutes and knew with total clarity that if they pinned it to the Entrance Hall, forty people would say _how pretty_ and walk past.

By the fourth, it was almost five, and the light in the high library windows had turned the colour of weak tea. They drew three lines. They stopped. They put down their pencil, and put their head in their hands, and sat there, very still, breathing in the smell of the table.

They had told the others on Sunday night. It had been on the stairs, after the snowball fight, in four sentences, quite fast, with their eyes on the banister. _My mother came. She asked what to call me. She used the right word. I think it's going to be all right._ Zara had said, "Good," in a voice so thick that she'd had to say it again. Finn had hugged them, for a long time, without a word. Mira had said nothing at all. She'd held their arm, just above the elbow, for a whole minute, in a grip that felt, curiously, like being given a coat. It had been the most astonishing relief, and they had not expected to find, underneath it, a small, new, trembling thing, like a window that had been opened in a room where it was suddenly possible to breathe.

They'd thought: _I could do anything_.

And now they were sitting in a library, with a blank sheet and four torn drafts and a pulse like a clock, and the thing they couldn't do was draw a picture of a lantern.

They screwed up the fourth. They did it with a certain savagery. They threw it, with a flick of the wrist, over the back of the chair and into the wastepaper basket at the end of the stacks, where it rolled, lopsided, off the pile, and lay on the floor.

Then they put on their bag, and went out into the corridor to find some air.

---

She was standing at the end of the corridor, under the window, with a crumpled ball of paper in her hand.

Elliot saw her from about thirty feet away and stopped dead. It was the small first-year, the Ravenclaw: the one with the fringe and the green ink, who had been, as far as they could tell, forever at the edge of everything for the last week, with a book held to her chest. She hadn't seen them. She was standing quite still with her back to the window, and in the dim, wintry light, she was slowly, cautiously, with both hands, smoothing out a piece of paper against the wall.

"That's mine," said Elliot.

It sounded harsher than they'd meant. She jumped, and the paper slipped, and she caught it against her chest, and for a moment the two of them looked at each other down the length of the corridor like two cats on a wall.

"I'm sorry," said Wren Calloway. "It fell. I thought it was a, I thought it was rubbish. I only looked."

"It is rubbish."

"It isn't."

"It's the fourth draft of a thing I can't do, and I threw it away."

"Can I—" She stopped. She clutched the paper. Her face had gone very pink, and her eyes were enormous, and she had, Elliot realised, the look of a person who is standing in front of something that is entirely too large for her, and will not move. "Can I tell you what it is? I mean, what it looks like? To me?"

"I don't think—"

"It's the one with the lantern."

It was. It was only the first twelve lines, the beginning of a thing they'd abandoned: a single lantern, drawn from above, hanging in the air, with a thin line of light going out from it, down, over a dark curve of water. It was hardly anything. A scribble. They'd lost interest after the glass.

"It looks," said Wren, slowly, as though each word had to be set down carefully, "like somebody's holding their breath. That's what it is. It's the moment before. And I think that's the best thing I've seen in the whole of the castle." She went crimson. "Sorry. That's an awful thing to say. I didn't mean to be, it isn't flattering, I don't know how to flatter, I just—I think it's true."

There was a very long silence.

Elliot didn't know what to say. They stood in the draughty corridor with the winter dusk coming down in the long windows, and a cold sensation in their chest, and looked at the small pink, defiant, trembling face, and a shift, a faint, golden, steady glow, began to spread across the floor.

They glanced down.

The lantern was lit.

It was in the bag at their side, and it was blazing, through the weave, with a clear, bright, amber light that fell across the flagstones and up the wall and over the pale, awed, upturned face of the girl who was looking at the paper, and at them. It was not hiding. It was not going out. It had lit the moment she said _the best thing_, and it was lit now, in front of somebody, in plain view, as steady as a candle in a window.

Wren looked at it.

"Oh," she said, quite softly. "That's pretty."

"It doesn't usually do that," said Elliot, and found that their voice had gone strange. "It goes out. When people look."

"Does it?"

"Always."

"I'm not looking at it very hard," said Wren, gravely. She thought. "Actually, I am."

"It hasn't gone out."

"No."

They stood together in the corridor, in the light of it, for a minute or more. And Elliot, who had been afraid for most of a year of exactly this, of the moment when somebody looked and the thing you had made was no longer yours, felt something loosen in them, something that had been tied in a very small, tight knot for a very long time.

"Can I have that back?" they said, and held out their hand.

Wren gave it to them. She did it with both of hers.

---

They did it in a day and a night.

The cloth arrived at half past six, as promised, while they were still at dinner being asked about a carp: a great pale roll of heavy linen, nine feet long and four feet high, carried up to the empty Transfiguration classroom by Mr Filch, who put it down on the floor and went away without a word. McGonagall had also left a tin of paints, and a note, in her small, upright, square hand, that said _A charm for the pigments is on the lid. Do not smell them. —M._

Elliot did not draw it again. They simply began.

It took the best part of four hours. It was the lake: the long curve of it, seen from the castle steps, pale and dark and ringed in ice, and across it, from the foot of the steps to the far shore, a line of lanterns. They hung in the air, small and gold and perfectly spaced, swinging in a long, slow, sweeping curve that rose and dipped over the water like a thread of beads. Each one cast a little pool of light on the surface beneath it. They went on and on, to the black trees at the end, and above them was a sky that went from deep blue to a thin line of green, and a single, exact, tiny star.

Wren held the end of the cloth. She did it without being asked, and without speaking, sitting cross-legged on the floor with her chin on her knees, from eight until a little after ten. Every so often she gave a small nod, or passed a brush, or moved a pot of paint, and once, at about eight, she said, "Is it all right if I'm here?" And Elliot, with a loaded brush in one hand and a smear of ultramarine on their cheek, had said, "Yes," and meant it, and had been surprised by how simple it was.

At a quarter to eleven, they put in the last of it, in the lower right-hand corner, where the water met the margin of the cloth. It was a very small mark. They did it in black, in two quick strokes, and then, after a moment, stood back.

_E.N._

"It's small," said Wren.

"It's meant to be."

"It's _there_, though."

"Yes," said Elliot. "That's the point."

---

They hung it on Tuesday evening, at a quarter to seven.

It took Filch, two seventh-years, a ladder and a short, sharp quarrel with the gallery rail, and it was done in the last of the dinner-hour, with half the school gone in to eat and half still drifting in the great, high, torch-lit Entrance Hall in twos and threes. Elliot stood at the foot of the stairs with their hands in their pockets. They'd refused to be anywhere nearer. McGonagall, at the head of the stair, in her hat and her green cloak, had said nothing at all, but she had looked at them once, down the whole length of the Hall, and had given a small, very slight nod.

"Ready?" called a voice from the gallery.

"Go on," said McGonagall.

The cord was pulled. And the banner, which had been rolled up like a scroll, with its hem tucked under, dropped.

It fell all at once, in a long, silent, heavy sweep, like a curtain lifted off a stage, and hung, swaying a little, with the whole of its great pale length unfurled from the gallery to the floor. The torches lit it from beneath. The lake shone, and the lanterns hung across it in their long gold curve, going away and away to the far black shore, and the whole of the vast stone hall seemed, for a moment, to take a breath.

People stopped.

Elliot saw it happen. It spread outward like a ripple, from the foot of the stair, a pause, and a lifting of heads, and then another. A group of third years in the doorway to the Hall fell silent in the middle of a sentence. A girl with a violin case stood quite still on the bottom step, with her mouth open. Somebody said, "Oh," and somebody else said, "Look at the _light_," and there was a small murmur, which grew, and grew.

They stood among them. Nobody had noticed them. They were only another dark figure in the shadows, with a scarf up to the chin.

A step sounded at their shoulder. It was brisk, and light, and smelt faintly of ginger newts.

"Six pennants," said Professor McGonagall, without turning her head. She was looking up at the banner, with her hands folded, and her profile, in the torchlight, was perfectly composed. "For the lake path. Poles at intervals, to Monday's procession. The same hand, if you've the time. Mr Filch has the cloth. I should not like to see the banner lonely."

"Six," said Elliot.

"Or four. Four would do."

"Six," said Elliot, and was surprised to find that they meant it.

"I thought so," said McGonagall, and went up the stair, with her cloak sweeping behind her, and did not look back.

And in the bag at Elliot's hip, where nobody could see, the lantern glowed gold, and stayed that way.
