The parcel from Mum arrived at breakfast, and inside it, wrapped in tissue and tied with a bit of bright green ribbon, were two pairs of pyjamas.

They were identical. That was the first thing. They were made of heavy, glossy, brilliantly patterned cotton, in a pattern of interlocking orange and gold and deep green, with a great number of small round suns in it, and big, bold, exuberant leaves, and each pair had been sewn, down to the last button, by hand. There was a note. It was folded in four and written in Mum's quick, slanting, exasperated script.

_Aunty Ngozi did these over the weekend. She says they are for the two of you to wear at the same time, in a photograph, and she will know if you don't. Do not let Amara wear hers to Hagrid's. Do not let her wear hers in the lake. Love, Mum. P.S. Your father says the bobble hat was a mistake._

"What's the bobble hat?" said Jamie.

"I don't know. He's been knitting. We don't talk about it."

Zara held up one of the tops. It was enormous, and bright, and smelt faintly of cedar and starch, and for a moment, with a sharp, sweet, unexpected pang, of the back bedroom in Lagos at five in the afternoon, with the shutters half closed against the heat. She folded it. She unfolded it again. She said, rather gruffly, "It's very orange."

"It's extremely orange."

"It's going to be in a photograph."

"I'd think so," said Jamie. She'd got her own pyjamas on already, a dark blue pair with small white stars, over her robes. She looked, in fact, rather sweet. Zara had never told her this, and was not going to. "You're refereeing in that?"

"I'm refereeing in _something_."

"It's Pyjama Quidditch," said Jamie. "It's the whole point."

---

There was a lot to be said for Pyjama Quidditch, and Zara, who had waited a fortnight to say it, set out the facts to herself on the way to the pitch.

It was the oldest game at Hogwarts, if you didn't count the first one. It was played, by tradition, on the first Saturday after the last lesson of the autumn term, between two teams of no fixed size or age or House, chosen not by loyalty but by the pattern on one's nightclothes: spots against stripes, in a straight fight, to the death, or at least until tea. There were no Bludgers. There was one Quaffle, a good deal softer than usual, and four hoops, and a hundred and ten brooms, of whom forty were genuinely safe. The Snitch was made of marzipan.

Zara had never played in it. She'd always been busy: with practice, with the house cup, with the tactics for the next one. She'd never thought of it as real. It was a thing for the little ones, and the sleepy ones, and the sort of people who didn't care very much who won.

And now she was standing at the edge of the pitch, in an orange-and-green pair of pyjamas six sizes too bright for the weather, with a whistle round her neck and a clipboard that somebody had thrust into her hands, looking up at a crowd of nearly two hundred people in nightclothes, all of whom were clutching mugs.

"Miss Okonkwo," said Madam Hooch.

She was at Zara's elbow. She was a lean, tough, upright woman with yellow eyes and iron-grey hair, who had been refereeing Hogwarts games since before Zara's mother was born, and she was wearing a bathrobe over her whistle, with a pair of enormous fluffy slippers, and she looked, if anything, more formidable than usual.

"I'll take the east hoops," said Hooch. "You take the west. You'll call the fouls on your side, and I'll call mine. Any argument, I decide. Is that understood?"

"Yes, Madam Hooch."

"Do not _help_."

"I won't."

"You will want to." She was looking at Zara with the faintest narrowing of the eyes. "I've watched you for five years, Okonkwo. You see a pitch like other people see a chess board. You are going to want to move the pieces. You are going to find that you can't." She turned away. "You'll live."

It was, Zara thought, the most terrifying thing anyone had said to her all term.

---

By ten past two she had understood what Hooch meant.

It was a mess. It was a glorious, exuberant, unorganised mess. The Spots were in blue and gold. The Stripes were in red and white. They were thrown together at random, by a hat, and a good half of them, to judge by the way they were holding their brooms, had never flown before. Finn was in goal for the Stripes, in a pair of flannelette ones with ducks, and had, within the first minute, got into a long and apparently satisfying conversation with a small robin that had landed on the hoop. Oliver was a Chaser for the Spots. He'd got his hair in his eyes. He had shot once, and missed by about forty yards, and was apparently quite happy. Mira, in a pair of tartan ones, which had _runes_ on the cuffs, was hovering at a steady and careful four feet above the ground, clinging to the broom with both hands and an expression of cold scientific interest, as if she were a Muggle in a lift.

"I'm fine," she called, as Zara flew past. "I'm doing very well. I'd like it noted that I haven't dropped it."

"It's four feet!"

"It's four feet _very steadily_."

Elliot had declined to play. They were sitting on the bottom row of the stands with a thermos of something and a sketchbook, in a long, narrow, charcoal-grey pair of pyjamas with a single thin yellow stripe at the hem, looking, as ever, like a person at a funeral who has been told it is a party. They glanced up when Zara went by. They raised the thermos, once, in an ironic salute, and went back to drawing.

It was chaos. And Zara, in the middle of it, at the west goalmouth, with the whistle between her teeth and her heart doing something quick and light and unaccustomed, discovered that she was _enjoying_ it.

It took her a full ten minutes to work out why. She was watching a game she could not help. She had no team to move, no formation to call, no plan to keep in her head. She could only look. And what she saw, from there, with the cold air on her face and nothing at all to do with her hands, was what she had never been able to see from inside: the whole of it, spread out beneath her like a map. The long diagonal run of a small boy in spots, who didn't know that he was dodging a Chaser. The way the light came down in slanting gold bars across the grass. The sheer, shining, ridiculous _gladness_ on a hundred and ten faces.

She blew the whistle. She called a foul. She was, she realised, grinning like a fool.

---

It was a little after three when she saw Amara.

She'd been looking for her. She had told herself that she wasn't. But a part of her had been following a small figure in a bright orange-and-green pair of pyjamas since the game began, across the pitch, up and down, in and out of the long wheeling crowd, in just the way that, six weeks ago, she'd have kept her eye on a loose Bludger.

She was on the Spots. She had a school broom, an old, bald Shooting Star with a twig sticking out of the end, which she had named, apparently, Dennis. She was riding it with a total and joyous disregard for the laws of physics. She had no idea how to steer. She had a vague conviction that the broom was a kind of horse, and should be talked to, and she was leaning forward, with her hair in its two puffs flying out behind her, bellowing directions at it in a loud, bright, ringing voice: "LEFT, Dennis! LEFT! I said _left_, you absolute—" and then, when it went right, laughing so hard that she nearly came off.

She flew fast. She flew with no sense of fear at all. She went through the middle of a knot of Stripes at about thirty miles an hour, with her eyes closed, shouting, and by some great miracle came out the other side with the Quaffle under her arm.

"Oh, you _beauty_," said Zara.

She stopped. She had said it aloud. She looked round, and found that Madam Hooch, across the pitch, was looking steadily at her.

She took the whistle out of her mouth. She looked at her sister. And the old hot familiar tug, the tug that had been there at every meal and at every corridor since September, the one that said _slow down_ and _be careful_ and _have you got your gloves_, rose in her chest as strongly as it had ever done.

She put her hands in her pockets.

It was the hardest thing she had done all term.

---

It happened at twenty to four.

The light was going. The great gold sun had dropped behind the hills, and the whole of the sky over the pitch had turned the deep, clear, tender colour of a winter afternoon, a pale, smoky green fading to rose, and the first of the floating lights, a thousand tiny enchanted lamps, hung by Madam Hooch from the stands that morning, had begun to come on, one by one, in the dusk. The match was a draw. It had been a draw for forty minutes. The Seekers had been going round in the upper air in a long lazy circle, like two cats looking at the same bird.

The marzipan Snitch was on the east goalpost.

Zara saw it before anyone else. She was on the west side, and she'd have missed it, except that she happened to look up: a small round golden shape, with two tiny wings, perched on the very top of the left-hand hoop like a bird in a tree. It was not moving. It had evidently decided, in the way of its kind, that it was quite comfortable.

And Amara, who had not seen it, who was at that moment travelling at about forty miles an hour at the east goalmouth with her eyes shut, her arms out, and a joyous, wavering, ear-splitting shriek, was heading directly for it.

"_Amara_—"

It was a very short flight. Zara, at the other end of the pitch, had time to put the whistle to her lips and to take it away again. She saw the broom lurch. She saw it veer, with sudden cunning, to the right, and then, with the instinct of a horse that has had enough, straight to the left. She saw her sister, in orange and green, with her arms flung wide, strike the goalpost at the top, at speed, with an audible and resounding _boing_.

She saw her go over the top.

For one, long, clear, frozen second she hung there. She was upside-down. Her puffs of hair were stuck straight out, and her mouth was an O, and her bald old Shooting Star had spun off, whistling, in the direction of the stands. A very small round golden shape, a good deal flattened, was clamped between her teeth.

Then she fell.

It was only about eight feet. Zara knew that. She knew it, quite calmly, and with absolute certainty, in the part of her mind that dealt with distances. She also knew that it was a good deal softer, underneath, than it looked, and that Madam Hooch, who had been watching the whole thing without moving, had a cushioning charm on her wand and had not, apparently, felt called upon to use it.

She landed in the grass with a thump.

Everything went still.

It lasted perhaps two seconds. A hundred and ten brooms stopped. Two hundred people in pyjamas drew breath. Zara found that her hands had come out of her pockets, and that she was already two steps off the ground, and was being held back, quite gently, by a hand on the back of her collar, and a voice, rather dry, said in her ear, "Miss Okonkwo."

"Madam Hooch—"

"Look."

She looked.

Amara was sitting up. She had grass in her hair. She had mud on her face, and a graze on her chin, and her pyjama top had come halfway off one shoulder. She was holding, in her right hand, held up in the air like a flag, a small, flat, slightly chewed, perfectly recognisable piece of golden marzipan.

She was laughing.

She had not, Zara saw, stopped. She was laughing in great, helpless, delighted gasps, with her head thrown back, in the flat blue light, and tears running down her face, and she lifted the Snitch higher, and said, in a ringing, wondering, unmistakably triumphant voice:

"_I did something!_"

The stands went up like a rocket.

---

It was a good hour before Zara got to Jamie.

There was a great deal to do. There was a ceremony, in which the marzipan was shared out in thirds, with great solemnity, by the winner. There was a speech, from the Headmistress, who had arrived at the touchline in a pair of tartan slippers and had a thermos of what smelt very much like whisky. There was hot chocolate, and a bonfire, and a good many people who wanted to shake Amara's hand. It was nearly five. The lights were all on now, a great canopy of tiny golden stars strung across the dusk above the pitch, and the stands had emptied, and the air smelled of woodsmoke and cold grass.

She found her by the broom shed.

Jamie was sitting on an upturned bucket, in her starry pyjamas, with her gloves in her lap and a mug of cocoa between her hands. She had a streak of mud down one cheek. She looked up as Zara came round the corner, and her face, which was usually a very good one for giving nothing away, gave away, for just a moment, a faint and wary hope.

"I wasn't sure you'd come," she said.

"Why not?"

"You've been looking at me for three days like a person with a very large sum in her head."

"I've been—" Zara stopped. She sat down on the other bucket. She looked at her hands. "I've got something to ask. It's been a while. I kept thinking I'd got it right and then it came out wrong, and I thought, I'll say it badly, and see."

"Say it badly."

"I want you to be my vice-captain."

There was a very short silence.

"I know that's not how it's done," said Zara, in a rush. "It's meant to be a vote. I've looked it up. But I'd have to ask the others, and they'd say yes, because they'd say yes to anything, and I wanted to ask you first, because it was you who said it on the changing room floor, and it was you who—and I should have done it in September. I should have done it the day I got the badge. I've been trying to do it alone and it's rubbish. I'm rubbish at it. I need somebody who can tell me when I've got it wrong, and who'll say it in a voice I'll listen to. And I think that's you."

She stopped, out of breath.

Jamie put down her mug.

"About time," she said.

"What?"

"I've been doing it since September. It's only that it hadn't got a name." She had the faintest ghost of a smile. "Yes. Obviously. I thought you'd never ask."

"You could have _said_."

"You weren't ready."

"I'm—" Zara began, and then stopped, because Jamie was right, and she'd been about to argue with her, and she heard it. She laughed. It came out unsteady, and surprised her. "Right. Yes. Okay. Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I'll be dreadful. I'll tell you things." Jamie was getting to her feet, and picking up the mug, and putting out one muddy hand. "Welcome to the other half of the job."

They shook. It was a very firm handshake. It went on a little longer than strictly necessary, with each of them looking at the other with a kind of dazed and wondering pleasure, and then Jamie said, "You're glowing."

"I'm what?"

"Your pocket," said Jamie. "It's lit."

---

It was.

She'd forgotten. She'd stuffed it into the pocket of her pyjama top that morning, as she had done for three weeks, along with a Knarl spine and a bent whistle and a spare lace, out of simple habit; and she had been so occupied, all day, with one thing and another, that she'd quite stopped listening for it to spit. But it wasn't spitting. It wasn't coughing, or spluttering, or sending out those small bad-tempered sparks. It was lit. It was shining out through the glossy cotton, steady and clear and unmistakable, a bright, warm, level, orange-red light, like a coal that somebody has just blown on, and it threw a ring of glow on the ground at her feet, and up the side of the shed, and across Jamie's astonished face.

She took it out.

It stood in her palm, a small brass frame, with a hinged door and a hook, and in it, behind the glass, burned a single tall, perfect flame. It did not waver. It did not flicker. It burned like a thing that had known, for some time, what it was for.

"Oh," said Zara.

"What's in it?" said Jamie, and then, hurriedly, "No. Sorry. It's rude. Sprout says."

"It's okay." She was looking at the flame. She had a feeling in her chest like a very large door being opened. She had, it came to her, been carrying a team, all this time. She'd carried it, as she'd carried everything, with both hands, tight, in a sort of ferocious panic, as if it might fall. And she'd only now put it down, a little, so that it could breathe, and find that it had been holding her up, too.

"I think," said Zara, "it's us."

---

She found Amara in the stands, in the end, at about six.

They'd gone mostly dark. The enchanted lights were still burning, a great canopy of them, thousands upon thousands of small gold stars hung over the pitch against the deep blue of the evening, so that the whole place had the quiet, glimmering, unreal look of a theatre after the show. The bonfire had burned down to a heap of red. Somewhere a long way off a group of people was singing, very softly, a carol with a great many verses.

She was in the middle of the top row, wrapped in a blanket, with her feet drawn up, and a half-eaten piece of marzipan in her fist, and a small spiky nose peering out of the front of her pyjama top.

"He's not supposed to be here," said Amara, without turning her head. "Hagrid said. But he was so lonely. He doesn't like fireworks."

"There weren't any fireworks."

"He doesn't like _the idea_."

Zara sat down. She was wearing her own pyjamas, which were orange and green, and Amara's were orange and green, and in the lamplight, in the quiet, in the gold and the dark, they matched exactly, down to the last small round sun.

She'd have to take a photograph. She had promised, in a manner of speaking. Aunty Ngozi would know.

"Did you see me?" said Amara.

"I saw you."

"Was it good?"

"It was the best thing I've seen all year."

"I crashed."

"You did."

"Did you want to stop it?" She had turned her head. Her face, in the glow, was smudged and bright and very serious. "I saw. You had your hands in your pockets. I thought, she's going to run. And then you didn't."

"I wanted to," said Zara, honestly. "Very badly. I'd got as far as the thing with the whistle."

"But you didn't."

"No."

"Why not?"

It was a good question. She thought about it for some time. She thought about a lantern in her pocket, and a wall of snow, and a bucket of baubles, and a small voice in a tin hat saying _I just wanted a bit of room_.

"Because you got up," she said. "Because I'd have been in the way."

Amara considered this, with the gravity of a judge.

"That's very sensible," she said.

"I'm told I'm learning."

"You're doing quite well." She yawned, enormously. It took her by surprise. She put the marzipan down on the bench, and shuffled sideways under the blanket, and with no more preamble than a cat settling into a basket, put her head on her sister's shoulder.

"Don't tell Nettle," she mumbled, "I'm tired."

"I won't."

"He'll say I'm not as brave as him."

"He's a Knarl."

"They're very brave," said Amara, with her eyes shut. "They just don't like to say."

She was asleep in about a minute. Her breath was warm on Zara's neck. The small spiky shape in the front of her pyjamas gave a long, contented snuffle, and burrowed deeper, and the flame in Zara's pocket, which had been burning all this while, quite steady, burned on.

Zara sat still. She didn't dare to move. Above them, over the pitch, the little golden lights shone out, thousands upon thousands, against the cold dark sky, and the whole wide empty stand lay hushed beneath them like the inside of a lantern, and she thought, quite clearly, in the middle of it, with a heart that was so full she could hardly breathe: _I'm somewhere. I'm right here_.
