The parcel from Mum arrived at breakfast, and inside it, wrapped in tissue and tied with green ribbon, were two pairs of pyjamas.

They were identical. The cotton was stiff and glossy, printed in orange and gold and green with small round suns and big bold leaves, and every button had been sewn on by hand. There was a note, folded in four, in Mum's slanting, exasperated script.

_Aunty Ngozi did these over the weekend. She says they are for the two of you to wear at the same time, in a photograph, and she will know if you don't. Do not let Amara wear hers to Hagrid's. Do not let her wear hers in the lake. Love, Mum. P.S. Your father says the bobble hat was a mistake._

"What's the bobble hat?" said Jamie.

"I don't know. He's been knitting. We don't talk about it."

Zara held up one of the tops. It smelt of cedar and starch, and for a moment, with a sharp unexpected pang, of the back bedroom in Lagos at five in the afternoon, shutters half closed against the heat. She folded it. She unfolded it again. "It's very orange," she said, rather gruffly.

"It's extremely orange."

"It's going to be in a photograph."

"I'd think so." Jamie had put her own pyjamas on already, dark blue with small white stars, over her robes. She looked, in fact, rather sweet. Zara had never told her this and was not going to. "You're refereeing in that?"

"I'm refereeing in _something_."

"It's Pyjama Quidditch," said Jamie. "It's the whole point."

---

There was a lot to be said for Pyjama Quidditch, and Zara, who had waited a fortnight to say it, set out the facts to herself on the way to the pitch.

It was the oldest game at Hogwarts, if you didn't count the first one. It was played, by tradition, on the first Saturday after the last lesson of term, between two teams of no fixed size or age or House, chosen not by loyalty but by the pattern on your nightclothes: spots against stripes, to the death, or at least until tea. There were no Bludgers. There was one Quaffle, a good deal softer than usual, four hoops, and a hundred and ten brooms, of which forty were genuinely safe. The Snitch was made of marzipan.

Zara had never played. She'd always been busy with practice, or the House Cup, or tactics for the next match. It was a game for the little ones and the sleepy ones, and for people who didn't care very much who won.

Now she stood at the edge of the pitch in orange-and-green pyjamas six sizes too bright for the weather, with a whistle round her neck and a clipboard somebody had thrust at her, looking up at nearly two hundred people in nightclothes, all clutching mugs.

"Miss Okonkwo," said Madam Hooch.

She was at Zara's elbow, lean and upright, with yellow eyes and iron-grey hair. She had refereed Hogwarts games since before Zara's mother was born. Today she wore a bathrobe over her whistle and enormous fluffy slippers, and looked, if anything, more formidable than usual.

"I'll take the east hoops," said Hooch. "You take the west. You'll call the fouls on your side, and I'll call mine. Any argument, I decide."

"Yes, Madam Hooch."

"Do not _help_."

"I won't."

"You will want to." The yellow eyes narrowed. "I've watched you for five years, Okonkwo. You see a pitch the way other people see a chessboard. You are going to want to move the pieces. You are going to find that you can't." She turned away. "You'll live."

It was, Zara thought, the most terrifying thing anyone had said to her all term.

---

By ten past two she understood what Hooch meant.

It was a glorious, unorganised mess. The Spots wore blue and gold and the Stripes red and white, thrown together at random by a hat, and a good half of them, to judge by how they held their brooms, had never flown before. Finn was in goal for the Stripes, in flannelette pyjamas with ducks on, and had within a minute fallen into a long and apparently satisfying conversation with a robin that had landed on the hoop. Oliver was a Chaser for the Spots, hair in his eyes. He had shot once, missed by about forty yards, and was quite happy. Mira, in tartan with _runes_ on the cuffs, hovered four feet above the ground, clinging to her broom with both hands and an expression of cold scientific interest, like a Muggle in a lift.

"I'm fine," she called, as Zara flew past. "I'm doing very well. I'd like it noted that I haven't dropped it."

"It's four feet!"

"It's four feet _very steadily_."

Elliot had declined to play. They sat on the bottom row of the stands with a thermos and a sketchbook, in charcoal-grey pyjamas with a single thin yellow stripe at the hem, looking like a mourner who has been told it's a party. They raised the thermos to Zara in an ironic salute and went back to drawing.

And Zara, at the west goalmouth, whistle between her teeth, discovered that she was _enjoying_ it.

It took her ten minutes to work out why. She had no team to move, no formation to call, no plan to keep in her head. She could only look. And from out here, with cold air on her face and nothing to do with her hands, she could see what she'd never seen from inside: the whole game laid out like a map. A small boy in spots made a long diagonal run, not knowing he was dodging a Chaser. Slanting gold light fell across the grass in bars. A hundred and ten faces were shining like idiots.

She blew the whistle. She called a foul. She realised she was grinning.

---

It was a little after three when she saw Amara.

She'd been looking for her. She had told herself she wasn't, but some part of her had been following a small figure in orange and green since the game began, up and down the pitch, the way six weeks ago she'd have kept an eye on a loose Bludger.

Amara was on the Spots. She had a school broom, an old bald Shooting Star with a twig sticking out of the end, which she had named Dennis. She rode it with total and joyous disregard for the laws of physics. She had no idea how to steer. She had a vague conviction that a broom was a kind of horse and should be talked to, and she leaned forward with her hair in its two puffs flying out behind her, bellowing directions: "LEFT, Dennis! LEFT! I said _left_, you absolute—" and then, when it went right, laughing so hard she nearly came off.

She went through the middle of a knot of Stripes at thirty miles an hour, eyes closed, shouting, and by some miracle came out the other side with the Quaffle under her arm.

"Oh, you _beauty_," said Zara.

She had said it aloud. Across the pitch, Madam Hooch was looking steadily at her.

Zara took the whistle out of her mouth. The old hot tug rose in her, the one that had been there at every meal and in every corridor since September, the one that said _slow down_ and _be careful_ and _have you got your gloves_.

She put her hands in her pockets.

It was the hardest thing she had done all term.

---

It happened at twenty to four.

The light was going. The sun had dropped behind the hills, the sky over the pitch had turned pale smoky green fading to rose, and the floating lights Madam Hooch had hung from the stands that morning, a thousand tiny lamps, were coming on one by one in the dusk. The match was a draw. It had been a draw for forty minutes. The Seekers had been circling in the upper air like two cats looking at the same bird.

The marzipan Snitch was on the east goalpost.

Zara saw it before anyone else. She'd have missed it if she hadn't happened to look up: a small round golden shape with two tiny wings, perched on the top of the left-hand hoop like a bird in a tree. It was not moving. It had evidently decided that it was comfortable.

And Amara, who hadn't seen it, who was at that moment travelling at forty miles an hour towards the east goalmouth with her eyes shut, her arms out and an ear-splitting shriek on, was heading directly for it.

"_Amara_—"

It was a very short flight. Zara had time to put the whistle to her lips and take it away again. She saw the broom lurch. She saw it veer, with sudden cunning, to the right, and then, with the instinct of a horse that has had enough, hard to the left. She saw her sister, arms flung wide, strike the top of the goalpost with an audible, resounding _boing_.

She saw her go over the top.

For one long, clear second Amara hung there upside down, puffs of hair stuck straight out, mouth an O, while Dennis spun off whistling towards the stands. A small round golden shape, a good deal flattened, was clamped between her teeth.

Then she fell.

It was about eight feet. Zara knew that, quite calmly, in the part of her mind that dealt with distances. She also knew it was softer underneath than it looked, and that Madam Hooch, who had watched the whole thing without moving, had a cushioning charm ready on her wand and hadn't felt called upon to use it.

Amara landed in the grass with a thump.

Everything went still. A hundred and ten brooms stopped. Two hundred people in pyjamas drew breath. Zara found that her hands had come out of her pockets and she was two steps off the ground, held back quite gently by a hand on her collar, and a dry voice said in her ear, "Miss Okonkwo."

"Madam Hooch—"

"Look."

She looked.

Amara was sitting up with grass in her hair, mud on her face, a graze on her chin and her pyjama top halfway off one shoulder. She was holding up, like a flag, a small, flat, slightly chewed, perfectly recognisable piece of marzipan.

She was laughing. She hadn't stopped. She was laughing in helpless gasps with her head thrown back and tears running down her face, and she lifted the Snitch higher and said, in a ringing, wondering, unmistakably triumphant voice:

"_I did something!_"

The stands went up like a rocket.

---

It was a good hour before Zara got to Jamie.

There was a ceremony, in which the marzipan was shared out in thirds, with great solemnity, by the winner. There was a speech from the Headmistress, who had arrived at the touchline in tartan slippers with a thermos that smelt very much like whisky. There was hot chocolate, and a bonfire, and a queue of people who wanted to shake Amara's hand. By five the lights were all on, a canopy of golden stars strung across the dusk, the stands had emptied, and the air smelt of woodsmoke and cold grass.

She found Jamie by the broom shed, sitting on an upturned bucket in her starry pyjamas with her gloves in her lap and a mug of cocoa between her hands, a streak of mud down one cheek. She looked up as Zara came round the corner, and her face, usually very good at giving nothing away, gave away for a moment a faint, wary hope.

"I wasn't sure you'd come," she said.

"Why not?"

"You've been looking at me for three days like you had a very large sum in your head."

"I've been—" Zara stopped. She sat down on the other bucket and looked at her hands. "I've got something to ask. I kept thinking I'd got it right and then it came out wrong, so I thought, I'll say it badly, and see."

"Say it badly."

"I want you to be my vice-captain."

There was a very short silence.

"I know that's not how it's done," said Zara, in a rush. "It's meant to be a vote. I've looked it up. But I'd have to ask the others, and they'd say yes, because they'd say yes to anything, and I wanted to ask you first, because it was you who said it on the changing-room floor, and I should have done it in September. I should have done it the day I got the badge. I've been trying to do it alone and it's rubbish. I'm rubbish at it. I need somebody who'll tell me when I've got it wrong in a voice I'll listen to. And I think that's you."

She stopped, out of breath.

Jamie put down her mug.

"About time," she said.

"What?"

"I've been doing it since September. It just hadn't got a name." There was the ghost of a smile. "Yes. Obviously. I thought you'd never ask."

"You could have _said_."

"You weren't ready."

"I'm—" Zara began, and stopped, because Jamie was right, and she'd been about to argue, and she'd heard herself. She laughed. It came out unsteady. "Right. Yes. Okay. Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I'll be dreadful. I'll tell you things." Jamie got to her feet and put out one muddy hand. "Welcome to the other half of the job."

They shook. It was a very firm handshake and went on a little longer than it needed to, and then Jamie said, "You're glowing."

"I'm what?"

"Your pocket," said Jamie. "It's lit."

---

It was.

She'd forgotten it. She had stuffed it into her pyjama top that morning, as she had for three weeks, along with a Knarl spine, a bent whistle and a spare lace, and she'd been so busy all day that she'd stopped listening for it to spit. But it wasn't spitting. It wasn't coughing or sending out its small bad-tempered sparks. It was shining through the glossy cotton, steady and orange-red, like a coal somebody has just blown on, and it threw a ring of glow on the ground at her feet and up the side of the shed and across Jamie's astonished face.

She took it out.

It stood in her palm, a small brass frame with a hinged door and a hook, and behind the glass burned a single tall flame. It did not waver. It burned like something that had known for some time what it was for.

"Oh," said Zara.

"What's in it?" said Jamie, and then, hurriedly, "No. Sorry. It's rude. Sprout says."

"It's okay." Zara looked at the flame. Then she looked at the bucket Jamie had been sitting on, and at the two mugs, and at Jamie's muddy hand, which was still half out in the air between them, and she held the lantern up between their two faces, so the light fell on both.

"I think," she said, "it's us."

---

She found Amara in the stands at about six.

They had gone mostly dark. The enchanted lights still burned in their thousands over the pitch, so that the place had the glimmering, unreal look of a theatre after the show. The bonfire had burned down to a heap of red. A long way off, somebody was singing a carol with a great many verses, very softly.

Amara was in the middle of the top row, wrapped in a blanket with her feet drawn up and half a piece of marzipan in her fist. A small spiky nose peered out of the front of her pyjama top.

"He's not supposed to be here," said Amara, without turning her head. "Hagrid said. But he was so lonely. He doesn't like fireworks."

"There weren't any fireworks."

"He doesn't like _the idea_."

Zara sat down. Her pyjamas were orange and green, and so were Amara's, and in the lamplight, in the quiet, they matched exactly, down to the last small round sun.

She would have to take a photograph. She had promised, in a manner of speaking. Aunty Ngozi would know.

"Did you see me?" said Amara.

"I saw you."

"Was it good?"

"It was the best thing I've seen all year."

"I crashed."

"You did."

"Did you want to stop it?" Amara had turned her head. Her face, smudged and bright, was very serious. "I saw. You had your hands in your pockets. I thought, she's going to run. And then you didn't."

"I wanted to," said Zara, honestly. "Very badly. I'd got as far as the thing with the whistle."

"But you didn't."

"No."

"Why not?"

It was a good question. She thought about it for some time. She thought about a wall of snow, and a bucket of baubles, and a small voice in a tin hat saying _I just wanted a bit of room_.

"Because you got up," she said. "And I'd only have been in the way."

Amara considered this with the gravity of a judge.

"That's very sensible," she said.

"I'm told I'm learning."

"You're doing quite well." She yawned, hugely, and it took her by surprise. She put the marzipan down on the bench, shuffled sideways under the blanket, and with no more preamble than a cat settling into a basket, put her head on her sister's shoulder.

"Don't tell Nettle," she mumbled. "I'm tired."

"I won't."

"He'll say I'm not as brave as him."

"He's a Knarl."

"They're very brave," said Amara, with her eyes shut. "They just don't like to say."

She was asleep in about a minute, warm against Zara's neck. The spiky shape in her pyjama top gave a long snuffle and burrowed deeper. Zara didn't dare move. In her pocket the lantern burned on, and the light came through the cotton and fell across both their sleeves, orange on orange, so that for a while it was hard to say where one pair of pyjamas stopped and the other began.
