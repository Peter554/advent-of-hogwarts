It was the banner that did it.

Mira had come down the great stairs on Tuesday evening, on her way to dinner, in the middle of a loud and cheerful stream of Ravenclaws, and had stopped so abruptly on the sixth step that three people walked into her. She had not meant to. She had simply looked up, as everyone did, at the enormous pale thing hanging from the gallery, with its long gold curve of lanterns going away across the lake; and she'd stood there, in the torchlight, with her hand on the banister, and had felt her whole chest go quiet.

It was beautiful. It was, quite simply, the most beautiful thing she had ever seen in the Entrance Hall, and she'd been looking at that Hall for four and a half years. It had the lake in it, and the dusk, and the small bright star over the far shore. It had that particular way of drawing a thing that was neither exact nor loose, but caught, as if the hand had known where to stop.

She went down three steps. She read the lower right corner. And her heart did something extraordinary.

_E.N._

It was very small. It was in black, in the corner, like a signature on a letter. She had stood and stared at it for the best part of a minute, while the crowd moved around her, quite unable to think, and what she had felt had been so large, and so confused, and so entirely made up of pride and amazement and a strange, aching lurch of something else, that she hadn't been able to name it until she was halfway to the Ravenclaw table, with a plate in her hand.

It was _fear_. It was the sudden, sharp, specific fear of a person who has just looked at the world and understood that it is moving faster than she is. Elliot had made that. Elliot, who had a sketchbook they'd shown to four people in their whole life, had hung a thing the size of a barn over the main staircase of Hogwarts and signed it. And she, Mira, who had known them for five years and sat opposite them at about nine hundred meals, hadn't known a thing about it.

_It's the drift_, she thought, and went cold. _It's real. It's not just that we're busy. It's that things are happening, and we're not there_.

It was five days to the solstice.

She'd written the schedule that night.

---

It took her three drafts, and a very large sheet of the good parchment, and by the end of it she was rather pleased.

It wasn't an _excessive_ schedule. She wanted that to be understood. It had four lines. It had a title, in capitals, at the top, and a footnote, and each of the four lines had a date and a time and a purpose and a name, and it was set out in a neat, clear, colour-coded table, in three kinds of ink, so that nobody could possibly be in any doubt as to what was required of them.

_SOLSTICE PACT: PREPARATIONS (DRAFT 3)_

_Wed 16th, 7pm. Wishes: format. Venue: 3rd-floor classroom. Refreshments: M. (tea)_

_Fri 18th, 7pm. Wishes: content. Refreshments: Z. (pasties?)_

_Sun 20th, 7pm. Final run-through. Refreshments: F. (mince pies)_

_Mon 21st, dusk. THE WALK. Refreshments: E. (TBC)_

_Please reply by owl by Tuesday midnight if you have a conflict. If none, I will assume yes._

It was a good schedule. It was, she thought, rather a _kind_ one: the whole point of it was that nobody would have to think. They'd been so very busy. They'd all had so much to carry. If somebody else did the remembering, then the solstice, the pact, the long night and its wishes, the thing they had promised in the glow of a fire on a December evening a whole year ago, would simply _happen_, on time, with snacks.

She folded it in three. She sent three owls. And at midnight, when no replies had come, she told herself, firmly, that this was because they had all, quite properly, assumed yes.

---

On Wednesday, at seven, she was in the classroom.

She'd got there at twenty to. It was on the third floor, at the end of a long cold corridor, behind a door with a stiff handle, and it had not been opened since October. It smelled of dust and old cushions. The great hearth was cold. The armchair sat where they'd left it, in the middle, with a rug, and the window overlooking the lake was grey with a thin film of December cobweb, and she had stood on the threshold for a moment, with a kettle in one hand and a bag of cushions in the other, and remembered all of it, the whole of last year, in a single clear sweep. The frost. The candles. Elliot, laughing. Finn, on the floor, with a blanket round his shoulders.

She'd got to work. She'd lit the fire. She had swept the floor with a charm, and arranged the cushions in a ring, and set out four cups, and a plate of shortbread, and a small card in front of each, in her best hand, with a name on it. She'd put the kettle on the hob. She had even found, in the cupboard, a sprig of holly, and put it in a jar.

It looked lovely.

It was five past seven.

She sat down, in her own place, and folded her hands, and waited.

It was ten past. It was a quarter past. The fire cracked, and settled, and sent a long flicker across the wall. Somewhere down the corridor a clock struck the half-hour, in a deep, flat, patient voice, and a draught came in under the door and stirred the shortbread's paper. She'd taken out her notebook. She wrote, in the margin, _7.30. No sign_. She crossed it out, and wrote, _Probably Quidditch_, and underlined it.

At twenty-five to eight, she heard feet.

They were running. They came pounding up the stairs, two at a time, and along the corridor, in a loud, uneven rhythm, and stopped, with a thump, outside the door. The handle rattled. It flew open. And Zara Okonkwo, in her Quidditch robes, with her braids half undone and mud on her knees, fell into the room, and stopped dead.

"Sorry," she gasped. "Sorry, sorry. Practice ran over. Jamie made me do one more, one more, and then Tamsin lost a glove, and we couldn't—" She'd put her hand on the doorframe. She was breathing hard. She looked round the room, at the cushions, and the fire, and the little cards.

Her eyes went to the parchment on the mantelpiece.

It was laid out flat, with a paperweight on each corner. In the firelight it looked, Mira thought, very large, and very orderly, and very much like a thing that had been done on purpose.

"Is this," said Zara, with a gleam of tired and affectionate mischief, "an _O.W.L._?"

It was a joke. Mira knew it was a joke. She had known Zara for five years, and she could have drawn that grin from memory. It was the kind of joke you make about a friend's colour-coded timetable, over breakfast, with a piece of toast. It was exactly what she herself would have said.

She'd been sitting by herself for forty minutes.

"Somebody," said Mira, "has to organise it."

It came out smaller than she had meant. It was stiff, and quite cold, in the warm room. And she saw it land.

She saw Zara's face change. It did not crumple, exactly. It went blank. It was a flicker, and a small, quick, closing, like the shutter of a camera, and the grin went out of it.

"I didn't know," said Zara, slowly, "that we needed organising."

Neither of them said anything.

It was one of those silences that gather their own weight. It went on for about four seconds. In the hearth a log shifted, and sent up a shower of sparks, and the little holly jar rocked slightly on the sill. Zara was standing in the doorway with mud on her knees and her gloves still in one hand, and Mira, in her own place on the cushion, with her notebook open on her lap, had a sudden vivid sense of the whole thing sliding sideways, very gently, like a tray, and she couldn't think of a single thing to say to stop it.

"I only meant—" she began.

"No, I know. It's fine." Zara looked at the cushions. "It's a lot of cushions."

"I thought, it's been—"

"Mira, I—"

"Sorry I'm late!" cried a voice from the corridor.

---

It was Finn. He came round the door like a small, apologetic avalanche, with a sack of compost under one arm, and a lantern in the other hand burning a strange, dull, wavering gold, and he had got a great deal of mud on his face. He'd clearly come straight from the greenhouses. He stopped on the threshold, and looked round the room, at the cushions and the cards and the fire and the two of them, and Mira saw the whole of it arrive in his face: the quick, bright, open look of a person who has come into a room he thinks is warm, and found it is slightly colder than he thought.

"Oh," said Finn. "Am I—is it—"

"You're fine," said Mira.

"I'm covered in—" He looked down. "Sorry. The frostblooms. Sprout needed a hand with the last of the, and I lost track, and then it was—" He set the compost down. "Where do I sit?"

There was a small card on each cushion. Hers. Zara's. Elliot's. And his own. They were all in the same neat hand. He stood looking at the one with _Finn_ on it, in the middle of the ring, with the whole of his large, freckled, muddy, well-meaning person, and he plainly could not work out whether it was a joke.

"There's—" said Mira. "It's just a—it's only a seating plan. It doesn't matter. Sit anywhere."

"I'll sit on the floor," said Finn, with great diplomacy, and did.

Nobody said anything.

It was not unfriendly. That was the odd part, she'd think afterwards. Nobody was angry. Nobody was cross. Zara had put her gloves in her pocket, and sat down heavily on the nearest cushion, and Finn was making a small, polite, careful effort to look at the fire. But the quiet in the room had the thick, slightly baffled quality of something that has been interrupted before it began. A cup rattled. The kettle began to sing.

"Where's Elliot?" said Finn.

"I don't know," said Mira. "They didn't reply."

"They're probably—"

"I expect so."

"It's a busy time," said Zara.

"It is," said Mira. "It's very busy."

"We could—" said Finn.

"It's all right," said Mira.

She heard her own voice. It sounded perfectly pleasant. It sounded, in fact, like the voice of a person who has decided to be sensible, and she got to her feet, and began, with great calm, to collect the cards.

"It was only a draft," she said. "I thought I'd try. It's quite all right if it's not—it was a bit much, honestly, I'm sorry. We'll sort it out another time. There's no—we'll just do it on the night. I expect it'll be fine."

"Mira," said Zara.

"I'll take the tea."

"Mira, wait—"

"It's _fine_."

She put the cards in her pocket. She picked up the plate of shortbread. And she stood for a moment in the middle of the room, in the firelight, with her back very straight, looking, she hoped, like a person who had been planning to go anyway.

It was Finn who got up. He did it slowly, with a hand on the cushion. He didn't say anything. He looked at her, with a long, steady, anxious, kind look, and then, instead of saying the thing she was afraid he would say, he opened the door.

---

She walked back alone.

It was dark in the corridors, and cold. Most of the torches had burned down to a few red coals, and the portraits were asleep, and the great, shadowy, echoing castle lay round her in the silence of a Wednesday night. She went slowly. She'd put the plate of shortbread down somewhere. She could not remember where.

It was, she told herself, perfectly all right. It was only a misunderstanding. They'd been tired, and she'd been too much, and there was no harm in it. It was the sort of thing that happens between friends, and by morning it would be a joke.

She came out on the gallery above the Entrance Hall, and stopped.

It was almost empty. A single torch was burning at the foot of the stair, and in its light the banner hung, huge and pale and perfectly still, from the rail to the floor. The lake went away across it. The lanterns went away over the lake. She stood at the gallery rail with her hands on the cold stone, and looked at them for a long time.

_E.N._

She had known it was Elliot's. She'd known it for a day. And in the whole of that day, she had not once gone and said, _it's the most beautiful thing I've seen_. She'd gone and written a timetable.

It came to her slowly, in the dark. It came as a series of small, separate, rather cold realisations, like a person turning on lights, one by one, down a long corridor. She looked at the schedule in her mind: every line of it. _Wishes: format. Wishes: content. Final run-through._ It had her tea. It had Zara's pasties, with a question mark. It had Finn's mince pies. It had a time, and a place, and a purpose. It had not one single thing in it that anybody else had said.

She hadn't asked.

She hadn't asked what Zara wanted. She hadn't asked where Elliot had been, or what they'd been making, or why they hadn't said. She hadn't asked Finn what he was carrying, in the lantern that burned so bright and so uncertainly, or why he had come in with mud on his face and a look as though he had been expecting an argument. She'd been so sure that she knew what they needed that she had not, in the whole of three weeks, once, simply, said, _how are you?_

Down in the Hall, the torch hissed.

She went to her room. She took out a fresh sheet of parchment, and her best pen. She wrote _SOLSTICE_ at the top, in capitals, and crossed it out. She wrote _PACT_, and crossed that out too. She sat for a long time, with the pen in her hand and the candle burning down, looking at the blank white page, and the thing that she wanted to put on it, which was a very short thing, and a very hard one.

At the bottom, in the middle, in small, plain letters, she wrote a single word.

_Ask._
