It was the banner that did it.

Mira had come down the great stairs on Tuesday evening on her way to dinner, in the middle of a loud stream of Ravenclaws, and had stopped so suddenly on the sixth step that three people walked into her. She hadn't meant to. She had simply looked up, as everyone did, at the pale expanse hanging from the gallery with its curve of lanterns going away across the lake, and her chest had gone quiet.

It was beautiful. It was, she thought, quite possibly the most beautiful thing she had seen in the Entrance Hall, though she had been looking at that Hall for four and a half years and was prepared to be corrected. It had the lake in it, and the dusk, and one small star over the far shore. It was neither exact nor loose, but caught, as if the hand had known where to stop.

She went down three steps and read the lower right-hand corner.

_E.N._

It was very small, in black, like a signature on a letter. She stood and stared at it for the best part of a minute while the crowd flowed round her, and what she felt was so large and so muddled, pride and amazement and an odd lurch of something else, that she couldn't name it until she was halfway to the Ravenclaw table with a plate in her hand.

It was fear. Elliot had a sketchbook they'd shown to perhaps four people in their life, and had hung a thing the size of a barn over the main staircase of Hogwarts and signed it. And she, Mira, who had known them for five years and sat opposite them at roughly nine hundred meals, hadn't known a thing about it.

_It's the drift_, she thought, and went cold. _It's not just that we're busy. Things are happening and we're not there._

At the Ravenclaw table, where Priya ate on Tuesdays under an arrangement nobody had written down, Priya asked if she was all right. "Perfectly," said Mira. "I'm only planning something." Priya, who had heard this before, passed her the potatoes and let it go.

It was six days to the solstice.

She wrote the schedule that night.

---

It took three drafts and a very large sheet of the good parchment, and by the end she was rather pleased.

It wasn't an _excessive_ schedule; she wanted that understood. It had four lines. It had a title in capitals and a footnote, and each line had a date, a time, a purpose and a name, in a colour-coded table, in three inks, so that nobody could be in any doubt what was required.

_SOLSTICE PACT: PREPARATIONS (DRAFT 3)_

_Wed 16th, 7pm. Wishes: format. Venue: 3rd-floor classroom. Refreshments: M. (tea)_

_Fri 18th, 7pm. Wishes: content. Refreshments: Z. (pasties?)_

_Sun 20th, 7pm. Final run-through. Refreshments: F. (mince pies)_

_Mon 21st, dusk. THE WALK. Refreshments: E. (TBC)_

_Please reply by owl by Tuesday midnight if you have a conflict. If none, I will assume yes._

It was a good schedule, and a kind one, she thought. The point of it was that nobody would have to think. They were all so busy. If somebody else did the remembering, then the pact, the long night and its wishes, the thing they had promised each other by a fire a year ago, would simply _happen_, on time, with snacks.

She folded it in three and sent three owls, one each, and then stood at the window of the Ravenclaw common room until the last of them was a dot over the lake. She checked the sill twice before midnight, in case of a reply, and once more after. At midnight, when no replies had come, she told herself firmly that this was because they had all, quite properly, assumed yes.

---

On Wednesday at seven she was in the classroom.

She had got there at twenty to. It was on the third floor at the end of a cold corridor, behind a door with a stiff handle, and it hadn't been opened since October. It smelt of dust and old cushions. The hearth was cold. The armchair sat in the middle of the rug where they'd left it, the window over the lake was grey with cobweb, and she stood on the threshold with a kettle in one hand and a bag of cushions in the other and remembered last year in one go. The frost. The candles. Elliot, laughing. Finn on the floor with a blanket round his shoulders.

Then she got to work. She lit the fire, swept the floor with a charm, arranged the cushions in a ring and set out four cups, a plate of shortbread, and in front of each place a small card with a name on it in her best hand. She found a sprig of holly in the cupboard and put it in a jar. She had also written an agenda: four items, twenty-five minutes, and Any Other Business, which she'd underlined and then, on reflection, put in brackets in case it sounded presumptuous.

She moved Elliot's card a quarter of an inch to the left, to give their cushion more room, and then back again, in case that looked like a hint.

It looked lovely.

It was five past seven.

She sat down in her own place, folded her hands, and waited.

Ten past. Quarter past. The fire cracked and settled. Somewhere down the corridor a clock struck the half-hour in a deep, flat voice, and a draught under the door stirred the paper under the shortbread. She took out her notebook and wrote in the margin, _7.30. No sign_. She crossed it out and wrote _Probably Quidditch_, and underlined it. At a quarter to eight, by her own calculations, one could still describe a person as fashionably late. At eight, one would have to use a different word. She had not decided what.

She tried the opening line aloud, to the fire. _Thank you all for coming._ It sounded like the start of a funeral. She tried _Hello!_ and that sounded worse.

At twenty-five to eight, before she'd had to choose a word, she heard feet.

They came pounding up the stairs two at a time, along the corridor, and stopped with a thump outside the door. The handle rattled. The door flew open, and Zara Okonkwo, in Quidditch robes with her braids half undone and mud on both knees, fell into the room and stopped dead.

"Sorry," she gasped. "Sorry, sorry. Practice ran over. Jamie made me do one more, and then Tamsin lost a glove, and we couldn't—" She put a hand on the doorframe and looked round at the cushions, the fire and the little cards.

Her eyes went to the parchment on the mantelpiece, laid out flat with a paperweight on each corner. In the firelight it looked very large and very orderly, and very much like a thing done on purpose.

"Is this," said Zara, with a gleam of tired, affectionate mischief, "an _O.W.L._?"

It was a joke. Mira knew it was a joke. She had known Zara for five years and could have drawn that grin from memory. It was exactly the joke you make about a friend's colour-coded timetable, over breakfast, with toast. It was what Mira herself would have said.

She had been sitting by herself for forty minutes.

"Somebody," said Mira, "has to organise it."

It came out smaller than she'd meant, stiff and rather cold in the warm room. And she saw it land.

Zara's face didn't crumple. It went blank, a quick flicker and a closing, like the shutter of a camera, and the grin was gone.

"I didn't know," said Zara slowly, "that we needed organising."

Neither of them said anything for about four seconds. A log shifted in the hearth. The holly jar rocked on the sill. Zara stood in the doorway with her gloves in one hand, and Mira sat on her cushion with the notebook open in her lap and the feeling that the whole evening was sliding sideways, very gently, like a tray, and nothing to say that would stop it.

"I only meant—" she began.

"No, I know. It's fine." Zara looked at the cushions. "That's a lot of cushions."

"I thought, since it's been—"

"Mira, I—"

"Sorry I'm late!" cried a voice from the corridor.

---

Finn came round the door like a small apologetic avalanche, a sack of compost under one arm and a lantern in his other hand burning a dull, wavering gold, with mud on his face. He had plainly come straight from the greenhouses. He stopped on the threshold and looked at the cushions, the cards, the fire and the two of them, and Mira watched him walk into a room he'd thought was warm and find it slightly colder than expected.

"Oh," said Finn. "Am I—is it—"

"You're fine," said Mira.

"I'm covered in—" He looked down. "Sorry. The frostblooms. Sprout needed a hand with the last of the, and I lost track, and then it was—" He put the compost down. "Where do I sit?"

There was a card on each cushion. Hers. Zara's. Elliot's. His own. He stood looking at the one that said _Finn_, in the middle of the ring, all freckled, muddy, well-meaning bulk, and plainly couldn't tell whether it was a joke.

"It's only a seating plan," said Mira. "It doesn't matter. Sit anywhere."

"I'll sit on the floor," said Finn, with great diplomacy, and did.

Nobody said anything.

It wasn't hostile. That was the odd part, she would think afterwards. Nobody was cross. Zara had sat down heavily on the nearest cushion, and Finn was making a polite, careful effort to look at the fire. But the quiet had the baffled quality of something interrupted before it had begun. A cup rattled. The kettle started to sing. Zara reached for a piece of shortbread, caught Mira's eye, and put it back, which was somehow the worst thing yet.

Mira noticed the lantern then, propped against Finn's knee. She had seen it lit at its best, that first week, the colour of the inside of a loaf. Now it burned dull and low, and wavered whenever he looked at it, as if it were trying to keep out of the way.

"Where's Elliot?" said Finn.

"I don't know," said Mira. "They didn't reply."

"They're probably—"

"I expect so."

"It's a busy time," said Zara.

"It is. It's very busy."

"We could—" said Finn.

"It's all right," said Mira.

She heard her own voice. It sounded perfectly pleasant, the voice of someone who has decided to be sensible, and she got to her feet and began, with great calm, to collect the cards.

"It was only a draft," she said. "I thought I'd try. It was a bit much, honestly. We'll just do it on the night. I expect it'll be fine."

"Mira," said Zara.

"I'll take the tea."

"Mira, wait—"

"It's _fine_."

Finn held out his own card as she came to him, and she took it without looking at him, which she regretted at once. She put the cards in her pocket, picked up the plate, and stood for a moment in the middle of the room with her back very straight, looking, she hoped, like somebody who had been planning to go anyway.

Finn got up, slowly, one hand on the cushion. He didn't say anything. He gave her a long, anxious, kind look, and then, instead of saying what she was afraid he would say, he opened the door.

---

She walked back alone.

The corridors were dark and cold, most of the torches down to red coals and the portraits asleep. She went slowly. At some point she put the plate of shortbread down on the outstretched gauntlet of a suit of armour, which accepted it without comment, and she walked on for a whole corridor before she noticed her hands were empty. She did not go back.

It was perfectly all right, she told herself, and began to build the case. A misunderstanding. They were tired, she had been a bit much, and it was the sort of thing that happens between friends and by morning would be a joke. Zara had been tired, and had made a joke, and it had been a good joke; the problem was that Mira had not laughed, and she had known as it left her mouth what the not-laughing would cost. She went through the evening again as one goes through a proof, looking for the line where it went wrong, and kept arriving at the same line, which was hers.

She came out on the gallery above the Entrance Hall and stopped.

It was nearly empty. One torch burned at the foot of the stair, and in its light the banner hung huge and still from the rail to the floor, the lake going away across it and the lanterns going away over the lake. She put her hands on the cold stone and looked at it for a long time.

_E.N._

She had known it was Elliot's for a whole day. And in that day she hadn't once gone and said _it's the most beautiful thing I've seen_. She had written a timetable.

The realisations came slowly, one at a time, like lights going on down a corridor. She went through the schedule in her head, line by line. _Wishes: format. Wishes: content. Final run-through._ It had her tea. It had Zara's pasties, with a question mark. It had Finn's mince pies. It did not have a single thing in it that anybody else had said.

She hadn't asked.

She hadn't asked what Zara wanted. She hadn't asked where Elliot had been, or what they'd been making, or why they hadn't said. She hadn't asked Finn what he was carrying in a lantern that burned so low, or why he'd walked in with mud on his face and the look of someone braced for an argument. She had been so sure she knew what they needed that in three weeks she hadn't once said _how are you?_

Down in the Hall, the torch hissed.

She thought of the three owls, going out into the dark that Tuesday with her handwriting, and not one of them bringing a thing back. She had taken that for agreement. It might equally have been three people with nothing to say to a table of dates.

In her room she took out a fresh sheet of parchment and her best pen. She wrote _SOLSTICE_ at the top in capitals and crossed it out. She wrote _PACT_ and crossed that out too. She sat for a long time with the candle burning down, looking at the blank page and at the thing she wanted to put on it, which was very short and very hard.

At the bottom, in the middle, in small plain letters, she wrote a single word.

_Ask._
