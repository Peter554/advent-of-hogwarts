By seven o'clock on Sunday evening, Mira's lantern was the best made in the Charms classroom, and the only one in it that had never been lit.

She had worked on it for three weeks, in spare half-hours and the long dark stretch after supper, with a set of jeweller's files, a tin of brass polish and the loupe Dad had given her when she turned eleven. The hinges were true. The panes were cut by hand and slid into their grooves with a faint, expensive sigh. Round the base she had carved a line of runes, three words of Elder Futhark from a charm for safe travel, very small, which she had not mentioned to anyone.

It stood on the end of the bench, gleaming. She had also left a card beside it, in her best hand: _Has been polished. Has been asked nicely._

"It's fine," she told it, quietly, so that nobody would hear. "I've no objection. I'd only like it noted that I followed the instructions."

Flitwick had left the classroom open until nine, with a lamp at the end of every row and a tray of tea going cold on his desk, for the fifth years who wanted to finish their charm-work before tomorrow. About a dozen of them were scattered round the long room, murmuring at their brass. In the black window Mira could see the whole class reflected, small and gold and far away, and in the middle of it a girl with a braid, sitting very straight, watching a lantern the way you watch a kettle.

It was ten past seven. It was a Sunday. She had been putting off thinking about that all afternoon.

_Sun 20th, 7pm. Final run-through. Refreshments: F. (mince pies)._

It was only a coincidence. It was not even a coincidence. It was where the days had fallen.

"Hello," said a small voice.

---

Wren was standing at the end of the bench with a book against her chest, like someone who has come to the right room and would like permission to be sure. She had pinned back her fringe. Mira noticed this, and felt something go soft in her, and looked quickly at the lantern.

"I know you said whenever," said Wren. "I thought I'd check it was a good time. I don't have to stay."

"It's a perfectly good time. I'm only staring at a lantern."

"Is it—"

"It isn't lit."

Wren looked at it for a long while, head on one side, reading it. "It's lovely, though."

"It isn't lit."

"I know. But it's _lovely_."

Mira opened her mouth to say that this was not the point of a lantern, and shut it again. Nobody had ever looked at her failure with so little alarm.

"Would you like to help with the glass?" she said instead. "There's one pane left. It wants steadier hands than mine."

Wren sat down at her elbow and took the last pane between her fingertips as if it were a soap bubble. It slid into the groove with the smallest click.

"Is that right?"

"That's exactly right."

Behind them somebody said, "Mira? Sorry. Mira, is the glass supposed to rattle?"

It was Oliver, holding his lantern at arm's length. It rattled, quite loudly, like a small tin of buttons. She took it, found the loose pane, eased it a hair to the left and handed it back. It stopped.

"You're a witch," said Oliver reverently, and went away.

---

The others came in one at a time, which was not arranged. Mira would have remembered if it was arranged.

Zara came first, in her training jumper and a pair of grey wool socks, with a paper bag from the kitchens that she put down on the bench like a cheque. "Pasties," she announced. "Don't say anything. They're from Dibby."

"I didn't—"

"It was on the schedule."

"It was a _draft_."

"It had a question mark on it," said Zara. "I've removed it." She patted Mira on the head, once, as you would a very good dog, and went to find her lantern, which turned out to be in the cupboard, and which she carried back by a new leather handle that fell off.

It was the third handle that week. She picked it up off the floor and looked at it. "It's the glove thumb," she said. "I wanted something from the pitch. It's very sentimental and it doesn't hold."

"Hold it by the frame," said Mira.

"I _like_ the handle."

"It's on the floor."

"It's a work in progress," said Zara, and bound it on again with a great deal of spit and string. Her lantern was lit. It had been lit since yesterday afternoon, a steady orange-red, and she kept glancing at it sideways, as if it might think better of it. On the top, scratched with a pin in cramped capitals, it said _J.W. WAS HERE._ "I got Jamie to sign it. For luck."

Finn arrived next, forty minutes late, with a warm tin under one arm and flour to the elbow. He opened it. Inside were two rows of small golden mince pies with stars on top. He did not say what day it was. He just looked at Mira over the lid, and the corner of his mouth went up.

"Seven o'clock," he said. "Mince pies."

"It's twenty to eight."

"I got held up. By a Mimbulus."

And last of all, slipping in at the back with a gust of linseed oil, came Elliot, with their hands in their pockets and a lantern swinging from one finger. "I've done the pennants," they said. "All six. Filch has them. I never want to see blue again."

"Show me."

"No."

"Please."

"Tomorrow," said Elliot, "by the lake," and put their lantern down on the bench, where it glowed, level and amber, as if it had never in its life been shy. Round the base, in a very fine hand, ran a line of small capitals. Mira read it upside down.

"'Please do not look directly at this.'"

"I did that on the third," said Elliot. "It was true at the time."

"It's lit now."

"I'm not going to scrape it off. It's my work."

Priya was the very last. She came in from the kitchens with a tray: a teapot under a knitted cosy in the shape of a pineapple, six cups, a jug of milk, and a plate of the shortbread Mira had made on Wednesday in a different frame of mind. She set it down in the middle of the bench, stood back, and looked at the whole table, at the lanterns and the paper bags and the five people in two inches of lamplight. Her dry, quick, cool face did something slow.

"I posted it yesterday," she said. "Two out of three. I'm at peace."

"The spiral?"

"Most of the spiral. The third problem has no solution. Or it does, and it's a rude one." She poured the tea. "I'm told that's the point."

Mira watched the steam go up from the cups. She thought, with a rush so unreasonable that she had to pick up a file to deal with it: _They came. Nobody told them to._

---

They worked.

It was odd, afterwards, how little anyone said. There were six lanterns on the bench in various stages of repair and a hundred small things to do to them, and without discussing it each person took whichever thing needed doing. Zara tapped a hinge straight with the back of a spoon. Elliot, with a steel nib, added something minute to the inside of their frame. Priya held the wicks. Wren passed things, a file, a pot of paste, a rag, with the anticipating look of a child at a very important tea party, and every so often said "Is this right?" and was told that it was.

And slowly Mira noticed a pattern.

"Mira, where's the little—"

"In the tin."

"Mira, would you look at this—"

"You've put the wick in backwards."

"Mira, is it meant to go _that_ way?"

By half past eight she had fixed two hinges, found a wick, retrieved a pane from under Zara's jumper, and explained the same thing about the Elder Futhark to three different people. Her own lantern was by the window. It had not moved. It was, as far as she could tell, the most consulted lantern in the room and the least lit.

"I've done everything right," she said, to nobody, or possibly to Finn, who happened to be next to her with a pastry. "That's the thing. I did every single thing it said. I made a list."

"I know," said Finn, with his mouth full. "I've seen the list."

"I'd just like to know what I did _wrong_."

"Maybe nothing," said Finn. "Maybe it's like seeds. You can do it all right and still have to wait."

She looked at him. He shrugged, with great simplicity, and went to see about the Mimbulus in his bag.

"Miss Patel."

---

Flitwick had come up beside her without a sound, which was the advantage of being small, and was climbing the stack of books at the end of the desk one at a time with an air of mild enjoyment. At the top he peered at her lantern, and then at her.

"Very fine work," he said. "I have rarely seen a neater binding. Whoever taught you the hinge-charm taught you well."

"Thank you, Professor."

"It is, however, still dark."

"Yes," said Mira. It came out small. "I think I've done something wrong. I've checked every step. I don't think it's the lantern."

"No," said Flitwick. "I should say not."

He looked at it a moment longer with his white head on one side. Then he remarked, in the cheerful, humming voice of a man commenting on the weather:

"It lights from what you carry, Miss Patel. Not from what you've planned."

And he was already climbing down, to ask a startled boy in Hufflepuff yellow about a loose rivet.

She stood where she was. It wasn't advice, particularly. He hadn't waited for an answer. But the words had gone in like a pin into a cork, and she stood there with her tea going cold and the clink of brass all round her, and heard them again.

_What you carry._

She had thought it was the schedule. She had thought so for three weeks. It was the obvious answer, and it was what people said about her, quite fondly, with a smile: _oh, that's Mira, she'll have a plan._ But the lantern hadn't lit, and the reason, she now saw, was that it wasn't true.

She turned round.

Oliver was sitting with his lantern in his lap, rattle-free, stroking the glass with his thumb as if it were a nervous animal. Zara's handle had held for four whole minutes. Priya was asleep on her folded arms with a wick in one hand. And at the end of the bench Wren sat with her chin on her fist and her lips moving very slightly, trying a line of something for sound. _A book I haven't written yet._

It was a small thing. It was a book on the Cheshire Cat, left on a chair. It was a note on a wall, in four hands. It was a green line in the dark asking _is that all right?_, and an old woman with faded blue marks on her knuckles turning Mira's hand over in the candlelight. It was finding something, and wanting, before anything else, to put it down where somebody else could find it.

It happened without a sound. There was no flash. There was only, at the edge of her eye, a faint pale softening, like the first thin light on snow, and she turned her head. On the bench by the window, behind its row of perfect panes, a small flame had stood up from the wick. It was a clear, cool, particular blue, the blue of a very early morning. It lit the runes at the base one by one, the way the sun lights a row of hills.

She did not call out. She did not say _look_. She stood perfectly still and breathed out, as if she had been holding that breath for nineteen days.

Then she took the card from beside it, _Has been asked nicely_, and put it quietly in her pocket, and picked up the lantern in both hands and carried it down the room.

---

They put them on the worktable at nine, when Flitwick came round with a taper murmuring "Time, time" and "Leave them, leave them, they sleep better warm." The others went ahead. Coats had to be found, and tin lids, and one glove. Priya was being steered to the door by Zara, in the manner of a sleepwalker being moved to a better bed.

Mira stayed a moment longer.

She set hers at the end of the row. Orange-red, gold, amber, blue: four little fires on a long dark bench, each lighting the one beside it, so that the wall and the great black windows were warm with four colours running together at the edge of a painting. She hadn't expected it to be so beautiful. She had thought, for some reason, that it would look like four separate lights.

"Mira?" called Finn, from the door.

"Coming."

She turned, and at the far end of the bench, on a stool, with her chin on her hand and her eyes very wide, sat a first-year with a smear of green on her cheek. She hadn't moved. She was looking at the lanterns the way she had looked at the alcove, the first time, with the face of someone who has been afraid a thing wasn't there, and has found it.

"It's blue," said Wren, very softly.

"Yes."

"I thought it would be."

Mira smiled. She went to join them at the door, and left behind her in the dark, in a long row of light, all four.
