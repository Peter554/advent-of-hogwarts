By seven o'clock on Sunday evening, Mira's lantern was the most beautiful thing in the Charms classroom, and the only one that had never been lit.

She had worked on it for three weeks. She had done it with great thoroughness, and in her own time, in spare half-hours between lessons and in the long dark stretch after supper, with a set of tiny files and a tin of brass polish and a jeweller's loupe that she'd had from Dad when she was eleven. The frame was perfect. The hinges were true. The little glass panes, which Flitwick had told them were no thicker than a fingernail, had been cut to fit by hand, and slid into their grooves with the faint, even, silky resistance of something well made. She had carved a line of runes round the base, in a very small and clean hand: three words of Elder Futhark, from a charm for safe travel, which she had found in a book and had not told anybody about. The whole thing gleamed.

It stood on the bench in front of her, a gleaming brass lantern with an immaculate wick. It was still dark.

"It's all right," she said, aloud, to nobody. "It's perfectly all right. I don't mind."

The classroom was almost empty. It was the last night. Flitwick had left the doors open until nine, with a lamp lit at the end of every row and a tray of tea going cold on the desk, for the fifth years who wanted to finish the final charm-work before tomorrow, and about a dozen of them were scattered round the long room in twos and threes, hunched over benches, murmuring to their brass. The window behind her was black. In it she could see the reflection of the whole class, small and gold and far away, and in the middle of it, very straight, a girl with a braid, with her hands flat on the bench on either side of a lantern, watching it as one watches a kettle.

It was ten past seven. It was a Sunday. She had a very odd and complicated feeling about that, which she'd been putting off thinking about all afternoon.

_Sun 20th, 7pm. Final run-through. Refreshments: F. (mince pies)._

It was only a coincidence. It wasn't even a coincidence. It was just where the days had happened to fall.

"Hello."

---

It was Wren.

She was standing at the end of the bench, in her school robes, with a book held against her chest, in the careful posture of someone who has come to the right room and is not at all sure that she has. She'd tidied her hair. She had been, Mira saw, with a little lurch of tenderness, to the trouble of pinning back her fringe.

"I'm sorry," said Wren. "I know you said whenever. I thought I'd check if it was a good time. I don't have to stay."

"It's a perfectly good time. I'm only staring at a lantern."

"Is it—"

"It's not lit."

"Oh." Wren looked at it. She looked at it for quite a long time, with her head slightly on one side, and the grave, deliberate attention of someone reading a very small print. "It's lovely, though."

"It's not lit."

"I know. But it's _lovely_."

Mira opened her mouth to say that this was not the point, that the whole point of a lantern was that it should be lit, that she'd had the best of intentions and the finest brass and had followed the instructions to the letter and nothing, in three weeks, not a spark. She closed it again. There was something in the face opposite, a patient, unhurried, quite unalarmed look, like a person standing at the edge of a pond, that made it impossible to go on.

"Would you like to help with the glass?" said Mira.

"Can I?"

"There's one pane left. It wants a steady hand. I've got too many fingers."

Wren sat down on the stool at her elbow. She put her book on the bench. She took the last of the panes between the tips of her fingers, with a delicacy that Mira had seen only twice in her life, once in a jeweller, and once in Finn, and held it in the lamplight so that it threw a thin, bright, trembling line across her cheek.

"Where does it go?"

"The back left. The groove's a bit tight. You've got to ease it in from the top."

"Like this?"

"Like that."

She eased it in. It went with the smallest possible click.

They sat for a little in silence, looking at the lantern. It was a good silence. It had the loose, easy, companionable weight of a thing that need not be filled, and it went on for about a minute, while the lamps hissed and somewhere at the other end of the room a boy said "_Ow_" very quietly, and sucked his thumb.

"What's yours?" said Wren.

"What's what?"

"What you carry. In it." She was looking at the glass. She said it very simply. "Not that I'm asking. I only wondered. I don't have a lantern. First-years don't. But I've been wondering what mine would be."

"What do you think it would be?"

Wren thought. It took her some time.

"A book I haven't written yet," she said.

It was said quite flatly, without any particular weight. It was only a thing she'd noticed about herself, like the colour of her hair. Mira found that she was looking at the girl properly, at the thin face, and the ink-stained hand, and the green sleeve with its worn cuff, and an idea went through her, slow and quiet, like a bell struck in a distant room.

"What's it about?"

"I don't know yet. That's why it's not written." She smiled. It was a little crooked. "It's just a feeling that it's in there. Waiting. Like, you know when you walk into a room, and somebody's just left it?"

"Yes," said Mira, "I do."

---

The others came in a little after half past.

They came one at a time. It wasn't planned. She'd have remembered if it was. Zara was first, with a paper bag from the kitchens and a very large grin, in a pair of grey wool socks and her old training jumper, announcing from the door, "_Pasties_. Don't say anything. They're from Dibby." She dropped the bag on the bench and put a hand on top of Mira's head, briefly, as one pats a dog. "I said I'd bring pasties. I'm bringing pasties."

"I didn't ask—"

"It was on the schedule," said Zara.

"It was a _draft_."

"It had a question mark. I've removed it." She grinned. "Where's my frame? I think I left it in the cupboard."

Finn came next. He had a large tin under his arm, still warm, which he put down, carefully, beside the pasties, and opened to show a double row of small golden mince pies, with stars on the top. He didn't say what day it was. He didn't have to. He only looked at Mira, over the lid of the tin, with his freckled, honest, wide-open face, and the corner of his mouth moved.

"Seven o'clock," said Finn. "Mince pies."

"It's twenty to eight."

"I got held up. By a Mimbulus."

And last, a long time after, slipping in at the back with a cold draught and the faint ultramarine smell of linseed oil, came Elliot, with their hands in their pockets and a wary, tired, rather pleased expression, and a lantern swinging from the crook of one finger.

"I've done them," they said. "The pennants. All six. Filch has them. I never want to see blue again."

"Show me," said Mira.

"No."

"Please."

"Tomorrow," said Elliot, "by the lake," and put the lantern on the bench with a faint, ironic, ringing clink, and Mira, who had been about to say a number of things, found that she couldn't, and only nodded.

Priya was the very last of all.

She came in at ten to eight, from the kitchens, with a great tray in both hands: a teapot under a knitted cosy shaped like a pineapple, six cups, a jug of milk, and a small silver dish of the sugar-dusted shortbread that Mira had made, on Wednesday, in a completely different frame of mind. She set it down, with a soft clatter, in the middle of the long bench, and stood back, and looked at the whole group, the four friends and the two first-years round a single worktable, in a pool of lamplight, with their lanterns and their brass and their paper bags, and her face, which was usually so quick and dry and cool, did something slow.

"I posted it," she said. "Yesterday. Two of three. I'm at peace."

"You got the spiral?"

"I got most of the spiral." She shrugged. "It turns out the third one doesn't have a solution. Or if it does, it's a very rude one. I'm told that's the point." She poured the tea. It came out dark and fragrant, in a long, steaming rope, and Mira, watching her, thought, with a sudden, overwhelming and quite unreasonable rush of gladness, _she's here, they're all here, and nobody told them to be_.

---

They worked.

That was the odd thing, afterwards. They didn't talk about it. They didn't arrange it. There were six lanterns on the bench, in various states, and a hundred small things to be done to them, and each of them, without a word, took the one that wanted doing. Zara cut a length of leather cord and bound a handle. Finn tapped a bent hinge straight with the handle of a spoon. Elliot, with a pen and a small steel nib, was inscribing something, very fine, in a circle round the base of their own. Priya held the wicks. Wren, between them all, passed things: a file, a pot of paste, a scrap of cloth, with the careful anticipating look of a child at a very important tea party, and every so often said "Is this right?" and was told that it was.

The pasties were quite good. The mince pies were better. The shortbread was gone before anyone noticed.

"I've found it," said Zara, at about half past eight, and held up her lantern. It was bound in new leather, and on the top, in neat, cramped capitals, scratched with a pin, someone had put _J.W. WAS HERE_. "I got Jamie to sign. For luck. I think it's the best thing I've done to it."

"It's already lit."

"It's lit _now_. It might go out."

"It won't go out," said Elliot, without looking up.

They were right, too. It was blazing on the bench, between the others, a clear, warm, level orange-red. Beside it Finn's, lower, steadier, an even gold, like a hearth. Beside _that_, Elliot's, a deep amber, glowing quietly in the heart of the circle of lamps in a room full of people, and showing not the slightest sign of going out.

Three lanterns. And, at the end of the row, Mira's.

She'd been trying not to look at it. She had been, in fact, trying not to look at it for the best part of an hour, with the stubborn, dignified, slightly desperate concentration of someone walking past a mirror. It stood by the window, in the dark, in a ring of brass gleam, with its fine wick and its row of tiny runes and its perfect, unlit glass, and every time she glanced at it, in the middle of a sentence, her whole chest tightened, like a fist.

_I did it right_, she thought. _I did everything. What else is there?_

"Miss Patel."

---

It was Flitwick.

He had come up beside her so quietly that she hadn't heard him: a very small, very old figure in dark robes, climbing up the books on the end of the desk, one at a time, as he did, with great agility and an air of mild enjoyment. He reached the top. He peered, from there, from the great height of about four feet and a half, at the lantern, and then at her.

"It's very fine work," said Professor Flitwick. "I have rarely seen a neater job of the binding. Whoever taught you the hinge-charm taught you well."

"Thank you, Professor."

"It is, however, still dark."

"Yes," said Mira. It came out small. She cleared her throat. "I think I've done something wrong. I've gone over it, I've checked every—I've followed the notes exactly, I made a list, and—I don't know what it is. I don't think it's the lantern."

"No," said Flitwick. "I should say not."

He looked at it for a moment longer, with his white head tilted. And then he said, in the same quiet, humming, cheerful voice, as a person might remark on the weather,

"It lights from what you carry, Miss Patel. Not from what you've planned."

She stood quite still.

It wasn't a speech. It wasn't, even, advice. It was said lightly, almost in passing, and he had already turned to the next bench, and begun a murmured conversation about a loose rivet with a startled boy in Hufflepuff yellow. But the words had gone into her like a pin into a cork, and she stood there, in the lamplight, with the tea going cold at her elbow and the voices and the clink of brass going on, and heard them again, in the long quiet inside her head.

_What you carry_.

She had thought it was the schedule. She'd thought it for three weeks. She had stood by the window, in the long evenings, and gone through it in her mind: the lists, the plans, the careful proportions of her days; the notebook with its fourteen futures; the great, high, gleaming, anxious structure that she had built to keep them all from falling. It was the first thing that had occurred to her. It was the obvious thing. It was the thing that people said, about her, quite fondly, with a smile, _oh, that's Mira, she'll have a plan_.

But the lantern hadn't lit. And she understood, at last, slowly, standing in a classroom full of the smell of brass polish and shortbread, that it hadn't lit because it wasn't true.

She looked round.

Wren was at the end of the bench, with her chin on her fist, watching Elliot's pen. She had a smear of green on her cheek. She was frowning, a little, in the grave, absorbed, delighted way that she had, and her lips were moving very slightly, as if she were saying something under her breath, a line of something, trying it for sound.

_A book I haven't written yet._

It came to Mira all at once.

It was a small thing. It was so small that she nearly missed it. It was a book on the Cheshire Cat, left on a chair. A note on a wall, in four hands, saying _you're part of it now_. A green line, in the dark, saying _is that all right?_ And a girl in a fringe, in a doorway, with her eyes enormous, saying _I thought it was the best thing I'd seen_. It was a trunk in a spare room, in a house she'd never seen, and an old woman's hand, with a row of faded blue marks, turning hers over in the candlelight. It was a long, quiet, patient, unglamorous habit of her whole life. It was finding a thing, and wanting, before anything else, to put it down where somebody else could find it.

She wasn't carrying a plan. She was carrying a door.

It happened without a sound.

There was no flash, no spark. Only, at the edge of her eye, a faint pale softening in the dark, like the first thin light on snow before the sun comes up, and she turned her head, and there, on the bench by the window, in its ring of brass gleam, behind its row of perfect panes, a small, steady, clear flame had stood up from the wick. It was a pale, gentle, cool, quite particular blue. It was the blue of a very early morning. It lit the runes at the base, one by one, as the sun lights a row of hills.

She didn't say anything.

She didn't call out. She didn't say _look_. She stood perfectly still, with her hands at her sides, and looked at it, and a long, slow, quiet breath went out of her, as though she'd been holding it for nineteen days.

Then she picked it up, in both hands, and carried it down the room.

---

They put them on the worktable in the end, at nine, when the lamps were being put out, and Professor Flitwick had gone round with a taper, saying "Time, time," in a murmuring, apologetic voice, and "Leave them, leave them, they sleep better warm." The others had gone ahead. They'd been collecting coats, and tin lids, and an odd glove, in the half-dark, in a loose, laughing, scattered way, with Priya shepherding them towards the door.

Mira stayed a moment longer.

She set her lantern down at the end of the row. It was the last. There were four of them now: orange-red and gold and amber and blue, in a line, on the long dark bench, in the quiet, in the thickening shadow, each with its small steady flame, each lighting the one beside it, so that the whole of the table, and the wall behind, and the great black windows, were warm with a glow like four colours running together at the edge of a painting.

It was very beautiful. She hadn't expected it to be. She had thought, for some reason, that it would look like four separate lights.

"Mira?" called Finn, from the door.

"Coming."

She turned. And at the far end of the bench, in the dark, quite still, on a stool, with her chin on her hand and her eyes very wide, a small, thin first-year with a smear of green on her cheek sat looking at them.

She wasn't saying anything. She didn't move. She only sat there, in the light of four lanterns, with an expression that Mira had seen only once before, in a library alcove, in a Ravenclaw first-year who had stood in the doorway, reading, and discovered that a thing she had been afraid was not there was, in fact, quite real.

"It's blue," said Wren, very softly.

"Yes."

"I thought it would be."

Mira smiled. She went to join them at the door, and left, behind her in the dark, on the bench, in a long row of light, all four.
