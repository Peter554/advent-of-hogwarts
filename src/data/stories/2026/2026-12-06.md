"A captain," Professor Vance had said, on Friday, in his office, with his hands folded on the desk and the expression of a man reading out a weather report, "is a very difficult thing to be on one's own."

Zara had said she wasn't on her own. She had a team.

"Quite," said Vance. "I'd think about that." He had handed her a leaflet on stress and talent and the proper management of both. She'd said thank you, read it in the corridor, and put it in the bin.

She thought about it now, at five to ten on Sunday morning, standing on the grass at the edge of the pitch with her broom across her shoulder and her breath coming out in clouds. The sky was a hard, clear blue. Ravenclaw filled the left-hand stand, a bank of blue and bronze with banners snapping, and Gryffindor the right, a roaring slope of red. There was frost on the goal hoops. It was the best Quidditch weather she could remember, and she'd have given a good deal for rain.

"You're doing the face," said Jamie, beside her.

"What face?"

"The one where you've got all the plays in your head and you're putting them in order." Jamie, who was small and straight-backed, with cropped hair and a way of looking at things as though she were taking their measurements, had her Seeker's gloves on already and was flexing her fingers. "It's a good face. It's just a bit loud."

"I'm not loud."

"You have _never_ been loud," said Jamie, and went to look for a piece of fluff on her broom.

---

It was a good match. That was the worst of it, afterwards.

They were up by ten past ten. Marcus took the first Quaffle off the Ravenclaw Chaser at the centre line and passed it, without looking, to Zara, who had known exactly where she'd be. She put it through the left hoop so cleanly that the Keeper was still diving at the right. Ten nothing. A red roar went up. Zara looped once, wide and slow, heart going like a drum, and took her place again.

At twenty past, it happened.

It happened the way things do in Quidditch, all at once. A Bludger came off a Beater's bat at a wild angle, not aimed at anyone, and Tamsin, who had her back to it and was shouting something at Marcus, took it between the shoulder blades.

She didn't scream. She folded forward over her broom, and the broom tipped and dropped, and for three seconds Zara, a hundred feet away and flying the wrong direction, forgot how to breathe.

Madam Hooch got there first. By the time Zara reached them, Tamsin was off the broom and sitting on the grass, white-faced and furious, with a hand pressed to her ribs. "I'm _fine_," she was saying. "It's nothing. Put me back."

"You are not going back up," said Madam Hooch, "with those ribs."

"Zara—"

"Go with Madam Hooch," said Zara. "It's all right. Go on."

Tamsin went, between two Gryffindor seventh years, chin up and eyes streaming. Zara hovered over the pitch and counted what she had left: Marcus, Katie in the goalmouth, the two Beaters, and Jamie, waiting at the edge with her eyes on the sky.

"We're a Chaser short," said Marcus.

"I know."

"We could ask for a substitute—"

"We haven't got one." The reserve had caught a cold on Thursday and was in the hospital wing with a fever and a lemon. She looked at Jamie and thought: _She flies as well as any of us. She's got the speed. She's got the hands._

She did not think of the other thing, the thing she would think of afterwards in the changing room and in the shower and at three in the morning with her eyes open: that if she took the Seeker off the Snitch, nobody would be watching for it.

She thought, instead: _We're ahead_.

"Jamie," she said. "I need you on the Quaffle."

Jamie gave her a brief look that Zara couldn't read.

"You're sure?"

"We get to a hundred and sixty and it doesn't matter when they find the Snitch. I'll watch for it myself, as best I can." She heard how that sounded. "I'll watch."

"All right," said Jamie, and dropped into place beside Marcus.

---

It worked. For forty minutes it worked as though it had been designed.

Jamie turned out to be a natural Chaser, which Zara hadn't known. She flew low and flat, elbows tucked, with an unhurried way of deciding things that made the Ravenclaw defence look as if it had been caught napping. She scored in her second minute and again in the eleventh. Marcus scored two. Zara scored four, one of them a long diving shot from the middle of the pitch that curved round the Keeper's glove and went through the hoop with a sound like a cork leaving a bottle, and the red stand rose to its feet.

Thirty. Sixty. A hundred. Ravenclaw hadn't scored at all.

"A hundred and thirty to nothing!" cried the commentator, a sixth-year Hufflepuff called Gwenllian whose amplified voice cracked on the high notes. "It's a massacre! It's a Gryffindor, uh, I'm told I'm not supposed to say massacre—"

A hundred and forty.

Zara hovered at the top of the pitch for half a second, pulse hammering, looked at the score, and did the sum.

_Twenty more_.

Two goals, and the game was won whatever happened with the Snitch. She turned her head, looking for Marcus, and saw the Ravenclaw Seeker.

She was a long way off, a small, tight figure in blue, hanging in the far corner of the sky, so quiet and still for so long that Zara had almost forgotten her. She was looking at nothing. She had, Zara thought, with a tiny cold shock, the posture of a dog that has heard something.

Zara opened her mouth.

The Seeker dived.

It wasn't a long dive. It was straight and fast and without any drama, like a stone dropped into a well, and at the bottom of it a hand shot out, and something small and gold flashed in the pale air, and a whistle blew.

"Ravenclaw have the Snitch!" said Gwenllian. "Ravenclaw have the Snitch, one hundred and fifty points, and, oh. Oh, that's—that's Ravenclaw by ten. A hundred and fifty to a hundred and forty."

For a moment nothing happened.

Then the left-hand stand exploded.

---

It was completely reasonable of them. Zara knew that. Ravenclaw hadn't won a game in two years. They went up like a firework, banners flung and scarves whirling, people hugging each other, and somewhere near the front a girl with a black braid and round glasses, who had been gripping her own scarf for fifty minutes, sprang up with both arms in the air and shouted a single wild _YES_.

It lasted about a second. Then Mira, hair coming out of its braid, scarf across her shoulder, looked across a hundred feet of empty air and met Zara's eyes.

She stopped so abruptly that she nearly lost her balance. Her arms came down. Her face, which had been wide open, shut like a book.

Zara, on her broom, with a Quaffle under one arm, found that she wasn't angry. It surprised her. She waited to be, the way you wait for a sneeze, and nothing came. She was only, very slightly, empty.

---

In the changing room, nobody blamed her.

That was the worst bit. It would have been easier if somebody had. Marcus sat on the bench with his head in his hands. Katie, who hadn't had a single shot to save all match, said very quietly, "We were so good." The Beaters sat side by side and said nothing. On the bench by Zara's locker, her lantern, which she'd brought down in her bag because she could no longer remember how not to, gave a dry little crackle, spat out one spark, and went dark again.

"We lost," said Marcus, to the floor.

"By ten," said Katie.

"We were up a hundred and forty."

"It was my call," said Zara. "I put Jamie on the Quaffle, and it was wrong."

"It wasn't," said Jamie.

She was on the bench opposite, gloves in her lap, quite still. She hadn't said a word since they'd come in.

"We were a Chaser short and a hundred and forty up. We'd have needed twenty more and we'd have got them." She turned her gloves over. "It's the Snitch. The Snitch just happened."

"I should have kept an eye—"

"You did. You looked, and she dived. You couldn't have got there." She said it levelly, without any particular kindness, like someone reading out a result. "That's not a mistake, Zara. That's Quidditch."

Zara looked at her. In the grey light, with her cropped hair and her straight back, Jamie was the steadiest thing in the room. And Zara thought, with a small clear shock like a drop of cold water, of Vance and his leaflet.

"Thanks," she said.

"I'm only telling you what happened," said Jamie.

---

She took a long time to come out.

By the time she did, the stands had emptied. The pitch lay in the thin white sun with its goalposts glittering and its trampled grass, and a few groundskeepers moved across it with sacks. She stood at the mouth of the tunnel with her broom on her shoulder. It was very cold. She did not particularly want to go anywhere.

"Zara."

Mira was sitting on the grass at the foot of the stand, knees drawn up, in a blue scarf. Two paper cups steamed beside her.

"I got you a hot chocolate," she said. "I mean, I got two. One was mine but I've drunk some of it. It's Winter Blend, from the kitchens. It's—" She stopped. "I'm sorry."

"What for?"

"I cheered."

"You're Ravenclaw."

"It was involuntary. I mean, I'm not sorry Ravenclaw won, I think on balance it's probably good for us, it's been a long time. But I saw your face, and I should have waited, I should at least have—" She drew a breath. "I only think there's a way to be pleased about a thing and also not be pleased about it, and I didn't manage it. So. Sorry."

Zara looked at her. Mira's scarf was crooked and there was grass on her skirt. She looked like someone preparing for a long argument, wound up so carefully, and so uselessly, round such a small thing that something in Zara came loose.

She laughed.

It came out without warning, a short surprised bark, and then another, and then she was bent over with her hands on her knees and her broom dropped on the grass, laughing helplessly.

"What?" said Mira. "What did I—"

"'I think on balance it's probably good for us,'" said Zara.

"Well, it is!"

"I know it is." She sat down heavily beside her and took a cup. It was almost too hot to hold. "It's a good win. I was furious for about two minutes and then I thought, they deserved it."

"They did," said Mira, a little doubtfully.

"They really did. That Seeker's brilliant. I want her." Zara drank. It tasted of cocoa and cardamom and, very faintly, chilli. "Ugh. That's good."

"Tibby says it's the cold that does it."

They sat for a while. Over the roof of the changing rooms, a small figure in yellow-and-black Hufflepuff robes had appeared on the path from the castle, walking quite fast and holding something above her head.

"Is that—"

"It's Amara," said Zara.

---

She had a piece of parchment, held out in front of her in both hands as if it might blow away. When she got near she stopped a couple of yards off and put one foot behind the other, like someone who had planned a speech and lost the front half.

"I wasn't sitting with you," said Amara. "In the stands."

"I know."

"I was on the other side. With Lottie."

"I know."

"I did want to. I just—" She went red and pushed out the parchment. "It's for you."

Zara took it. Inside, in thick round careful letters, with a great many lines under the important words, someone had written:

_CHEER UP. NETTLE SAYS HELLO._

Underneath was a drawing of a Knarl, remarkably accurate from nose to quills. It was sitting in a shed, wearing a small paper hat and a very reluctant expression, as if it had been told to say something polite.

"It was Hagrid's idea," said Amara. "The hat."

"I like the hat."

"He didn't like it. He chewed it a bit."

"I can see that," said Zara. There was a small damp dent in one corner of the page. She looked at it for longer than she needed to. Then she folded the parchment into her pocket, next to the Knarl spine, and said gruffly, "It was a good match."

"It was an _amazing_ match," said Amara. "You were really good. I'd have won, if I was you."

"You'd have lost."

"I'd have _done something_," said Amara, with deep conviction, and Mira made a small noise and turned it into a cough.

---

They walked back up to the castle together, the three of them, with the snow squeaking under their boots and the sound of Ravenclaw celebrations drifting from the Great Hall. Amara walked in the middle, telling them about Nettle's latest opinions on cheese. Mira said "Mm" at intervals, and "Really?", and once, "That's not generally considered a vegetable."

Zara listened and said nothing much. She was thinking of a leaflet in a bin. On Wednesday, or perhaps Thursday, when it wasn't so raw, she'd have a word with Jamie. A question, rather. She didn't know yet how to ask it. She'd think of a way.

The cocoa in her cup had gone cold. She drank it anyway.
