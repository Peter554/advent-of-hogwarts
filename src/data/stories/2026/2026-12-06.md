"A captain," Professor Vance had said, on Friday, in his office, with his hands folded on the desk and the expression of a man reading out a weather report, "is a very difficult thing to be on one's own."

Zara had said she wasn't on her own. She had a team.

"Quite," said Vance. "I'd think about that." And he'd handed her a leaflet on stress and talent and the proper management of both, and she'd said thank you, and left, and read it in the corridor, and put it in the bin.

She thought about it now, at five to ten on Sunday morning, standing on the grass at the edge of the pitch with her broom across her shoulder and her breath coming out in clouds. The sky was clear and hard and pale, a great glassy bowl of blue, and the stands on either side were already filling. There was Ravenclaw on the left, a solid bank of blue and bronze, banners snapping. There was Gryffindor on the right, a roaring slope of red. There was frost on the goal hoops. It was the best weather for Quidditch that she could remember, and she'd have given a good deal for it to be raining.

"You're doing the face," said Jamie, beside her.

"What face?"

"The one where you've got all the plays in your head and you're putting them in order." Jamie, who was a small, straight-backed girl with cropped hair and a way of looking at things as though she were taking their measurements, had her Seeker's gloves on already and was flexing her fingers. "It's a good face. It's just that it's a bit loud."

"I'm not loud."

"You have _never_ been loud," said Jamie, gravely, and went to look for a stray piece of fluff on her broom.

---

It was a good match. That was the worst part of it, afterwards: that it had been a really good match.

They were up by ten past ten. Marcus took the first Quaffle off the Ravenclaw Chaser at the centre line and passed it, without looking, to Zara, who had known exactly where she'd be; and she put it through the left hoop so cleanly that the Keeper was still diving at the right. Ten nothing. A great red roar went up. Zara looped once, wide and slow, her heart going like a drum, and took her place again.

And then, at twenty past, it happened.

It happened in the way that things always happen in Quidditch: all at once and very fast. A Bludger came out of nowhere. It wasn't aimed at anyone in particular, only a stray, a hard, vicious, wild ricochet off a Beater's bat, and Tamsin, who had her back to it and was shouting something at Marcus, took it between the shoulder blades.

She didn't scream. She just folded, forward over her broom, and the broom tipped and dropped, and for three long seconds, Zara, who was a hundred feet away and flying in the wrong direction, forgot how to breathe.

Madam Hooch got there first. By the time Zara reached them she had Tamsin off the broom and down on the grass, sitting up, white-faced and furious, with a hand pressed to her ribs. "I'm fine," Tamsin was saying. "I'm _fine_. It's nothing. Put me back."

"You are not going back up," said Madam Hooch, "with those ribs."

"Zara—"

"Go," said Zara. "Go with Madam Hooch. It's all right. Go on."

Tamsin went, between two Gryffindor seventh years, with her chin up and her eyes streaming. And Zara, hovering above the pitch with five minutes of whistle time left to find an answer, looked at the line of her team: Marcus, Katie in the goalmouth, the two Beaters, Jamie, hovering at the edge of the pitch with her eyes on the sky.

"We're a Chaser short," said Marcus.

"I know."

"We could call it. Ask for a substitute—"

"We haven't got one." They'd have been allowed to bring a reserve, but the reserve had caught a cold on Thursday and was in the hospital wing with a fever and a lemon. She looked at Jamie, and she thought: _Jamie flies as well as any of us. She's got the speed. She's got the hands_.

She didn't think of the other thing, the thing that, afterwards, she would think of over and over again, in the changing room and in the shower and at three in the morning with her eyes open. That if she took the Seeker off the Snitch there'd be nobody watching for it.

She thought, instead: _We're ahead_.

"Jamie," she said. "I need you on the Quaffle."

Jamie looked at her. It was the briefest of looks, and Zara couldn't read it.

"You're sure?"

"We're ahead," said Zara. "We keep scoring. We get to a hundred and sixty and it doesn't matter when they find the Snitch. I'll watch for it myself, as best I can." She heard how it sounded. "I'll watch."

"All right," said Jamie, and dropped into place beside Marcus.

---

It worked. For forty minutes, it worked as though it had been designed.

Jamie was a natural Chaser. Zara hadn't known that. She flew low and flat, with her elbows tucked in, and she had a cool, unhurried way of deciding things that made the Ravenclaw defence, which was used to a bigger, noisier game, look as though it had been caught napping. She scored in her second minute. She scored again in the eleventh. Marcus scored two. Zara scored four, one of them a long diving shot from the middle of the pitch that curved round the Keeper's glove and went through the hoop with a sound like a cork leaving a bottle, and the whole of the red stand rose to its feet.

Thirty. Sixty. A hundred. Ravenclaw hadn't scored at all.

"A hundred and thirty to nothing!" cried the commentator, a sixth-year Hufflepuff called Gwenllian whose amplified voice had a tendency to crack on the high notes. "It's a massacre! It's a Gryffindor, uh, I'm told I'm not supposed to say massacre—"

A hundred and forty.

And Zara, hovering at the top of the pitch for half a second, with her broom tilted and her pulse hammering in her ears, looked down at the score, and did the sum.

_Twenty more_.

That was all. That was two goals. Two more goals and the game was won, whatever happened with the Snitch. She turned her head, looking for Marcus, and she saw the Ravenclaw Seeker.

She was a long way off. She was a small, tight, determined figure in blue, hanging in the far corner of the sky, who had been so quiet and still for so long that Zara had almost forgotten she was there. She was looking at nothing. She had, Zara thought, with a tiny cold shock, the posture of a dog that has heard something.

Zara opened her mouth.

The Seeker dived.

It wasn't a long dive. It was straight, and fast, and without any drama at all, like a stone dropped into a well, and at the bottom of it, a hand shot out, and something small and gold flashed in the pale morning air, and a whistle blew.

"Ravenclaw have the Snitch!" said Gwenllian. "Ravenclaw have the Snitch, one hundred and fifty points, and, oh. Oh, that's—that's Ravenclaw by ten. A hundred and fifty to a hundred and forty."

For a moment nothing happened.

Then the left-hand stand exploded.

---

It was, of course, completely reasonable of them. Zara knew that. Ravenclaw hadn't won a game in two years. They went up like a firework, a roar of blue and silver and bronze, banners flung up and scarves whirled and people hugging each other and, somewhere near the front, a girl with a black braid and round glasses who had been sitting on her hands for fifty minutes with a face as tight as a drum, and who sprang up, with both arms in the air, and cried out.

It was one cheer. It lasted about a second. Mira, on her feet, her hair coming out of its braid and her scarf across her shoulder, shouted a single wild unselfconscious _YES_, and then she looked across the pitch, across a hundred feet of empty air, and met Zara's eyes.

She stopped.

She stopped so abruptly that she nearly lost her balance. Her arms came down. Her face, which had been wide open, shut like a book.

Zara, on her broom, with a Quaffle under one arm, found that she wasn't angry. It surprised her. She waited to be, in the way you wait for a sneeze, and nothing came. She was only, very slightly, empty.

---

In the changing room, nobody blamed her.

That was the worst bit, in fact. It would have been easier if somebody had. Marcus sat on the bench with his head in his hands. Katie, who hadn't had a single shot to save all match, said very quietly, "We were so good." The Beaters sat side by side and said nothing at all. On the bench by Zara's locker, her lantern, which she'd brought down in her bag for no reason except that she could no longer remember how not to, gave a small, dry, miserable crackle and spat out a single spark, and went dark again.

"We lost," said Marcus, to the floor.

"By ten," said Katie.

"We were up a hundred and forty."

"It was my call," said Zara. "I took Jamie off the Seeker. I put her on the Quaffle. It was my call and it was wrong."

"It wasn't," said Jamie.

She was sitting on the bench opposite, with her gloves in her lap, quite still. She hadn't said a word since they'd come in.

"It was the right call," she said. "At the time. We were a Chaser short. We were a hundred and forty up, and Ravenclaw hadn't scored. We'd have needed twenty more points and we'd have had them. It's only that—" She turned her gloves over. "It's the Snitch. The Snitch just happened."

"I should have kept an eye—"

"You did. You looked. You looked and she dived. You couldn't have got there." She said it levelly, without any particular kindness, as though she were reading out a result. "That's not a mistake, Zara. That's Quidditch."

Zara looked at her. In the grey light of the changing room, with her cropped hair and her straight back, Jamie was the steadiest thing in the world. And Zara thought, with a small clear shock like a drop of cold water, of Vance and his leaflet.

_It's a very difficult thing to be on one's own_.

"Thanks," she said.

"I'm only telling you what happened," said Jamie.

---

She took a long time to come out.

By the time she did the stands had emptied. The pitch lay in the thin white winter sun with its goalposts glittering and its trampled grass, and a few groundskeepers moving across it with sacks. She stood for a minute at the mouth of the tunnel with her broom on her shoulder. It was very cold. She did not particularly want to go anywhere.

"Zara."

Mira was sitting on the grass at the foot of the stand, with her knees drawn up, in a blue scarf. She had two paper cups beside her. They were steaming.

"I got you a hot chocolate," she said. "I mean, I got two. One was mine but I've drunk some of it. It's Winter Blend. I got it from the kitchens. It's—" She stopped. "I'm sorry."

"What for?"

"I cheered."

"You're Ravenclaw."

"It was involuntary. I mean, I'm not sorry Ravenclaw won, I think on balance it's probably good for us, it's been a long time. But I saw your face, and I shouldn't have, I mean, I should have waited, I should have at least—" She drew a breath. "I'm only saying that I think there's a way to be pleased about a thing and also not be pleased about it, and I didn't manage it. And I'm sorry."

Zara looked at her. Mira's scarf was crooked. She had grass on her skirt. She had the sort of tight, anxious, level-headed expression of someone preparing for a long argument, and the whole of her was wound up so carefully, and so uselessly, around such a small thing, that something in Zara's chest came loose.

She laughed.

It came out of her without warning, a short, surprised bark, and then another, and then she was bent over with her hands on her knees and her broom dropped on the grass, laughing helplessly, her eyes streaming.

"What?" said Mira. "What did I—"

"'I think on balance it's probably good for us,'" said Zara.

"Well, it is!"

"I know. I know it is." She sat down, heavily, beside her, and took a cup. It was almost too hot to hold. "It's a great win. It's a fantastic win. I was furious for about two minutes and then I thought, they deserved it."

"They did," said Mira, a little doubtfully.

"They _really_ did. That Seeker's brilliant. I want her." Zara drank. It tasted of cocoa and cardamom and, very faintly, chilli. "Ugh. That's good."

"Tibby says it's the cold that does it."

They sat for a while. The sun was climbing. Over the roof of the changing rooms, far off, a very small figure in yellow-and-black Hufflepuff robes had appeared on the path from the castle, and was walking towards them, quite fast, and holding something above her head.

"Is that—"

"It's Amara," said Zara.

---

She had a piece of parchment. She was holding it out in front of her with both hands, as if it might blow away, and when she got near she stopped, a couple of yards off, and put one foot behind the other, in the manner of someone who had planned a speech and lost the front half.

"I wasn't sitting with you," said Amara. "In the stands."

"I know."

"I was on the other side. With Lottie."

"I know."

"I did want to. I just—" She went red. She pushed out the parchment. "It's for you."

Zara took it. It was folded once. Inside, in thick, round, careful letters, with a great many lines under the important words, someone had written:

_CHEER UP. NETTLE SAYS HELLO._

Underneath was a drawing. It was a Knarl, drawn with remarkable accuracy, from the nose to the quills. It was sitting in a shed. It had a small paper hat on its head and a very small, very reluctant expression, as if it had been told to say something polite.

"It was Hagrid's idea," said Amara. "The hat."

"I like the hat."

"He didn't like it. He chewed it a bit."

"I can see that," said Zara. There was a small, damp dent in one corner of the page. She looked at it for longer than she needed to. Then, because there was a lump in her throat that she did not want anyone to notice, she folded the parchment and put it in her pocket, next to the Knarl spine, and said gruffly, "It was a good match."

"It was an _amazing_ match," said Amara, and grinned. "You were really good. I'd have won, if I was you."

"You'd have lost."

"I'd have _done something_," said Amara, in tones of the deepest conviction, and Mira made a small noise and turned it into a cough.

---

They walked back up to the castle together, the three of them, in the pale, glittering light, with the snow squeaking under their boots and the sound of the Ravenclaw celebrations coming faintly from the Great Hall. Amara walked in the middle, telling them in detail about Nettle's latest opinions on cheese. Mira said "Mm" at intervals, and "Really?", and once, "That's not generally considered to be a vegetable."

Zara listened, and nodded, and said nothing much.

She was thinking of a leaflet in a bin. She was thinking that on Wednesday, or perhaps Thursday, when it wasn't raw any more, she'd have a word with Jamie. A question, rather. She didn't know yet how she was going to ask it. She'd think of a way.

The cocoa in her cup had gone cold. She drank it anyway.
