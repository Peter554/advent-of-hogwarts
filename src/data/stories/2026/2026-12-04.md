The owl came at dinner, which meant it was from home.

It was a tawny with a bent left feather, who had been delivering the MacLeods' post to Hogwarts for four years and had developed strong views about the Hufflepuff table. It landed in the gravy. It held out its leg with dignity. Finn took the letter, apologised for the gravy, and gave it half a roll, and the owl ate the roll and left the dish exactly as it was.

"Your dad?" said Oliver, who was beside him moving a plate of shepherd's pie about as if it were a puzzle.

"Mm."

_Dear Finn,_ his father had written, in the big, slanting hand that always looked as if it were running to catch a bus.

_The orders for next September are coming in already, forty-one before the first of December, which your mother says is a record, and I say is because the Highlands have finally got a better class of baby. I've cleared the second bench in the back for July. You'll like it, it's by the window and the afternoon light comes in over the shavings like something out of a painting. I've been saving a length of rowan for you. Beautiful grain. I won't say what it's for._

_We're looking forward to Oliver at Hogmanay. Your mum has promised not to cook anything with a face._

_Love, Dad._

_P.S. The heating's gone again. Don't tell your mum I said so. She knows._

Finn read it twice, and smiled in the right places, and then he folded it very small and put it in his pocket with the Remembrall.

"What does he say?"

"He's saving some rowan for me," said Finn. "For July."

"That's lovely."

"Yeah," said Finn. "It is."

Oliver looked at him sideways for slightly too long, but Finn was looking down the hall, past the long tables and their four loud rows of chatter. The chair at the end of his own bench was still pulled out, a hand's breadth from the table, exactly as he'd left it on Tuesday night. Nobody had pushed it in. Nobody had sat in it. He didn't know which of those two things was worse.

At a quarter to eleven he took his lantern and a spare pair of socks and went out through the kitchens, where Dibby pressed a hot flask into his hands without a word, and across the dark grounds to the glasshouses.

---

"Bring a scarf," Professor Sprout had said, on Thursday, after Herbology, "and don't bring a hurry."

Finn had brought two scarves and, he hoped, no hurry. He stood in the doorway of Greenhouse Four at ten to midnight on Friday with the cold coming up through his boots and a lantern in one hand, and looked down the length of the long glass house to the last bed, where the frostblooms were waiting.

They didn't look like much, at first. Fifty or sixty low grey-green clumps in a bed of black soil, each topped with a tight bud the size of a thumbnail and the colour of a cloud. The glass over them was white with frost, so thick that the stars came through as a blur. It was very quiet, the way a room is before someone starts to sing.

"There you are," said Sprout.

She was kneeling at the end of the bed in a pair of fingerless gloves, a coat of patched green tweed and her usual hat, which had acquired a rim of frost. A flask stood by her knee. A small wooden trug sat beside it, lined with moss.

"Sorry, Professor. Am I late?"

"You're early, which is the same thing, only more tiresome." She patted the soil beside her. "Sit. Put the lantern down. They'll want to get used to it."

Finn set the lantern on the path. It lit the way it always did, at once, a steady gold, and a shine ran over the frozen glass and the dark soil and the ranks of grey buds. Sprout glanced at it.

"Flitwick's lanterns," she said. "I had one of those, you know. In my own fifth year."

"I thought they'd stopped doing them."

"They had, for a bit. I was one of the last." She screwed the top off the flask and poured something into the cap, and the smell that rose was nominally tea. "Mine was green, as you'd expect. A bit dull, I thought. It came out lovely on the night." She took a sip. "I don't ask what's in them, Mr MacLeod. It's rude. I just like to see them lit."

---

The first one opened at five past midnight.

It happened so quietly that Finn nearly missed it. The nearest bud, a grey knot at the edge of the bed, gave a small shiver, and then the tip unfolded, one petal and then another, until it stood open like a star. It was white at the edges and deep blue at the heart, and every petal was frosted, as if dipped in sugar.

As it opened, it made a faint sound, not quite a note: a spoon touched to the rim of a glass.

"Ah," said Sprout softly. "Now."

"Gently?"

"Gently. Hold the stem below the flower, here, with your thumb and finger, and don't pull. Ask."

"Ask?"

"You'd be surprised how many people try to take them." She had done it already, with a quiet turn of the wrist, and laid a white star in the trug. "They'll come if they want to. It's the same as a cat."

Finn put out his hand. He didn't think about what he was doing. He was good at this, and knew it, the way you know you can carry water without spilling it. His thumb and finger found the stem. He waited. There was a small, cold pause, like someone deciding to say yes, and the flower came free in his hand.

"Good," said Sprout.

They worked their way down the bed.

It was slow. That was the point. There was one bloom every ten minutes, give or take, and the waiting was most of the job. Sprout had said so to the Hufflepuff fifth years in September, in a lecture about patience, and the whole class had nodded and written it down and not one of them had understood. Finn was beginning to. If you reached too soon, or shifted your weight, or let a sour thought into your head, the bud closed like a fist, and you had to wait ten minutes for it to forgive you.

It was easier if he stopped trying to be useful. If he just sat on the cold path with the lantern beside him and let the glasshouse tick around him, one would open somewhere, with its small clear note, and he would be ready.

"They're for the Christmas Eve puddings," said Sprout, around half past twelve. "Sugared. The elves do it, usually, but I like to get the first picking myself. The petals take the sugar like nothing else. You've had them, I expect. Last year, on the trifle."

"The blue ones?"

"Those were the frostblooms. They taste of the first cold morning in October." She shifted on her knees. "The late ones are fussy, mind. Come the middle of the month you'll get the last of them, a different strain, and they won't open at all unless they've got light from four sides. Four lamps, one at every corner. Takes four people." She snorted. "I've done it with three, and a very patient Gnome."

"I'd help," said Finn, and was surprised to find he meant it.

"I know you would." She was looking at him with the level attention she gave a seedling she hadn't decided about yet. "That's rather what I wanted to talk to you about."

---

She waited for a bloom, first.

It opened at ten to one, out near the middle of the bed. It rang its little note. Sprout didn't pick it. She just looked at it, the way you might look at a view from a train, and then she said, without turning her head, "Do you know the Fenmoor Gardens?"

Finn thought. "In Cambridgeshire?"

"The fens, yes. Out past Ely. It's been a wizarding garden for three hundred years, and the Ministry would like you to believe it's been a conservation site for two hundred and fifty, but that's the Ministry for you. They grow things there that don't grow anywhere else. They've had a pair of Gurdyroots in the same bed since 1790, and a Mimbulus that nobody's dared to touch in decades." She picked up the flask, found it empty, and put it down again. "They take six students every summer. Six weeks, July and half of August. You live on site. You get your hands in the soil. You get taught by the people who actually do it, which is not at all the same as being taught by me."

Finn's heart had started to do something unhelpful.

"I put your name forward," Sprout said. "In October. Rather without asking. I'd have said sooner, but I wasn't sure they'd take a fifth year, and I didn't want to promise anything. They wrote on Tuesday." When Sprout was moved she got brisker. "They'd like you. They'd like to hear your answer by the middle of January, and I have the form in my pocket, and if you say anything polite at this point I'll give you a month of detention."

For a moment Finn couldn't say anything polite or otherwise.

"I—Professor, that's—" He took a breath. "I don't know what to say."

"That's a start."

"I didn't know you'd—" He stopped. His face had gone hot, in spite of the cold. "Thank you. Really. That's the best thing anyone's ever—" He heard himself and laughed, a little unsteadily. "I sound like a Puffskein."

"You sound like a boy who's been given something," said Sprout. "It's allowed."

He looked at the lantern. It was blazing, and the glass threw it back at them in a hundred small copies.

And then, as if somebody had put a cold hand on the back of his neck, he thought of July.

July was the wand shop. July was always the wand shop. It was the month the first-years' owls came, a flood of them, from every corner of Scotland: the lists, the orders, long careful letters from nervous parents asking whether it was too early. His dad did every wand himself. He'd done them for thirty years. He would stand at the long bench in the back with a lens in his eye and a curl of wood in his hand, and Finn had been there for as many of those Julys as he could remember, sweeping shavings, sorting cores, and learning, without ever being asked, to be the one who was _there_.

_I've cleared the second bench for you._ It was in his pocket, folded small, next to the Remembrall. He had read it at dinner and smiled at it, and he could not now think what he had been smiling at.

"It's a lot to think about," said Sprout. She was watching him. "Take your time. Talk to whoever needs talking to. I'm not going to push."

"I will," said Finn. "I just. I need to think."

"Of course."

"It's not that I don't want to."

"I know that."

"It's—" He didn't know how to finish. He didn't want to say the word _Dad_, because as soon as he did, it would become a problem, and as long as it stayed unsaid it was only a shining possibility with a small shadow behind it.

"Another one," said Sprout gently. "On your left."

He turned. A frostbloom had opened at his elbow while he wasn't looking. It stood in the lamplight with its frosted petals spread, ringing its little note into the cold, and had been doing so, he realised, for some time.

He put out his hand and asked.

---

They finished at two. The trug was heavy with white stars. Sprout counted them twice, a habit she'd had since 1971, and tucked a sprig of moss over the top, and then, as an afterthought, took one out again and set it in a small glass jar with a cork lid. She handed it across.

"One for the shift. Perk of the work. I'd suggest someone who'll appreciate it."

Finn took the jar. The bloom lay in it, humming very softly. It was the size of a coin, and left a ring of mist on the glass.

"Thank you, Professor."

"Thank _you_." She got stiffly to her feet, one hand on her knee, and for a moment she stood at the end of the bed, looking back along the rows of grey buds. "The best Herbologists I ever knew," she said, "were the ones who liked waiting. You like waiting, Mr MacLeod. Don't let anyone tell you it's a small thing."

She turned out the lamps. He took his lantern, and she didn't comment on it, and he didn't need her to.

---

The grounds were white and still. The stars over the Forbidden Forest were hard and many, the way they only are in the dead middle of a frost, and his boots made the only noise on the lawn. Behind him, the lights of the glasshouses went out one after another. Ahead, the castle stood up black against the sky with a few windows still lit.

He didn't hurry.

The Hufflepuff common room was dim and warm, the fire banked low under its grate. He took the stairs to the dormitory on tiptoe, and slid the door open on a darkness full of the breathing of four sleeping boys.

Oliver's bed was the second on the left. He was lying on his side with one arm flung over the pillow and his sandy hair standing straight up, and an open copy of _Mandrakes: A Practical Guide_ face down on his chest. In his sleep he looked about twelve.

Finn put the lantern on the floor, with its gold turned low, and took the cork out of the jar.

It was a small, silly gesture. He knew that. But he set the jar on Oliver's bedside table, very softly, next to the glass of water and a half-eaten biscuit and a flashcard that said _Mandrake: three uses_, and he sat on the edge of the bed in the dark, with his scarf still on, and watched.

The frostbloom stood a moment in the lamplight. Then, slowly, its petals folded one over another like a hand closing, and a faint, clear note sounded in the dark.

Oliver stirred. Without waking, he turned his head towards it and said, to nobody, "Stew."

Finn put the cork back in the jar.
