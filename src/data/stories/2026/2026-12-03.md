The potion was the colour of a pale lemon, and absolutely perfect.

Elliot knew it was perfect without needing to look at the book. It had gone clear the exact moment it should have done, and stayed clear, and now it sat in the cauldron in the cold stone light of the dungeon with a slow, silky shimmer on the surface, like the first light on a pond. An Elixir to Induce Euphoria, fifth-year standard. It had taken them forty minutes. They had stirred it seven times anticlockwise and once clockwise, and they hadn't thought about it once, any more than you think about folding a shirt.

"Outstanding," said Professor Slughorn, from behind them. He had a way of arriving at your shoulder that made it seem as though the room had rearranged itself to put him there. "Quite, quite outstanding. Look at that colour, Marcus, look at that. That's what we're aiming for."

Marcus, Gryffindor's second Chaser, at the next cauldron but one, looked at it and then at his own, which was a muddy grey and had begun to make a noise like a sink. "It's lovely," he said, with great sincerity. "I'd like to be that colour."

"Novak, have you thought of bottling it? The way you've got that stability. Extraordinary." Slughorn tapped the rim of the cauldron with one fat, pleased finger and moved on, humming.

"I hate you," Zara said quietly, from the other side. "I hate you _so much_."

Elliot glanced round. Her cauldron was a startling shade of khaki and smelled faintly of boiled cabbage. She was stirring it with a look of intense concentration, her braids tied up and her sleeves shoved above her elbows, and a streak of something green across her cheekbone that she plainly didn't know about.

"It's sulking," Elliot said.

"_I'm_ sulking. It's furious." She leaned over, scowling at their cauldron. "What did you do?"

"Nothing."

"You did something."

"I stirred it."

"How many times?"

"Eight."

"Seven and one," said Zara, who had been listening, and this made her laugh, a short bark of a laugh that she tried to turn into a cough. It was the most normal thing that had happened in about three weeks. Elliot felt something shift in them, very slightly, like a key turning a quarter of the way in a lock.

"Hogsmeade on Saturday," Zara said, still looking at her cauldron. "You going?"

"Probably," said Elliot. "Alone. With purpose."

"Cool. Me too. Alone. With purpose."

They both stirred.

"Great," said Elliot.

"Great," said Zara.

The bell rang. Zara's potion, as the class emptied, gave a small, damp pop and turned from khaki to a sort of hopeful beige. She looked at it in despair, and then, to Elliot's surprise, back at them. "Good luck with the careers thing," she said. "I've got mine tomorrow. Vance wants to talk to me about 'managing my talent', which I think is code for the captaincy." She hesitated. "Let me know how it goes."

"There's nothing to know," said Elliot.

"Sure," said Zara, and went.

---

Elliot arrived eleven minutes early for their careers meeting, because arriving early meant they could count things.

Slughorn's office was on the dungeon corridor, behind a door with a brass knocker in the shape of a pineapple. It had been left ajar and nobody was inside. The fire was lit. The room smelt of old leather, crystallised ginger and something faintly medicinal underneath, like a very expensive cough sweet. On the desk sat a silver dish in the shape of a pineapple, filled with actual pieces of pineapple, shining like amber glass.

Elliot sat down in the chair that looked safest and began.

There were photographs on every wall, hundreds of them, in plain frames and gilt ones, the figures inside all waving or smiling or nodding at nobody. They were Slughorn's old pupils, and he had arranged them with a collector's attention, by field and by seniority, in tidy columns. Elliot counted eleven potioneers, three Heads of Department at the Ministry, two Quidditch captains, one conductor, and a man who had evidently been photographed in the middle of a sneeze in 1964 and framed regardless.

They had reached the end of the third wall when a small cough made them look round.

"That chair," said Professor Slughorn, from the door, "has opinions."

"I'm sorry?"

"It's a very old chair." He came in sideways, with a tray, like a ship coming into a harbour that was slightly too small. He was a large, rosy, walrus-moustached man in a velvet jacket the colour of plums, and on the tray he had two cups of tea and a plate of ginger biscuits. "It tends to take against people who sit forward. You're sitting forward. Take the green one, Novak. The green one's easy. It was my mother's."

Elliot took the green one. It was, in fact, very comfortable. They suspected this was deliberate.

"Now," said Slughorn, settling into an armchair that creaked as if it was glad to see him. "O.W.L.s. Careers. A little chat. I always say it's the most important conversation of the fifth year, and I always say it with a biscuit." He held out the plate. "Go on."

---

For the first quarter of an hour it was all rather easy.

They went through the subjects. Potions was a given, said Slughorn, and Elliot let him say so. Charms, certainly. Transfiguration, good for the discipline. Defence, Herbology, Astronomy. He wrote them down on a long strip of parchment with a quill that signed itself at the bottom. He had a way of making each one sound like the opening of a door. He said "Outstanding, I should say, Outstanding," several times, with great satisfaction, and Elliot let that go by as well.

"And after," said Slughorn. "That is the question, isn't it? After. The N.E.W.T.s. And after _that_." He put down his cup. "Now. I won't hide it from you, my dear, I've been watching your cauldron work for four years, and I can tell you I haven't seen hands like yours since—well. Since your mother's."

"You taught my mother?"

"Oh, heavens, no. I _met_ her. At a conference in Salzburg, years ago. She gave a paper on stabilising unicorn-hair solutions that made grown wizards cry." He dabbed at his own eye, as though in sympathy. "So you see, I was bound to take an interest."

Elliot nodded. They were looking at the fire.

There was a pamphlet on the arm of Slughorn's chair, and he picked it up and passed it over. Then another, and a third. The Apothecaries' Guild. The Potions Department at St Mungo's. The Ministry's Office of Potions Regulation. They had glossy covers and pictures of smiling people in goggles, holding up vials against the light.

"These are all quite respectable," said Slughorn. "Some are more than respectable. I wrote to your mother this morning, by the way."

Elliot looked up. "You did what?"

"Only a note. Nothing to alarm you." He beamed. "I told her you were doing splendidly. And I asked whether she'd consider coming to give a masterclass to the fifth years on Saturday week. The twelfth. You can imagine what it'll mean to the others. A real, working Potions Master, not a stuffed old thing like me."

"That's generous," said Elliot. They were rather proud of how steady it came out.

"Not at all. The least I can do." He settled back. "So. What are you thinking? Healing? Research? The Guild? You needn't say today, of course, but a sense of direction, a general feeling—"

This was the moment.

Elliot had known it was coming. They had been preparing for it for most of November, in the way you prepare for a dentist, which is to say that they'd thought about it a good deal and rehearsed nothing. There was a sentence in their head. It was a short one. It began with _I_ and the next word was _draw_.

Their mouth was dry.

"I'm keeping my options open," said Elliot.

"Excellent!" Slughorn clapped his hands together, delighted. "Very wise. One should always keep one's options open. To the right ones."

And that was that, Elliot thought. They had said a thing, and he had heard another thing, and the two things were now standing side by side in the room like a pair of strangers at a bus stop.

---

"Anything else?" said Slughorn. "Any hobbies? I find it's always useful to know a pupil's hobbies."

"Duelling."

"Ah! Of course. The tournament. February, wasn't it? You came second in the juniors."

"I did."

"Marvellous. A splendid pastime. Every great potioneer should have one. Dear old Horace Slughorn took up croquet, and he was a menace." He chuckled. "Anything else? Reading? Chess?"

There was a pause. In it, Elliot's eye, which had been wandering, found a photograph on the far wall that they hadn't seen before. It hung all by itself near the bookcase, in a slender silver frame, and it showed a long table set for a party with a half-eaten cake and a chair pushed back from it. The chair was empty. Whoever had been in the photograph had, apparently, gone off to find something.

"Who was that?" they asked.

"Who? Oh." Slughorn followed their eyes. "Ignatius. He's still out of the frame. Gets bored, you see. He left in the middle of the sitting and he's never quite come back." He sounded fond. "Brilliant at Potions, absolutely brilliant. And then one summer he went off to make cheese in the Pyrenees. Happy as a Hippogriff. Writes to me every Christmas. I don't approve of the cheese, but I must say he looks very well on it."

It was a door, Elliot thought. It was a very small door, a little ajar, in the middle of a conversation about something else, and a more honest person would have put a foot in it. They opened their mouth.

"Professor," they said, "if someone were thinking of something that wasn't—"

"Mm?"

The clock on the mantelpiece chose that moment to strike. It had a round face and a bad temper and it was three minutes slow, and it announced the hour in a series of long groans, as though it were being asked to carry something heavy. Slughorn looked at it, then at his watch, then at the chair, and made a little noise of surprise.

"Goodness. My next. I've the third-year Slytherins at half past." He heaved himself up. "I'm sorry, my dear. You were saying?"

"It doesn't matter," said Elliot.

"Are you sure?"

"Quite sure."

"Well, if it comes to you." He pressed the three pamphlets into their hands and then, as an afterthought, a ginger biscuit. "Oh, and Novak. I do hold a little gathering on the eleventh. Friday week. Christmas, you know. A few friends, a few old pupils. You're invited, of course, and you must bring someone. Someone interesting."

"I'll ask around."

"Capital," said Slughorn, beaming, and ushered them out into the dungeon corridor.

---

The corridor was cold. After the office, with its fire and its pineapples and its hundreds of small waving strangers, it felt like walking out of a warm bath.

Elliot didn't go back upstairs. They went along the corridor to the first window, which was a deep narrow one set into the thickness of the wall, with a stone seat, and they sat down on it with their bag beside them and took out the sketchbook.

It was a ridiculous thing to do. They had a Transfiguration essay due. But the office was still in their head, the way a tune is, whole and complete, and they could feel it beginning to fade already; and if there was one thing Elliot had never learned to bear, it was seeing something vanish before they had put it down.

So they put it down.

The chair with opinions came first, with its crossed arms and its look of faint disapproval. Then the clock, three minutes slow, with the face of a person who has been asked to hold a heavy box and not told for how long. The wall of photographs, in tidy columns, small enough that it took them a moment to decide which waves to put in. The pineapple dish. Slughorn's own chair, sagging towards the fire. And in the very middle of it all, because it seemed to belong there, the little silver frame, with its table and its half-eaten cake and its empty seat.

They drew for most of an hour. Their hand knew what to do. It was the only part of the day where nothing had to be translated.

When they glanced down at the margin, there was something there that they hadn't intended to put. It was a door. It was very small, no bigger than a thumbnail, with a round top and a little black knob, and it had been drawn slightly open.

Elliot looked at it for a while.

Then they realised the window seat was warm, and that it wasn't the sun. A soft, low, amber light was coming up through the grey cloth of their bag, steady as a banked fire, and the lantern in the bottom of it, which had spent the whole of the previous day going out in front of people, was shining.

It lit the stone of the sill. It lit the ink on their fingers. It lit the side of the sketchbook, and the little door.

They didn't move. They hardly breathed. It was like finding a cat on your lap and not wanting to be the one to frighten it.

"Novak?"

It was a second-year Slytherin, one of the ones who sat at the end of the table and said very little, carrying a stack of books almost as tall as his head. He had stopped at the end of the corridor and was looking at them with the open, slightly worried face of the very young.

"Are you all right?"

"Fine," said Elliot. "Thank you."

He went on. But in the bottom of the bag the light had faltered, like a held breath, and gone out.

Elliot sat for a moment longer. Then they took the Potions pamphlets out of the pocket of their robe, the glossy one, the St Mungo's, the Guild's, and slid them between the pages of the sketchbook, over the drawing, over the door, and closed the book on them with a soft, flat sound.

They ate the ginger biscuit, in the end. It was a very good one.
