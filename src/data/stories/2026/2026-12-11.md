The robe was too big, and it was not Mira's.

It was plum-coloured velvet with a high collar and a row of small silver buttons, and it had belonged until Wednesday to Priya's cousin Anjali, who had worn it to a wedding in Leicester and been, by every account, the best-dressed person there. Mira had been inside it for nearly an hour. The sleeves came down over her knuckles and the hem trailed an inch on the floor. She had tried pinning it, and tucking it, and finally pushing the sleeves up to the elbow, where they stayed for about forty seconds before sliding down again, so that she moved along the dungeon corridor like a small, determined bell.

"You look wonderful," said Priya.

"I look like a curtain."

"A _very good_ curtain."

"I'm going to trip."

"You're not going to trip," said Priya, and took her hand, and Mira, in her borrowed velvet, with her hair in its best braid and a faint suspicion that she had been invited to a party whose rules everyone else had read, went through the door.

---

Slughorn's Christmas party was held, as it had been every year since nobody could remember when, in his own rooms, which had acquired a number of extra walls for the occasion.

The study where he held his careers meetings had been stretched into a long, candlelit hall with a vaulted ceiling and a black-and-white floor. A table ran down the middle, laid for thirty. Crystallised pineapples hung from the rafters on silver threads, glowing like small amber moons, and at the far end an ice sculpture of a cauldron dripped quietly into a dish. Somewhere above, a quartet that Mira could not see was playing something slow.

The guests stood about in clusters with glasses in their hands, wearing the bright, effortful look of people who have been told this is a Useful Evening. Mira recognised the sixth-year Quidditch captain, and a Hufflepuff who had once won a national essay prize. She did not recognise the very old wizard asleep in an armchair with a plate of cheese on his chest, or the woman in an emerald turban who was talking to a ghost.

"They all do something," Priya murmured. "That's the point. He collects them. He says it's for the school."

"And what do we do?"

"I'm the Arithmancy Challenge. You're my plus-one. I don't think there's a category."

"There's a category for _curtains_," said Mira, and was just beginning to feel that this might be fine when Professor Slughorn swept up between them, an arm for each, in pale gold silk with a pineapple embroidered on the pocket.

"My dears! At last! Come, come, come. We are going to find you your places."

---

The Placement Charm had been invented in 1974.

It was described on the card at each place setting, in a short and self-satisfied paragraph. _Seating by Affinity. The guest will find that, in the course of the evening, their chair is exchanged, by degrees and with perfect courtesy, for the chair of the person it will most profit them to meet. No offence is given. No offence need be taken._ Mira read it standing, with one hand on the back of a heavy oak chair, and found to her faint dismay that she liked it.

"I should explain," said Slughorn, who was moving round behind them like a large and happy tug. "One doesn't sit anywhere in particular. One sits _initially_ here, and then as the evening proceeds the cards, ah, drift. Quite harmless. Rather like a dance."

"What if you want to stay where you are?" said Mira.

"Then one has chosen to be a wallflower, my dear, and it is not for me to say." He beamed. "Sit, sit. The soup is coming."

She sat between the old wizard, who had woken and was regarding her with a mild, cloudy eye, and an empty chair. Priya was a few places down, opposite a stout man with a broken nose and a ring on every finger. The soup came. It was a good soup, somewhere between pumpkin and gold, and as Mira lifted her spoon she saw that her name card was moving.

It was not sliding fast. It moved with the dignified air of a card that had made up its mind, past the salt, past the bread, round a vase of holly, and came to rest in front of a chair six places down, where a small, brisk, silver-haired woman in a scarf had just sat. Mira's own chair, with a gentle, courteous shudder, followed.

"Oh," said Mira, relocated mid-spoonful.

The silver-haired woman looked round. She had a lined, sharp face like a clever bird's and a mass of white hair held up by a green silk scarf, and on the back of each hand, in faded blue, a row of tiny marks that Mira recognised with a jolt, because she had copied them out of a book. Runes. Tattooed a long time ago by someone with a steady hand.

"Sit down, dear, you look as though you've been picked up by a wave. It's the charm. He'll have decided I'm the most useful person in the room for you. Very flattering, and quite wrong. I'm retired." She held out one marked hand. "Hephzibah Lund. I used to run the runic archive at the Ministry. Now I mostly run my allotment."

"Mira Patel," said Mira, and took it. "I'm sorry. I'm not anyone."

"Nonsense, everyone's someone. What do you read?"

"Ancient Runes. And Arithmancy. And a, a number of things."

"A number of things! Good." She leaned in. "And what are you wondering about?"

It was, Mira would think afterwards, the question. It was so plain, and so unlike anything she had been asked at a party before, that her mind stopped and then restarted with a wrench, and the next thing she knew she was telling an eighty-year-old stranger, over soup, about the third-floor corridor.

---

She had been telling it for ten minutes when she saw Elliot.

She looked up in the middle of a sentence about bindrunes, and whether a rune's meaning shifts when it is drawn in one continuous line, and saw at the far end of the table a tall, familiar figure in a long dark jacket and a waistcoat of dull green silk, standing very straight beside an empty chair.

They had come alone. Slughorn's invitation had said _someone interesting_, and Elliot had told her in the dungeon corridor, with their usual dry composure, "I've brought myself. I'm told I'm interesting." Slughorn, delighted, had taken it as a compliment.

But Elliot wasn't looking at the table. They were looking at the door, which had opened at the end of the hall.

The woman who came through it was tall in the way that fills a doorway without trying. Forties, perhaps, with a sleek, jaw-length bob and a single streak of silver at the temple, a plain dark green dress, and a coat she held over one arm as though she had been asked to guard it. Her face was narrow and unsmiling. Her eyes were grey, and Mira knew them, with a shock like a stair that isn't there.

"Oh," said Mira, quietly.

"That's Lucia Novak," said Hephzibah, following her glance. "The potioneer. I've read her work. A monster of precision. Is she someone's?"

"I think," said Mira, "that she's Elliot's."

She did not know how she knew. She only saw that Elliot, at the far end of the table, had gone very still. She had seen that stillness before, on the Quidditch pitch, in Zara, in the half-second before a dive. It wasn't fear. It was the stillness of someone trying to take up as little room as possible to get through a moment in one piece.

The woman in green crossed the hall. It took her about fifteen seconds. She did not hurry and did not look at anyone else, and when she reached the chair she stopped, a yard away.

"Elliot," said Lucia Novak.

"Mama."

"You are taller."

"That tends to happen."

There was a very small pause. From six places away Mira couldn't hear what came next. She saw only that the woman put out a hand, stiffly, as though unsure of the protocol, and rested it on Elliot's arm. It stayed there a second. Then, with a movement so slight that Mira almost missed it, it moved up to the shoulder and held on.

It was the stiffest embrace in the world. It was also, unmistakably, an embrace.

And at that moment, with a clink of spoon on glass and a rumble of pleasure, Professor Slughorn rose at the head of the table, arms spread, gold silk shimmering, and bore down on the two of them like a ship under full sail.

"_Lucia!_ My dear, dear lady! You've come! Now, I must tell you what I've told the whole table, about the unicorn-hair paper, in Salzburg, you remember—"

Elliot's eyes went up the table and found hers.

It was a level, unreadable look, the look of someone requesting a very small piece of help without the faintest hope of being understood. Then Elliot did something that Mira would turn over for the rest of the evening. They stepped back, very slightly, and held out their glass.

Elderflower punch, in a tall frosted glass. Full. Untouched.

She had moved before she had decided to.

---

She had no idea what she was going to say. She had the time it took to cross the hall: to push back her chair, gather her sleeves and say "Excuse me" to Hephzibah, who looked at her once and nodded as if it had all been arranged. Halfway across, she remembered the thing in the book.

"Professor Slughorn!"

He stopped, arms still open. "Miss Patel?"

"I'm so sorry to interrupt. I know it's a party. It's only that I've been wondering all evening, and I think you're the only person here who'd know." She took the glass from Elliot's hand, quite smoothly. It was cold. "It's about runic potions."

"Runic—"

"I mean the inscription on the cauldron base. Whether it affects heat transfer. I know it's a strange question." She was holding the glass in both hands, in front of her, like a candle. "I've been reading about the Beaumont theorem, and there's a school of thought that the _direction_ of the rune matters, clockwise or anticlockwise, and I wasn't sure, and I thought, well, you've got a famous cauldron."

Slughorn's face did several things. It began in faint puzzlement, passed through dawning respect, and ended in a look of such unaffected personal joy that she knew at once she had made the most successful move of her life.

"_My dear girl_. Do you know, nobody has asked me that in thirty years."

"Haven't they?"

"Come here. Come, sit. No, don't move the glass, put it down, put it _there_—" He had her by the arm and was steering her to the end of the table, and she heard herself saying "Of course, of course," and she caught, as she was borne away, two things: the back of a tall woman in green going quietly through a side door with a dark-jacketed figure at her shoulder; and, down the table, Priya, who raised her eyebrows a very little, then got up and came to join them with a plate of cheese and a question about the Beaumont theorem so detailed that Slughorn actually sat down.

---

It took twenty-five minutes.

It was the longest and most thorough conversation about the thermal conductivity of runic copper that anyone had ever had at a Christmas party. They were joined by Hephzibah (who had a view), a tall curse-breaker from Gringotts named Mr Khan (who had another), and the very old wizard, who woke apropos of nothing to announce that in his day they had done it with salt. It was warm and noisy and confusing, and at some point Mira found, to her surprise, that she was enjoying herself.

"You must write to me," Hephzibah said at the end, while Slughorn was off fetching a leather-bound book from a shelf. She had taken Mira's hand and turned it over in the candlelight, as one might examine a piece of cloth. "I mean it. I've got a trunk in the spare room with a couple of hundred pages of eleventh-century bindrune sketches that nobody has looked at since the war, and I'm too old to do them justice. I'd rather it was someone who asked about the direction."

"I'd—" Mira stopped. Her heart was doing something odd. "I'd love to."

"Good." She let go. "I shan't be offended if you don't."

"I'll write. I promise."

For three months Mira had been looking for a door. She had a notebook with fourteen possibilities in it, each on its own page, with pros and cons, and not one had ever said _yes_. And here, on a Friday evening, in a borrowed robe, at a party she hadn't wanted to attend, somebody had looked at her hand and asked her to come and help.

It was the first time a career had gone after her, and not the other way round.

---

They left at nine, by the usual method, which was to look at each other across a crowded room until the same idea occurred to both of them, then walk out of the door in opposite directions and meet in the corridor. Priya was already there, leaning against the wall with her arms folded.

"Well," she said.

"Well."

"I think you were the most useful person in the room."

"I think the Placement Charm needs a cooling-off period," said Mira. "I'm not sure about the long-term effects."

They went up the stairs together, past the empty Entrance Hall, with the quartet fading behind them, and took the side passage to a flagged corridor lined with barrels, where a painting of a fruit bowl hung at the end. Priya tickled the pear. It giggled. The door swung open on a warmth that smelled of a hundred kinds of hot butter.

Dibby was at the near table in a tea-cosy, directing a team of elves with a spoon. She turned at the sound of the door, looked at them, and her ears did something complicated.

"Miss Patel. Miss Desai. At this hour. You have not had enough to eat."

"We had soup," said Priya.

"Soup is not eating."

"We'd like some chips," said Mira. "If it's not a bother."

"It is never a bother," said Dibby, with a sort of terrible gentleness, and vanished.

They sat on the stone steps down to the Hufflepuff corridor, with a cold draught from the passage on one side and the glow of the kitchen on the other. The chips came in a paper cone the size of a hat, with salt and vinegar and a small silver pot of something red. Mira ate the first with her fingers. It was hot enough to hurt. It was the best thing she had ever eaten.

"I slept for eight hours last night," said Priya, after a while. "I wanted you to know. And seven on Wednesday."

"Oh, _good_."

"It tastes of mint. All week. I think I've started to taste it all the time." She held a chip against the light. "I haven't solved the third problem. But I've decided it can wait until the new year."

"That's very sensible."

"Is it?" Priya looked at her sideways. "It doesn't feel like me. But it's quite nice."

A scullery elf went past with a pile of plates and nodded. Far above, someone was playing a piano, badly, in what sounded like a ballroom.

"Do you think Elliot's all right?" said Mira.

"I think they've got a lot to carry."

"I wish I'd known what to do."

"You asked a very hard question at a very awkward time," said Priya, "and then you took the glass. I think that's rather a lot." She put her head on Mira's shoulder, briefly, took it off again, and reached for the vinegar. "You're doing it, you know. You're making a list."

"I'm not."

"What's number one?"

"Find out what's happened," said Mira, slowly. "Number two. Don't ask."

"There you go. That's wisdom."

"That's _contradictory_."

"It's both," said Priya, comfortably, and licked a finger.

The robe was ruined. Mira discovered this a few minutes later, when she looked down at her lap. A large, glistening stain in the shape of Africa was spreading across the velvet from knee to hip, where she had put the cone. It was the colour of a wet plum and smelled faintly of vinegar.

"Oh no," she said. "Your cousin's—"

"Anjali will survive."

"She'll _kill_ me."

"She'll say it was the best thing that ever happened to that robe," said Priya. "She's a very particular sort of person."

They looked at each other. And then, because there was nothing else to be done about it, they began to laugh, quietly, in the half-dark on the cold stone step, with their feet at the edge of the warm light and the last of the chips between them.
