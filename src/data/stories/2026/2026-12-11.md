The robe was too big, and it was not Mira's.

It was made of heavy plum-coloured velvet, with a high collar and a row of small silver buttons, and it had belonged until Wednesday to Priya's cousin Anjali, who had worn it to a wedding in Leicester and been, by every account, the best-dressed person there. Mira had been inside it for nearly an hour. The sleeves came down over her knuckles. The hem trailed an inch on the floor. She had tried pinning it, and tucking it, and finally pushing the sleeves up to the elbow, where they stayed for about forty seconds before sliding down again, so that she moved through the dungeon corridor in the manner of a small, determined bell.

"You look wonderful," said Priya.

"I look like a curtain."

"A _very good_ curtain."

"I'm going to trip."

"You're not going to trip," said Priya, and took her hand, and Mira, in her borrowed velvet, with her hair in its best braid and a faint creeping sense that she'd been asked to a party where she was the only person who had not read the rules, went through the door.

---

Slughorn's Christmas party was held, as it had been every year since a date that nobody could quite remember, in his own rooms, and his rooms had, for the occasion, acquired a number of extra walls.

It was the only way to describe it. The study where he held his careers meetings had been stretched, by some art of his own, into a long, high, candlelit hall, with a vaulted ceiling that disappeared into shadow and a floor of black-and-white stone, and down the middle, running the whole length of it, a table. It was laid for thirty. Candles floated above it in clusters. Crystallised pineapples hung on silver threads from the rafters, glowing from within like small amber moons, and at the far end an enormous ice sculpture of a cauldron was quietly dripping into a dish.

There was music. Somewhere above, invisible, a quartet of what sounded like violins was playing something slow. There were people. There were a great many people, in a great many sorts of robe, standing about in clusters with glasses in their hands and talking, with the particular, bright, effortful look of those who have been told that this is a Useful Evening. Mira recognised the sixth-year Quidditch captain. She recognised a Hufflepuff who had once won a national essay prize. She did not recognise the very old wizard in the corner, who seemed to be asleep in an armchair with a plate of cheese on his chest, nor the tall, lean woman in an emerald turban who was talking to a ghost.

"They all do something," Priya murmured. "That's the point. He collects them. He says it's for the school."

"And what do we do?"

"I'm the Arithmancy Challenge," said Priya, in a low, wry voice. "You're my plus-one. I don't think there's a category."

"There's a category for _curtains_," said Mira, and was just beginning to feel that this might, in fact, be fine, when Professor Slughorn swept up between them, with an arm for each, in a robe of pale gold silk with a pineapple embroidered on the pocket, and said, "My dears! At last! Come, come, come. We are going to find you your places."

---

The Placement Charm had been invented in 1974.

It was described, on the card that lay at each place setting, in a short and rather self-satisfied paragraph. _Seating by Affinity. The guest will find that, in the course of the evening, their chair is exchanged, by degrees and with perfect courtesy, for the chair of the person it will most profit them to meet. No offence is given. No offence need be taken._ Mira read it standing at the table, with her hand on the back of a heavy oak chair, and found, to her faint dismay, that she liked it.

"I should explain," said Slughorn, who was moving round behind them like a very large and happy tug. "One doesn't sit anywhere in particular. One sits _initially_ here, as you see, and then as the evening proceeds the cards, ah, drift. It's quite harmless. It's rather like a dance."

"What if you want to stay where you are?" said Mira.

"Then one has chosen to be a wallflower, my dear, and it is not for me to say." He beamed. "Sit, sit. The soup is coming."

She sat. She was between the very old wizard with the cheese, who had woken and was regarding her with a mild, kind, cloudy eye, and an empty chair. Priya was at the next place down, across from a stout man with a broken nose and a ring on every finger.

The soup came. It was a beautiful soup. It was somewhere between pumpkin and gold.

And as she lifted her spoon, Mira looked down, and her name card, a small white rectangle on a silver stand, was moving.

It was not sliding fast. It was moving with the dignified, unhurried air of a card that had made up its mind. It crossed the table, past the salt, past the bread, round the end of a vase of holly, and came to rest in front of a chair that had until that moment been empty, six places down, where a small, brisk, silver-haired woman in a scarf had just sat. The chair next to it had been pulled out. Mira's own chair, with a gentle, courteous shudder, followed.

"Oh," said Mira, and found herself, mid-spoonful, relocated.

The silver-haired woman looked round at her.

She had a lined, sharp, delighted face, like a clever bird, and a mass of white hair held up by a green silk scarf, and on the back of each of her hands, in faded blue, a pattern of tiny marks that Mira recognised, with a jolt, because she'd copied them out of a book. They were runes. They'd been tattooed, a long time ago, in a careful, practised hand, in a row.

"Sit down, dear," said the woman, "you look like a person who's been picked up by a wave. It's the charm. He'll have decided I'm the most useful person in the room for you. It's very flattering. And entirely wrong. I'm retired." She held out one of the marked hands. "Hephzibah Lund. I used to run the runic archive at the Ministry. Now I mostly run my allotment."

"Mira Patel," said Mira, and took it. "I'm sorry. I'm—I'm not anyone."

"Nonsense, everyone's someone. What do you read?"

"Ancient Runes. And Arithmancy. And a, a number of things."

"A number of things! Good." She leaned in. "And what are you wondering about?"

It was, Mira would think afterwards, the question. It was so plain, and so kind, and so unlike anything she had been asked at a party before, that her mind simply stopped, and then started again with a wrench, and the next thing she knew she was telling an eighty-year-old stranger, over soup, about the third-floor corridor.

---

She'd been telling it for ten minutes when she saw Elliot.

She didn't see them arrive. She only looked up, in the middle of a sentence about bindrunes and the problem of whether a rune's meaning shifts when it's drawn in a continuous line, and saw, at the far end of the table, a tall, lean, familiar figure, in a long dark jacket and a waistcoat of dull green silk, with a silver chain at the pocket, standing very straight by an empty chair.

They hadn't brought anyone. Slughorn's tip had said _someone interesting_. Elliot had told her, in the dungeon corridor, with their usual dry composure, "I've brought myself. I'm told I'm interesting." And Slughorn, who had been delighted, had taken it as a compliment.

But Elliot wasn't looking at the table. They were looking at the door.

It had opened, at the end of the hall, and somebody had come in.

She was tall. That was what Mira noticed first: the sort of tall that fills a doorway without apparently trying to. She was in her forties, perhaps, with a sleek, dark, jaw-length bob that had a single streak of silver at the temple, and a long, plain, dark green dress, and a coat that she'd taken off and was holding over one arm as though it were something she'd been asked to guard. Her face was narrow and pale and unsmiling. She had, Mira saw, with a shock like a step that isn't there, a pair of grey eyes that she knew.

"Oh," said Mira, quietly.

"That's Lucia Novak," said Hephzibah, following her glance. "The potioneer. I've read her work. A monster of precision. Is she someone's?"

"I think," said Mira, "that she's Elliot's."

She did not know how she knew. She only knew, as she watched, that the whole of Elliot, at the far end of the long table, had gone very still. It was a stillness she'd seen before, on the Quidditch pitch, in Zara, in the half-second before a dive. It wasn't fear. It was the stillness of a person trying to take up as little room as possible, so as to get through a moment in one piece.

The woman in the green dress crossed the hall. It took her about fifteen seconds. She didn't hurry, and she didn't look at anyone else, and when she reached the chair, she stopped.

They stood, for a moment, a yard apart.

"Elliot," said Lucia Novak.

"Mama."

"You are taller."

"That tends to happen."

There was a very small pause. Mira, from six places away, couldn't hear what came next; only saw that the woman put out a hand, stiffly, formally, as if she were not sure of the protocol, and rested it on Elliot's arm. It lasted a second. And then, with a movement so slight and unexpected that Mira would almost have missed it, she moved it up, to the shoulder, and held on.

It was the stiffest embrace in the world. It was also, unmistakably, an embrace.

And at that precise moment, from the head of the table, with a clink of his spoon on his glass and a great rich rumble of pleasure, Professor Slughorn rose, with his arms spread wide and his gold silk shimmering, and began to bear down on the two of them like a ship under full sail.

"_Lucia!_" he cried. "My dear, dear lady! You've come! Now, now, I must tell you what I've told the whole of the table, about the unicorn-hair paper, in Salzburg, you remember—"

Elliot's eyes went up the table. They found hers.

It was nothing. It was a tiny, level, entirely unreadable look, the look of someone requesting, without the faintest hope of its being understood, a very small piece of help. And then Elliot, without a word, did something that Mira would turn over in her mind for the rest of the evening. They stepped back, very slightly, and held out their glass.

It was a tall, frosted glass of elderflower punch. It was full. They hadn't touched it.

She'd moved before she had decided to.

---

She had no idea what she was going to say. She had a couple of seconds to find it, in the time that it took to get across the hall: to push back her chair, and gather her too-long sleeves, and say "Excuse me" to Hephzibah, who looked at her once, and nodded as if it had all been arranged. She had the sensation of falling very slowly down a long flight of stairs, and just as she reached the bottom she remembered the thing in the book.

"Professor Slughorn!"

He'd stopped. He turned, with his arms still open. "Miss Patel?"

"I'm so sorry to interrupt. I know it's a party. It's only that I've been wondering all evening, and I think you're the only person here who'd know." She took the glass from Elliot's hand. She did it quite smoothly. It was cold. "It's about runic potions."

"Runic—"

"I mean, the inscription on the cauldron base. Whether it affects the heat transfer. I know it's a strange question." She had got her hand round the glass, and was holding it, in both of hers, in front of her, like a candle. "It's only that I've been reading about the Beaumont theorem, and there's a school of thought that says the _direction_ of the rune matters, clockwise or anticlockwise, and I wasn't sure, and I thought—well, you've got a famous cauldron."

There was a pause.

Professor Slughorn's face did a number of things. It began, at the top, in an expression of faint puzzlement. It passed, through the middle, a stage of what looked like dawning respect. And it ended, at the bottom, in a look of such deep, unaffected, personal joy that she knew at once she'd made the single most successful move of her life.

"_My dear girl_," he said. "Do you know, nobody has asked me that in thirty years."

"Haven't they?"

"Come here. Come here, sit. No, don't move the glass, put it down, put it _there_—" He had her by the arm, and was guiding her to the end of the table, and she heard herself say "Of course, of course," and she caught, out of the corner of her eye, as she was borne away, two things: the back of a tall woman in green, going quietly through a side door with a tall, dark-jacketed figure at her shoulder; and, further down the table, the sharp, dark, level gaze of Priya, who raised her eyebrows a very little, and then, without a word, got up and came to join them, with a plate of cheese and a question about the Beaumont theorem so detailed that Slughorn actually sat down.

---

It took twenty-five minutes.

It was the longest and most thorough conversation about the thermal conductivity of runic copper that anyone had ever had at a Christmas party. They were joined, by degrees, by Hephzibah Lund (who had a view), and a tall curse-breaker from Gringotts named Mr Khan (who had another), and the very old wizard with the cheese, who woke up, apropos of nothing, in the middle of the discussion and announced that in his day they had done it with salt. It was warm, and noisy, and confusing, and at some point Mira found, to her great surprise, that she was enjoying herself.

"You must write to me," Hephzibah said, at the end of it, while Slughorn was off fetching a particular, enormous, leather-bound book from a shelf. She'd taken Mira's hand, and turned it over, and was looking at it in the candlelight, as one might look at a piece of cloth. "I mean it. I've got a trunk. It's in the spare room. It's got a couple of hundred pages of bindrune sketches from the eleventh century that nobody's looked at since the war, and I'm too old to do them justice. I'd like someone to have a look. I'd rather it was someone who asked about the direction."

"I'd—" Mira stopped. Her heart was doing something odd. "I'd love to."

"Good." She let go. "I shan't be offended if you don't."

"I'll write," said Mira. "I promise."

She did not, in that moment, quite know what had happened. She'd been, for the past three months, a person looking for a door: she had a notebook with fourteen possibilities in it, each with its own page, and a long list of pros and cons, and none of them had ever once said _yes_. And here, on a Friday evening, in a borrowed robe, at a party she hadn't wanted to go to, someone had looked at her hand and asked her to come and help.

It was the first time in her life she'd been hunted by a career, and not the other way round.

---

They left at nine.

They did it by the usual method, which was to look at each other across a crowded room until the same idea occurred to both, and then to walk out of the door in opposite directions and meet in the corridor. Priya was already there, leaning against the wall with her arms folded and the faintest of smiles.

"Well," she said.

"Well."

"I think you were the most useful person in the room."

"I think the Placement Charm needs a cooling-off period," said Mira. "I'm not sure about the long-term effects."

They went up the stairs together, past the dungeon corridor and the empty Entrance Hall, with the sound of the quartet fading behind them, and took the little side passage, round the back, to a long flagged corridor lined with barrels, where a painting of a fruit bowl was hanging at the end. Priya tickled the pear. It giggled. The door swung open on a warmth that smelled like a hundred different kinds of hot butter.

Dibby was at the near table, in a tea-cosy, directing a team of elves with a spoon, and she turned at the sound of the door, and looked at them, and her ears did something complicated.

"Miss Patel," she said. "Miss Desai. At this hour. You have not had enough to eat."

"We had soup," said Priya.

"Soup is not eating."

"We'd like some chips," said Mira. "If it's not a bother."

"It is never a bother," said Dibby, with a sort of terrible gentleness, and vanished.

They sat on the stone steps that led down to the Hufflepuff corridor, in the half-dark, with a cold draught from the passage on one side and a warm glow from the kitchen on the other. The chips arrived in a paper cone, as big as a hat, with salt and vinegar and a small silver pot of something red. Mira ate the first one with her fingers. It was hot enough to hurt. It was the best thing she had ever eaten.

"I slept for eight hours last night," said Priya, after a while. "I wanted you to know. And seven on Wednesday."

"Oh, _good_."

"It tastes of mint. All week. I think I've started to taste it all the time." She was holding a chip against the light. "I haven't solved the third problem. But I've decided it can wait until the new year."

"That's very sensible."

"Is it?" Priya looked at her sideways. "I don't know. It doesn't feel like me. But it's quite nice."

They sat. A scullery elf went past, with a pile of plates, and nodded. Far away, on the floor above, someone was playing the piano, badly, in what sounded like a ballroom.

"Do you think Elliot's all right?" said Mira.

"I think they've got a lot to carry."

"I wish I'd known what to do."

"You gave them a very hard question to answer at a very awkward time," said Priya, "and then you took the glass. I think that's rather a lot." She put her head on Mira's shoulder, briefly, and took it off again, and reached for the vinegar. "You're doing it, you know. Whatever you're thinking. You're making a list."

"I'm not."

"What's number one?"

"Find out what's happened," said Mira, slowly. "Number two. Don't ask."

"There you go. That's wisdom."

"That's _contradictory_."

"It's both," said Priya, comfortably, and licked a finger.

The robe was ruined. Mira discovered this, quite casually, a few minutes later, when she looked down at her lap: a large, translucent, glistening stain, the shape of a map of Africa, was spreading slowly across the velvet from knee to hip, where she'd put the cone. It was the colour of a wet plum. It had a faint smell of vinegar.

"Oh no," she said. "Your cousin's—"

"Anjali will survive."

"She'll _kill_ me."

"She'll say it was the best thing that ever happened to that robe," said Priya. "She's a very particular sort of person."

They looked at each other. And then, because there was nothing else to be done about it, they began to laugh, both of them, quietly, in the half-dark, on the cold stone step, with their feet on the edge of the warm light and the last of the chips between them.
