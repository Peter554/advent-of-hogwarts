The note arrived at breakfast, folded into a tight square and delivered by a barn owl with the expression of someone who had been made to fly before nine.

Zara Okonkwo read it once, sitting very still, with a piece of toast halfway to her mouth.

_Miss Okonkwo, it began, in Professor Sprout's square, no-nonsense hand. I am sorry to trouble you, but I would be grateful for your help. Your sister has something in the first-year Hufflepuff dormitory that she should not have, and she will not tell me what. Two of her dormitory-mates have asked me to come and "see for myself". I would prefer to ask you first, since I understand she talks about you constantly. —P. Sprout_

"She talks about you constantly," Zara read aloud.

"Does she?" said Jamie, across the table. Jamie was Gryffindor's Seeker and not a person who wasted words; she was eating a boiled egg with the focus of someone defusing it. "I thought she'd moved her seat."

"She hasn't moved her seat."

"She sits at the far end of Hufflepuff, facing the wall."

"That's because of the light."

Jamie raised one eyebrow and went back to her egg.

Zara folded the note into a smaller square, and then into a smaller one still. The thing was, she had _promised_. Mum had said it on the platform in September, with a hand on Zara's shoulder and one eye on Amara, who was already halfway up the steps of the train arguing with a conductor about a toad. _Keep an eye on her._ And Zara had kept an eye on her. She had kept an eye on her at every meal, in every corridor, across the entire Great Hall; she had kept it on her so hard that three weeks ago Amara had stopped saying hello.

Something fizzed against her hip. She glanced down. Her lantern was in her bag again (she hadn't dared leave it in the dormitory, after the thing with Katie's pillow), and it was coughing sparks through the flap, one of which landed on Jamie's toast.

"Sorry."

"It's fine," said Jamie, brushing the toast. "It's _charred_ now. Very fashionable." She nodded at the note. "Go on. Go and be a sister."

"I'm not going to do anything. I'm going to see."

"Mm," said Jamie.

---

The tin was under her arm by the time she got to the corridor by the kitchens.

It was a round tin of chin chin in a drawstring cloth bag, her grandmother's, flown over from Lagos in a hold-all and sent on by Mum with a note that said _for the two of you_. Zara had been saving it. She wasn't sure what for, exactly. But she had found, in the last few weeks, that if you walked into a room with something to hand over, it was easier to say the next thing. Sweets made a speech go down.

She had rehearsed the speech all the way from the Great Hall. _Amara. I'm not here to tell you off. I'm here to help. Whatever it is, we'll sort it out. Together._ It had sounded good on the stairs. Now, outside the stack of barrels where the Hufflepuff entrance hid, it sounded as if it might, at some point, have to be said to a person.

She tapped the barrel. Two, one, two, the rhythm that Finn had once drilled into her, with great seriousness, against the day she might have to rescue him. The lid swung back and she wriggled through the tunnel on her hands and knees.

The Hufflepuff common room was warm and round and smelt of baking, with a low ceiling and sunny little windows that looked out at the roots of the grass. A fifth-year boy was kneeling in the middle of the floor with a mug in one hand and a Herbology textbook in the other. He looked up.

"Oh," said Oliver Fenwick. "It's you. Sprout said you might be coming."

"Is it bad?"

"I wouldn't say bad." Oliver considered. "I'd say it's got spikes." He nodded towards a round door in the far wall. "Third on the left. Mind the milk."

"What milk?"

"You'll see," said Oliver, and went back to his book.

---

The door to the first-year dormitory was ajar, and a very small girl was standing just behind it with her arms folded, as though she had been put there to guard it. She stood aside when she saw Zara and said, in a whisper, "She's on the bed. Please don't shout."

"I'm not going to shout."

"Everyone says that," said the very small girl, darkly, "and then they shout."

The room was round, like all the Hufflepuff rooms, with five beds under the curved ceiling and a patchwork quilt on each. Four were tidy. The fifth, under the window at the far end, was surrounded by a barricade of jumpers.

There were jumpers on the bed. There were jumpers on the floor. There was a heap of jumpers on the pillow, rolled and tucked and moulded into a nest as round and deep as a bird's, and in the middle of the nest, on her stomach with her chin on her fists, lay Amara.

She was eleven years old. She was wearing her school robes inside out. A creature-card of a Bowtruckle had slipped out of her pocket and she hadn't noticed. Her hair was in two puffs, one slightly higher than the other, and the whole of her face, which was round and bright and generally ready to argue, had gone completely soft.

She was singing, very quietly, in Igbo. It was the song their grandmother used to sing in the kitchen in Lagos, when the rice was nearly done and she was tired and pleased.

And in the middle of the nest, between the heaps of wool, sat something about the size of a loaf of bread.

It was brown, and round, and covered from nose to tail in spines, each as long as a finger and tipped with a dull sheen like old pewter. It had a blunt, sad, wrinkled snout. It had very small, very black eyes, which were fixed on Zara with a look of the most profound and personal suspicion.

"Amara," Zara said.

Amara's song stopped. She didn't turn round. "I knew you'd come."

"Sprout sent a note."

"I _knew_ she would." Amara rolled onto her side. "I'm not doing anything wrong."

"I didn't say you were."

"You've got your face on."

"I haven't got a face on."

"You've got your captain face."

"That," Zara said, "is just my face." But she lowered it, a little, and sat on the end of the next bed, and put the tin on her knees. "What is it?"

"He's a Knarl."

"A _Knarl_?"

"Don't say it like that. His name's Nettle."

---

It took ten minutes to get the story, and it came out in the way that Amara's stories did: all at once, out of order, and with a number of important facts concerning the diet of the creature. A Knarl was a sort of hedgehog, said Amara, except that it wasn't, and the difference was important. They were shy and proud and easily offended. They had been getting a bad name for centuries because people kept mistaking them for hedgehogs and putting out milk, and a Knarl _hated_ milk, and if you gave it some it assumed you were laughing at it, and fired its spines at you out of sheer dignity. Which was exactly what had happened to Lottie's pillow.

"We found him in the grass by the greenhouses," Amara said. "On Saturday. He had a bit of fishing line caught round one leg. I was only going to take it off and put him back."

"And then?"

"And then he wouldn't go."

Zara looked at Nettle. Nettle looked at Zara. In the bleak little black eyes she thought she recognised something, the expression of a person who has been made to stay in a place for reasons they don't altogether understand, and is determined not to give anyone the satisfaction.

"He's been here four days," said Amara. "He doesn't like noise. He doesn't like Lottie. He likes me, because I don't hover."

Zara opened her mouth. Closed it.

She thought of Mum on the platform. She thought of the speech she had rehearsed. She thought, with a rush of the plain, simple energy that always got her through the first ten minutes of a match, _I can fix this_.

"Right," she said. "Okay. First thing, this is going to be a problem, because Sprout knows. So you'll have to take him back to the grounds. I'll come with you. I'll tell Hagrid. We can say we found him this morning." She was on her feet. "And you need to clear this up, you can't leave all this wool on the floor, it's a fire hazard—"

"Zara—"

"—and there's a saucer under the bed, that's got milk in it, Amara, honestly, I told you what Hagrid said—"

"_Zara_."

She had picked up the saucer. She had picked up an armful of the nearest jumpers. And in the nest, with a small dry sound like a pack of cards being riffled, every spine on Nettle's back stood straight up.

Three of them went into Zara's sleeve.

"Ow," she said. "Right. That's—ow."

"Put it down," said Amara.

She said it quietly. She had sat up. In her robes put on inside out, with her small round chin up, she looked all at once, and for the first time that Zara could remember, exactly like their mother when she'd finished listening.

"I'm fine, Zara. I'm not your project."

The very small girl at the door made a little noise like a hiccup and went away.

"I'm just trying to look after you," Zara said.

It sounded different out loud. On the stairs it had been a promise. In the round room, with a Knarl and an armful of other people's jumpers, it sounded like what it was, a thing she had said so many times that she had stopped checking whether it was true.

Neither of them said anything. Outside, from a long way off, somebody was practising a scale on a recorder.

---

It was Hagrid who ended it, in the way that he ended most things, by arriving.

"Amara? Zara? Yeh in there? Professor Sprout said—" The door was too small for him, and the rest of the sentence came in a series of grunts as he got himself through it sideways, with Professor Sprout behind him holding a flowerpot, as a precaution. "Yeh've got a _what_? Yeh never! Let's see him. No, don't mind me, I'll stay down here."

He lowered himself onto the floor, which was an operation in several stages, and lay on his stomach at the foot of the nest, with his chin on the boards and his enormous beard fanned out beside him like a rug. For a moment nothing happened. Then Nettle took three small steps down the jumper slope, and sniffed Hagrid's nose.

"Well, I'll be," Hagrid said, very softly. "Look at the state o' yeh. Yeh've been livin' on cheese rind, I'd say. An' fishing line." He glanced up, and the whole of his huge face was shining. "Amara, he's a beauty. How did yeh get him ter stay?"

"I didn't do anything," said Amara. "I didn't go near him for two days."

"Quite right," said Hagrid. "That's the trick of it. Yeh've got ter wait till he comes ter you."

Zara, at the end of the next bed, held very still.

Sprout looked at the Knarl, and then at the jumpers, and then, over her glasses, at the girl in the middle of the nest, and Zara watched her decide not to be cross. "I think," said Professor Sprout, "that the first-year dormitory is not the proper place for a Knarl."

"No, Professor."

"But I think a shed would be."

"A _shed_?" said Amara.

"There's one behind me hut," Hagrid said. "Nice an' dry. Knows how ter keep the cold out. He can have it for the winter, if yeh like, an' yeh can come an' see him every day. After lessons. Between you an' me, I'd rather like the company."

Amara's face did something Zara hadn't seen it do since the end of August. It lit up from underneath, the way a lantern does.

"Every day?"

"Every single one."

"Can I bring him jumpers?"

"He can have as many jumpers as he can sit on," said Hagrid gravely.

Amara flung her arms round Hagrid's neck, which given the position he was in was a feat of some skill. Then she stopped, and looked sideways at the end of the next bed, where her sister was sitting with three spines in her sleeve and an unopened tin on her knee.

"Good job," said Zara. It came out gruffer than she'd meant. She cleared her throat. "Really. Good job."

"Thanks," Amara said, a little warily.

And then, quick and hard, before Zara could do anything about it, she crossed the room and hugged her. It lasted about one second, the way a Snitch is visible for about one second, and then she was gone, back to Hagrid and the shed and the Knarl.

---

Zara stayed where she was for a bit.

She pulled the spines out of her sleeve one at a time and laid them on the quilt. They were beautiful, in their way, banded with grey and brown like something you might find on a seashore. She tucked one into her pocket.

When she finally stood up, the dormitory had emptied. The very small girl was back in the doorway with her arms folded, looking at the tin.

"Is that for her?"

"It was," said Zara.

"Can I—"

"No."

She went out into the round room, past Oliver, who made a point of not looking up, and through the tunnel and out into the corridor, where she stopped, and stood for a minute with the tin against her chest.

Then she went back.

The dormitory door was still ajar. She hung the bag from the door handle by its drawstring, where nobody could miss it, and she didn't write a note.

She was three flights up before she heard it, faint and far below her: the small, round, unmistakable sound of a tin lid coming off.

She kept walking.
