"Again," said Dibby.

"Sorry. Sorry, I'll—"

"_No_ sorry."

"No. Right. Sorry." Finn closed his eyes, and opened them. He was standing in the middle of the kitchens at Hogwarts, on the scrubbed flagstones between the great hearth and the long copper table, with a roll of parchment in his hand and three dozen elves watching him from every direction, and he had never in his life felt so entirely like an upright carrot. "Professor McGonagall. I'm sorry to—I mean, I'd like to—"

"Stop."

"I haven't—"

"You have said 'sorry' four times in ten seconds and 'I'd like to' once, which is two too few." Dibby, who was about three feet high and was wearing, in honour of the part, a pair of Professor McGonagall's own square spectacles that somebody had found for her, with a tea-towel folded on her head for a hat, folded her arms. "A Professor does not wish to be told you are sorry. A Professor wishes to be told what you want. You say: _We would like._"

"We would like."

"_We would like_."

"We would like," said Finn, with enormous effort, "to ask for—"

"No."

"Why _no_?"

"Because your voice went up at the end," said Dibby, "like a question. It is not a question. It is a request. They are quite different. A question gives them the whole of the decision. A request only asks them to hold the door."

Behind her, the other elves, who had been watching with an intensity that Finn found, on the whole, rather alarming, made a number of small, thoughtful, approving noises. Somebody at the back said, "Hear, hear." Somebody else, very quietly, was taking notes.

It had all begun, as most of the kitchens' best ideas began, with Tibby.

---

She had found him at breakfast. She'd appeared at his elbow as he was finishing his porridge, in the usual way, which is to say with a faint pop and no preamble, in her neat grey waistcoat with the small silver _T_ stitched on the pocket, and said, "Master Finn. May I speak? It will not take long. It is a matter of some delicacy."

It had, in the end, taken forty minutes.

They were the best forty minutes he'd spent that month. She had led him down to the kitchens by the back stairs, and sat him on a barrel, with a mug of Winter Blend, in the glow of the huge, ancient, roaring hearth, and with a certain shy ceremony had unrolled the parchment. It was a scroll. It was a good foot and a half long. It was written in a small, upright, extraordinarily neat hand, in blue ink, with a green silk ribbon, and it was headed, in capitals, with a flourish:

_A Petition from the Kitchens of Hogwarts, Concerning the Matter of Christmas Eve._

"We have been here," said Tibby, "a very long time."

"I know."

"The feast on Christmas Eve is a great thing. We are proud of it. We have made it every year, I think, since before the Founders." She looked at the fire. "I have counted, Master Finn, in the records. We have never once been out of this room on Christmas Eve. We cook, we serve, we clear. When it is over the students go to bed and the staff go to bed and we begin to prepare for the morning. It is very good work. We do not complain."

"No."

"But it has occurred to us," said Tibby, rather faintly, "that we have never eaten it."

Finn put down his mug.

"We have tasted, of course. One must. But we have never sat down. We have never been at the table when the candles are lit, and the carols are sung, and it is nearly midnight. We hear it. We hear it through the door." She smoothed the parchment. "We should like to know what it is like."

It was a very quiet request. It was so quiet, and so reasonable, and so unspeakably touching that for a moment Finn was afraid he would do something foolish, like cry into the Winter Blend.

There were four points. She read them aloud. One: that the kitchens might be released from duty, from four in the afternoon on the twenty-fourth. Two: that the feast might be cooked, for that one night, by someone else. Three: that those elves who wished to do so might sit at the long table with the staff and the students, as guests. Four: that there might be singing.

"And you want me to take it to the Headmistress," said Finn.

"We should like you to _ask_."

"Why me?"

"Because we trust you," said Tibby, as if it were the plainest thing in the world. "You listen. You are not frightened of us, and you are not frightened _for_ us, which is worse." She pushed her glasses up her nose. "And because we know you are not good at asking, Master Finn. We have watched. It is the reason that we chose you. If you are brave enough to do a thing that is difficult for you, she will know that it is serious."

He had looked at her, and at the long blue lines of her small neat writing, and at the roaring red hearth, and in spite of everything, in spite of the tightening in his stomach and the heat in his face, he had laughed.

"That's—" he said. "That's really quite clever."

"We have had a great many years," said Tibby, "to think."

---

The Headmistress's office was at the top of a spiral stair, behind a stone gargoyle that did not ask for a password, because it had known him since the first year and found him, on the whole, reasonable.

He climbed it with the scroll in his hand and his heart in his mouth.

He had been there twice before: once for a form that needed a Head's signature, and once, in the first year, to be told off for something that he now couldn't recall. It was a round room, full of afternoon light, with a high window and a great curved desk and a row of old headmasters in frames, all of whom appeared to be asleep. There was a tartan tin on the corner of the desk. Behind it, writing, with her hat on, with a quill in one hand and the remains of a ginger newt in the other, sat Professor McGonagall.

"Mr MacLeod," she said. She didn't look up. "Come in. I understand you have a proposal."

"I—how did you—"

"I have been a teacher for a very long time." She finished her sentence, signed it, and looked at him over her glasses. "Dibby sent a note. Sit down, Mr MacLeod. You look as though you might fall."

He sat.

He had rehearsed it. He had said it to the Mimbulus, and to the second-year Herbology class, and to Dibby, in a pair of borrowed spectacles, fourteen times. He opened his mouth to begin, and every word of it, in order, cleanly, one after the other, got up and left.

"Well," said Finn.

"Yes?"

"It's—they'd—I mean we—" He heard his own voice. It had gone up at the end. "Sorry. I'm sorry. I'll start again."

"Take your time."

He took a breath. He thought of Dibby, in a tea-towel hat. He thought of a small, calm, neat hand, in blue ink, going _we have never eaten it_.

"The elves would like Christmas Eve off," he said.

It came out quite flat. It sat there on the Headmistress's desk like an egg.

"Would they," said Professor McGonagall.

"They'd like to come to the feast. Not to cook it, and not to serve it. As guests." He swallowed. He held out the scroll. "They've asked me to say, they've never eaten it. In a thousand years. They've never sat down."

She took the parchment. She unrolled it, and read it, and the whole of the room went quiet; she read it very slowly, from the top to the bottom, with her mouth pressed into a thin line, and her eyes going back, once, to a place near the end. In the silence the old headmasters snored. A beam of sun moved across the floor.

"Tibby wrote this," she said.

"Yes."

"In her own hand."

"She'd have liked to read it out herself. She thought you'd—" He faltered. "She thought it'd carry more weight."

"I see." McGonagall laid the scroll flat. Her face had not changed, exactly. But there was something at the corner of her mouth, and something about the set of her shoulders, that Finn would think about, afterwards, for a long time. "Mr MacLeod, I want to be quite clear. I do not need it to carry weight. The elves of this school may come to me at any hour and about any matter, and they know that. I should have been glad to hear it from Tibby." She paused. "I am, however, pleased that she thought of you."

"Yes, Professor."

"I have a few questions."

She had eleven. They were short, and precise, and not unkind: who would cook, and how many, and what was to be done about the washing-up; whether any elves, she wondered, might feel, out of a long habit, that they ought to stay in the kitchens regardless; whether there was an arrangement in place for the little ones. Finn answered four of them and said "I'll ask" to the rest, and she nodded, once, to each, and wrote them down.

It took a quarter of an hour. At the end she put down her quill.

"Yes," said Professor McGonagall.

He stared at her.

"I beg your pardon?"

"The answer is yes." She took off her glasses and polished them on her sleeve. "With conditions. First: they are to rest. I do not mean a fortnight of polite refusals followed by a midnight raid on the larder. I shall be inspecting the kitchens at five o'clock myself, and I shall know." She put her glasses back. "Second: the feast will not cook itself. I shall appoint a committee. Hagrid will see to the roast, which means that it will be very large. Professor Sprout will see to the vegetables. Professor Flitwick will be in charge of puddings. And you, Mr MacLeod, will represent the kitchens, since the kitchens appear to have appointed you."

"Me?"

"I'd have thought it was self-evident." The corner of her mouth moved. "Third. If the elves wish to sit at the table, they are to be given a chair. Not at the end. In the middle."

Finn did not say anything. He found that he couldn't.

"And Mr MacLeod," she said, as he got slowly to his feet, "one thing more. You did not, at the end, say sorry."

"No, Professor."

"I thought I should mention it."

He was already at the door, with his hand on the latch, and his ears on fire, when he heard her say, in a different, dryer, much quieter voice, as if to herself, "I have wanted to try cooking a turkey since 1962. I am told that it would be inadvisable."

---

He ran. He had not run like that since the Bouncing Bulb incident in the third year. He ran down the spiral staircase and along the corridor and across the Entrance Hall, past the Treaty of the Front Lawn, and through the little side door, and down the steps, and by the time he reached the pear he had a stitch like a knife and was making a noise like a horse.

They had been waiting for him in a ring, in the kitchen. All of them. It must have been eighty elves, standing about the great table in complete and anxious silence, holding spoons.

"She said yes," said Finn.

Nothing happened. For a moment he thought he'd been misunderstood.

"She said _yes_," he said again. "With conditions. You're to rest, properly, and she's going to inspect. And the staff will cook. And you're to have a chair. In the middle."

It was Tibby who moved first. She had come forward, a step, from the head of the table, and she had put up one hand, in a slow, strange, uncertain way, to her face. For a very long moment she didn't say a word. And then her ears went, one by one, quite flat, and her whole small frame began to shake, and she sat down, straight on the floor, in the middle of the flagstones, and began, in great, quiet, helpless, heaving sobs, to cry.

The kitchen exploded.

It was a roar of high, shrill, ecstatic, joyful squeaks. It was eighty elves leaping, and hugging, and throwing tea-towels in the air. Somebody banged a pot. Somebody began, at the back, in a thin, rapturous, cracked little voice, to sing "Deck the Halls," and was joined by a dozen others, and then by thirty. Dibby, in the middle of it, with the spectacles still on and the tea-towel still on her head, had collapsed against the long table and was saying, over and over, "A chair, a _chair_," in a voice of thunderstruck delight.

Finn didn't know where to put his hands. He stood in the middle of it, in the warm, brilliant, roaring noise, with a stitch in his side and a great shining stupid grin on his face, and he was a good deal closer to tears than he cared to admit.

"Master Finn."

Tibby had got up. She had taken off her glasses and wiped her eyes with the end of her waistcoat. She was holding something in her hand, a small, stiff, creamy piece of card, covered in neat blue writing, and she held it out with both hands, very formally, as if it were a diploma.

"The recipe," she said. "For the Winter Blend. The true one. With the proportions." She sniffed. "For the cooks. They will need it. It will not do to let them try it by guesswork."

"Tibby, I can't—it's your recipe—"

"It is a gift," said Tibby, "and I am giving it. Take it, please. Before I begin again."

He took it.

---

He spent the rest of the day, after that, asking.

It was a curious sensation. It was not at all what he'd expected. He had thought it would be like climbing a wall, but it was more like being lifted over one: a long, odd, sunny afternoon, in which people he'd been afraid to approach for weeks turned out to be exactly where he had left them.

He found Mira at the Ravenclaw table, at dinner, with a pencil and a notebook, and a first-year beside her who was doing a very small, careful drawing of a rune. "Of course," said Mira at once, before he had finished the sentence. "Yes. Oh, that's wonderful. I'll—what time? I'll put it in the—" She stopped. She looked at her notebook. "No. No, I won't. I'll just come."

"Can I come?" said the first-year, very softly.

"You'd better ask Finn."

"Wren, is it?" said Finn. "Yes. Please. We'll need someone who can write a menu in a nice hand."

At the Gryffindor table Zara said, "Can I carve?" and Finn said, "Hagrid's doing the roast," and she said, "Can I stand next to Hagrid?" and he said, "I imagine so." Priya, at the Slytherin table, looked up from a piece of parchment covered in spirals and said, "I can do sprouts. I'm exceptionally good at sprouts," in a voice of such serene authority that he didn't dare ask for evidence. And Elliot, a little further down, with a plate of cold beef and the long, thoughtful, deadpan look of a person who has been through something recently and come out the other side, listened to the whole thing, nodded, and said, "I'll do a carp."

"A carp?"

"It's traditional. Where I'm from. It's Christmas Eve. You have a carp." They paused. "It lives in the bath for a week beforehand."

"Does it have to?"

"It's a very long story," said Elliot. "Yes."

Oliver said "I can't cook," in a tone of absolute despair, and Finn said, "You can peel," and Oliver said, "I can _peel_," as if it were a gift. And Amara, at the end of the Hufflepuff table, said "Can Nettle come?" and Finn said "No," and she said "Can he come if he's very good?" and Finn said "He's a Knarl," and she thought about this for some time.

He'd gone round the whole of the Hall, one table at a time, and not one of them, not a single one, had said, _I'm busy_.

He thought about that, on the way to the kitchens, at ten that night, with the list in his hand.

---

The kitchen was empty.

It was warm, and dim, and clean, with the fire banked low and the long tables scrubbed and bare. A single lamp burned over the dresser. A heap of tea-towels lay folded on the table. It was the sort of quiet you get in a house when everyone has gone up to bed happy.

"Master Finn."

She was sitting on a stool beside the hearth, in the dark, quite alone, with her hands in her lap and a tin on her knee.

"I have been waiting," said Tibby. "I wished to say thank you. I did not say it properly. There were a great many elves."

"You don't have to."

"I do." She hopped down. She came across the flags, and reached up, and pressed the tin, quite firmly, into the pocket of his robe. It was round, and warm, and smelt of ginger and brown sugar. "You are not to open it until you are in your bed. They are for the nights when you cannot sleep."

"How did you—"

"We are elves," said Tibby. "We know everything." She patted the pocket, twice, as one pats a horse, and stepped back. "Go to bed, Master Finn. You have a feast to cook."
