It snowed all night, and in the morning the world had been turned inside out.

Zara knew before she opened her eyes. It was the light. It came through the gap in the Gryffindor curtains with no yellow in it at all, and the dormitory was so silent that she could hear the clock on the mantelpiece, as if Hogwarts had been packed in cotton wool. She lay still for a moment. Then she sat up, looked out of the window, and said, with great and genuine feeling, "Oh, _yes_."

The grounds had vanished. In their place lay a smooth blue-white country without a single footprint, rolling down to the lake in drifts. The greenhouses were hump-backed and fat. The Forbidden Forest had been dipped in sugar. A plume of smoke went straight up from Hagrid's chimney, and far off on the Quidditch pitch the three hoops stuck out of the white like the masts of a sunken ship.

It was Sunday the thirteenth of December, and for the first time in about three weeks Zara Okonkwo woke up with nothing on her mind.

It was a strange feeling. She tried it out on the stairs. She hadn't thought about the match for at least forty hours. She hadn't thought about Vance's leaflet, or the vice-captain question, which she was going to put to Jamie properly in a week, when she had her words in order. She hadn't even thought about the fact that in two weeks she had eaten dinner with all three of her friends together exactly twice, and neither time on purpose. She had slept like a log after a strange night at the bottom of a castle, and she had come out of it with something steady under her ribs that had not been there in the autumn. It wasn't an idea. It was more of a place. It was where she kept _somewhere_.

She was using it right now. She had just walked into the Great Hall and seen, at the end of the Hufflepuff table, a small girl with two puffs of hair and a yellow scarf, eating porridge in the middle of a loud cluster of first-years. Amara was explaining something with a spoon. She hadn't looked up. And Zara had picked up her own spoon and gone to sit with Jamie.

"You're humming," said Jamie.

"I'm not."

"It's the one from the pitch. The 'da, da-da-da'." Jamie waggled a hand. "It's been in my head since Wednesday."

"It's my grandmother's."

"It's _everyone's_ now," said Jamie, and handed her the marmalade.

---

The ambush came at ten past nine.

Zara was crossing the lawn in gloves and scarf, in the middle of a straggling line of Gryffindor fifth years who had been told, by some unspoken law, that on a morning like this one went outside. Marcus had a bag of buns. Katie wore a red bobble hat. Jamie walked with her hands in her pockets and her usual even tread, and Tamsin, whose ribs had been mended on Monday, was bundled to the chin and moving with injured dignity, like a duchess. They were about thirty yards from the front steps, with the first light going yellow on the drifts, when Marcus, in front, stopped dead.

"What?" said Zara.

"There's a wall."

"What?"

"Look."

There was a wall. It stood about forty yards ahead, in a long low curve across the lawn: a waist-high rampart of packed snow with a row of neat battlement notches along the top. It was well built. It had _buttresses_. Behind it, small heads were bobbing against the sky. They were all wearing hats. Some were bobble hats, some were balaclavas, and one, in the middle, was a battered tin bucket tied under the chin with rope.

"Is that—" said Katie.

"_FIRE_," shrieked a high, commanding voice from somewhere behind the bucket.

The air went white.

It was the best volley Zara had ever seen. It rose in a single arc with a faint _hiss_, two hundred snowballs, perfectly timed, and came down on the fifth years with the Newtonian inevitability of a thing that has been planned.

"_GET DOWN!_" bawled Marcus.

Too late. It was all over in four seconds. Zara took one in the ear, one in the neck and one, quite precisely, in the mouth. Katie's bobble hat went flying. Tamsin sat down in the snow with a thump and said, in a tone of deep injury, "My _ribs_." Jamie, who had somehow seen it coming, was crouched behind a bush she had not been standing near a moment before, looking coolly interested.

"They've got a _wall_," said Marcus.

"They've got a plan," said Jamie.

"They've got a _bucket_."

Zara spat out snow. She stood up in the middle of the lawn, white from head to foot, with a wild grin spreading over her face, and looked at the wall, and at the row of triumphant heads behind it, and at the one in the middle, with the bucket, which was beginning to peer over the top.

It had a round face and a very large grin.

"Hello, Zara," said Amara.

---

She built the counter-wall in five minutes.

It was not her finest work. Jamie did most of it, with a quiet Hover Charm that lifted the snow in slabs and set them down in a line, while Marcus and Katie packed the seams, and Zara, who had been forbidden to go near a Hover Charm since the business with the Quidditch nets, ran up and down the line with her sleeves rolled, stamping and bawling directions. She had not enjoyed anything so much in months. Katie's contribution was a snow cannon that fired once, sideways, and took out Marcus's bag of buns. Zara was out of breath and soaked to the knee, and she had forgotten how good it was to do something idiotic together in the cold, for no reason, with no consequences.

"Parley!" she bellowed, finally. "I demand a parley!"

"A _what_?" said a voice.

"A meeting of the commanders! Under truce! Come out where I can see you!"

There was a long pause and a great deal of whispering. Then the bucket rose over the opposite wall, followed by a small face, a thick red hand-knitted scarf, and Amara, who climbed to the top of the rampart and stood there against the bright sky with her chin up, solemn, in an enormous tin hat. Half-hidden behind her stood a small, pale girl with straight light-brown hair and a fringe, holding a pile of snowballs in the crook of her arm like someone who had been handed an important parcel.

"I'm General Okonkwo," said Amara. "This is my second-in-command."

"Wren Calloway," said the second-in-command, in a whisper.

"Right," said Zara, equally grave, and climbed up on her own wall. "I'm Captain Okonkwo. We're related."

"I know," said Amara. "It's a nuisance."

They faced each other across forty feet of snow, on two ramparts, with their breath going up like smoke, a crowd of first years on one side and a disarrayed row of fifth years on the other. Somewhere to the left Tamsin, still sitting in the snow, said, "I'm not even sorry, it was a really good plan."

"On behalf of the fifth years," said Zara, "I would like to say that we were taken by surprise, in a most underhand fashion. We concede that your wall was good."

"It had buttresses."

"It had buttresses. I'd like to say—"

"On behalf of the first years," said Amara, "I would like to say that you hovered."

There was a pause.

"I did _not_—" said Zara, and then, slightly, she grinned. "I did. A bit."

"A lot."

"A good deal."

"It was a good ambush," said Wren suddenly, in her tiny voice, to nobody in particular. "I thought it was the best bit."

"It _was_," said Amara.

"Oh, _good_ morning!"

It cut through the cold like a spade through ice cream. Elliot had come down the front steps with a tray of paper cups on one forearm and a dry look that said they had been watching for some time. Behind them came Finn with a second tray, mince pies steaming in a tea-cloth, wearing a hat with a bobble from somebody's Christmas cracker. And behind _him_, a good deal more carefully, came Mira, with a clipboard.

"Someone has to keep score," said Mira, defensively, before anyone had spoken. She had drawn a table. It had columns for hits, near misses, and, in a smaller hand, "disputed".

"It's a snowball fight," said Zara.

"It's a snowball fight that has gone on for twelve minutes and has two sides and a wall. I thought somebody should be impartial."

"You've got a _pencil_ behind your ear."

"It's a precaution."

Zara looked at the three of them standing on the path in their coats, with the trays and the long blue shadows across the snow. She hadn't asked. She hadn't sent a note or arranged a time. It was a bit like finding that someone had left a light on.

Elliot's eyes were pink at the rims, the way they are after sleeping either very well or very badly, and they looked, if anything, lighter. She would ask later. She had a feeling she might not need to.

"Hot chocolate?" said Elliot, to the whole lawn, in a voice of great calm. "Mince pies. Terms of surrender to be negotiated after refreshments."

There was a pause. Then both armies, with a roar, put down their weapons and ran.

---

It was Professor McGonagall who ended it, at a quarter to ten.

Nobody had seen her come. She had been standing in the long window above the Great Hall steps for some time, arms folded, hat on, watching the lawn with the expression of a woman who was not sure she approved and could not look away. By the time somebody noticed, there were nearly sixty students on the grass, in teams of no known allegiance, in a battle so entangled that three Slytherin third years had been recruited, with their consent, as artillery.

She raised her hand. She did not shout. The lawn went quiet in under four seconds, and a snowball already on its way through the air seemed to feel the pressure and drop, quite gently, to the ground.

"Miss Okonkwo," said Professor McGonagall.

"Professor."

"Miss Okonkwo, the _younger_."

"Professor," said Amara, rather faintly, out of a drift.

"I should like," said McGonagall, "a treaty."

---

It was drawn up on the front steps, on the back of Zara's Quidditch schedule, with a pencil borrowed from Mira. The discussion lasted twenty minutes. There were fifteen interested parties, a number of procedural objections, and a ruling by the Chair that the term "hostilities" did not include "mince pies". Marcus argued that a bucket counted as a weapon. Amara argued that it was a hat. Jamie, asked for a view, said the bucket had been the single best piece of equipment on the field and should be left alone, and the Chair, after a long look at both of them, agreed.

At the end of it, it read:

_THE TREATY OF THE FRONT LAWN_

_1. No snowballs shall be thrown in corridors._

_2. No ambushes shall be conducted on the way to meals. (Amendment: or lessons. Amendment to the amendment: or the library.)_

_3. Hostilities shall be conducted, at all other times, on the lawn, by mutual consent, with a minimum of one (1) warning._

_4. One grand and final Battle shall be fought on Saturday the 19th, on the Quidditch pitch, by the whole of the school, in pyjamas, according to the old tradition. All grievances shall be settled by this means._

"What's that?" said Zara, looking at the fourth line.

"Pyjama Quidditch," said Amara. "Wren says there's a tradition."

"There is," said McGonagall, from the top of the steps. "It has not been held for some years. I believe the Heads of House might be persuaded." She regarded Zara. "Madam Hooch will require a deputy. A referee, you might say. It will not be a game in which the Gryffindor captain will, on this occasion, be playing."

"Professor?"

"A captain who has never watched a match from the outside," said McGonagall, "has a good deal to learn."

Zara opened her mouth. She shut it. Behind her she distinctly heard Jamie make a sound very like a cough.

"Yes, Professor."

"Sign it. All of you. I shall witness."

They signed. Zara went first, in thick squared capitals, and Amara second, with a flourish under the _a_. Then a dozen others, a mass of scrawls, a drawing of a hedgehog, and in the corner, last of all, small and neat and slightly slanting, _W. Calloway_, in green ink.

"I've never been in a snowball fight," said Wren quietly, handing back the pencil, as if confessing something. "A proper one. In Whitby we get sleet, mostly. It comes in off the sea. You can't make anything out of it."

Amara looked at her.

It was a look of absolute, personal horror. She turned to Zara, as if for confirmation, and found Zara had gone very still. Then, with a speed that would have done credit to a Seeker, Amara bent down, scooped up a double handful of snow, packed it in two movements, and put it with great accuracy into the middle of Wren's chest.

"_That's what it's like_," said Amara.

Wren looked down at the white splodge on her coat. She looked up. Her narrow, watchful face came apart from eyebrows to chin, and she grinned like a fox let into a henhouse.

---

They pinned the treaty on the wall of the Entrance Hall, between the staircase and the great doors, with four drawing pins and a sticking charm. McGonagall had insisted. It hung there in neat curling pencil with its forty signatures, beside a notice about lost scarves and a poster for the Gobstones Society, and was studied all afternoon by passing students with expressions of respectful interest.

Zara stood in front of it with her hands in her pockets and read it once more from the beginning. It was a ridiculous document, written in pencil on the back of a Quidditch schedule. She had never been so proud of anything.

"It's a good one," said Finn at her elbow. He had come up quietly with a pie in each hand and was holding one out. "I think it'll hold."

"It's got buttresses."

"It's got _everything_."

They stood and ate and looked. The pie was hot enough to steam in the cold of the doorway, and tasted of cloves. It was warm in the Hall. Beyond the open doors, across the white lawn, a few small figures were still running in the late sun.

Then, from somewhere behind them on the great stair, came a rustle, a muffled giggle, and the unmistakable creak of a small person leaning very far over a banister.

"Don't," said Zara, without turning round. "Article one. It's a corridor."

"It's a _staircase_," said Amara's voice, very clear.

"It's—"

"And you've got a warning. That's one. That's your one."

There was a short, loaded pause.

Then a snowball, quite perfectly aimed, dropped through the air and struck Zara Okonkwo, captain of Gryffindor, fifth-year Chaser, signatory to the Treaty of the Front Lawn, squarely on the top of her head.
