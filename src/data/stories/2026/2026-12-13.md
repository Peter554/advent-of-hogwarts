It snowed all night, and in the morning the world had been turned inside out.

Zara knew before she opened her eyes. It was the light. It came through the gap in the Gryffindor curtains in a clean, bright, bluish sheet, with no yellow in it at all, and the dormitory was so silent that she could hear the individual ticks of the clock on the mantelpiece, as if the whole of Hogwarts had been packed in cotton wool. She lay still for a moment, enjoying it. Then she sat up, and looked out of the window, and said, with great and genuine feeling, "Oh, _yes_."

The grounds had vanished. In their place lay a vast, smooth, blue-white country, without a single footprint, rolling away down to the lake in long soft drifts. The greenhouses were hump-backed and fat. The Forbidden Forest had been dipped in sugar. A thin plume of smoke was going up, quite straight, from the chimney of Hagrid's hut, and from the Quidditch pitch, far off, the three tall hoops stuck up out of the white like the masts of a sunken ship.

It was the thirteenth of December, a Sunday, and for the first time in about three weeks, Zara Okonkwo woke up with absolutely nothing on her mind.

It was a strange feeling. She tried it out, on the stairs. She hadn't thought about Sunday's match for at least forty hours. She hadn't thought about Vance's leaflet, or the vice-captain question, which she was going to ask Jamie, properly, in a week, when she'd got her words in order. She'd had a long, warm, deep, dreamless sleep, after a long, strange, dark night at the bottom of a castle, and she'd come out of it with a small, bright, steady thing under her ribs that had not been there in the autumn. It wasn't an idea. It was more of a place. It was where she kept _somewhere_.

She was doing it right now. She had just walked into the Great Hall, and looked across to the end of the Hufflepuff table, and seen a small girl with two puffs of hair, in a bright yellow scarf, eating porridge in the middle of a loud cluster of first-years. Amara hadn't looked up. She was explaining something with a spoon. And Zara had felt the old familiar tug, the pull of the hand that wanted to go across the hall and straighten a collar and say _have you got your gloves_, and she had picked up her own spoon and gone and sat with Jamie, instead.

"You're humming," said Jamie.

"I'm not."

"It's the one from the pitch. The one with the—" Jamie waggled a hand. "The 'da, da-da-da'. It's quite catchy. It's been in my head since Wednesday."

"It's my grandmother's."

"It's _everyone's_ now," said Jamie, and handed her the marmalade.

---

The ambush came at ten past nine.

Zara had been walking across the lawn, with her gloves on and her scarf round her neck, in the middle of a long straggling line of Gryffindor fifth years who had been told, by some magnetic and unspoken law, that on a morning like this one went outside. There was Marcus, with a bag of buns. There was Katie, with her hair in a great red bobble hat. There was Jamie, hands in pockets, walking with her usual even tread, and Tamsin, whose ribs had been mended on Monday and who was bundled to the chin and moving with a certain injured dignity, like a duchess. They had come about thirty yards from the front steps, with the lake spread out in front of them and the first light going yellow on the drifts, when Marcus, who was in front, stopped dead.

"What?" said Zara.

"There's a wall."

"What?"

"There's—" He pointed. "Look."

There was a wall.

It stood about forty yards ahead, running in a long low curve across the lawn: a thick, white, waist-high rampart of packed snow, with a row of neat, deliberate, battlement-style notches along the top. It was well built. It had _buttresses_. Behind it, quite clearly visible against the sky, a number of small heads were bobbing up and down. They were wearing hats. They were all wearing hats. Some of the hats were bobble hats and some were balaclavas and one, in the middle, was a battered tin bucket, tied under the chin with a bit of rope.

"Is that—" said Katie.

"_FIRE_," shrieked a high, ringing, commanding voice, from somewhere behind the bucket.

The air went white.

It was the most beautiful volley that Zara had ever seen. It rose in a single arc, with a faint sibilant _hiss_, and a great many small bright points of light on it, like the sparks off a firework: a hundred, two hundred snowballs, perfectly timed, going up over the wall in an unbroken curve, and coming down on the line of fifth years with the beautiful, terrible, Newtonian inevitability of a thing that has been planned.

"_GET DOWN!_" bawled Marcus.

It was too late. It was all over in about four seconds. Zara took one in the ear, one in the neck, and one, quite precisely, in the mouth. Katie's bobble hat went flying. Tamsin sat down in the snow with a thump, and said, in a tone of deep injury, "My _ribs_." Jamie, who had somehow seen it coming, was crouched behind a bush that she had not been standing near a moment before, with an expression of cool, interested appraisal.

"They've got a _wall_," said Marcus.

"They've got a plan," said Jamie.

"They've got a _bucket_."

Zara spat out snow. She wiped her face. She stood up, in the middle of the lawn, covered with white from head to foot, with a fresh, wild, delighted grin spreading slowly over her face, and she looked at the wall, and at the long row of small, bobbing, triumphant heads behind it, and at the one in the middle, with the bucket, that was beginning, slowly, to peer over the top.

It had a round face. It had a very large grin.

"Hello, Zara," said Amara.

---

She built the counter-wall in five minutes.

It was not her finest work. Jamie did most of it, in the end, with a quiet Hover Charm that lifted the snow in great slabs and set them down in a line, while Marcus and Katie packed the seams, and Zara, who couldn't do charms, ran up and down the line with her sleeves rolled, stamping, and bawling directions. She had not enjoyed anything so much in months. She had forgotten that this was what it felt like. She had forgotten the pure, clean, idiotic, physical joy of doing a thing together, in the cold, for no reason at all, and with no consequences. She was out of breath and soaked to the knee and she couldn't have said where the next five minutes would take her, and every muscle in her body was saying, _yes_.

"Parley!" she bellowed, finally, from behind the wall. "I demand a parley!"

"A _what_?" said a voice.

"A parley! A meeting of the commanders! Under truce! Come out where I can see you!"

There was a long pause, and a conference, in which a great deal of whispering could be heard. Then the bucket rose, slowly, over the top of the opposite wall, followed by a small face, and a pair of shoulders, and a thick, red, hand-knitted scarf. Amara climbed to the top of the rampart. She stood there, quite solemn, against the bright sky, with her chin up, in an enormous tin hat. By her left hand, half-hidden behind her, stood a second figure: a small, pale, narrow girl, with straight light-brown hair and a fringe, holding a pile of snowballs in the crook of her arm with the grave, anxious, careful look of someone who has been handed a very important parcel.

"I'm General Okonkwo," said Amara. "This is my second-in-command."

"Wren Calloway," said the second-in-command, in a voice like a whisper.

"Right," said Zara, with equal gravity, and climbed up on her own wall. "I'm Captain Okonkwo. We're related."

"I know," said Amara. "It's a nuisance."

They faced each other across forty feet of snow, on two ramparts, with their breath going up like smoke, and a crowd of first-years on one side and a disarrayed row of fifth years on the other, and somewhere to the left, quietly, Tamsin, still sitting in the snow, said, "I'm not even sorry, it was a really good plan."

"On behalf of the fifth years," said Zara, "I would like to say that we were taken by surprise, in a most underhand fashion. I would like to say that we, ah, concede that your wall was good."

"It had buttresses."

"It had buttresses. I'd like to say—"

"On behalf of the first years," said Amara, "I would like to say that you hovered."

There was a pause.

"I did _not_—" said Zara, and then, very slightly, she grinned. "I did. A bit."

"A lot."

"A good deal."

"It was a good ambush," said Wren, suddenly, in her tiny voice, to nobody in particular. "I thought so. I thought it was the best bit."

"It _was_," said Amara.

"Oh, _good_ morning!"

It came from behind them, on the path, in a voice that cut through the morning like a spade through ice cream. It was Elliot. They had just come down the front steps with a tray of paper cups balanced on one forearm, and a long, dark, wry look on their face that said they had been watching for some time. Behind them came Finn, with another tray, this time of mince pies, steaming, in a tea-cloth, and wearing a hat with a bobble that had come out of somebody's Christmas cracker. And behind _him_, a good deal more slowly, in a careful, deliberate, level-headed way, came Mira, with a clipboard.

"Someone has to keep score," said Mira, defensively, before anyone had spoken.

"It's a snowball fight," said Zara.

"It's a snowball fight that's gone on for twelve minutes and has two sides and a wall." She tucked the clipboard under her arm. "I thought somebody should be impartial."

"You've got a _pencil_ behind your ear."

"It's a precaution."

Zara looked at them. She looked at the three of them, standing on the path, in the first light, in their coats, with the trays of hot chocolate and pies and the long blue shadows lying across the snow. They'd come. She hadn't asked. She hadn't sent a note, or arranged a time, or even, strictly speaking, thought of it. It was a little like finding that someone had left a light on.

Elliot's eyes, she noticed, were pink at the rims, the way a person's are who has slept either very well or very badly, and they looked, if anything, lighter. She would ask later. She had a feeling she might not need to.

"Hot chocolate?" said Elliot, to the whole lawn, in a voice of great calm. "Mince pies. Terms of surrender to be negotiated after refreshments."

There was a pause. And then both armies, with a roar, put down their weapons and ran.

---

It was Professor McGonagall who ended it, at a quarter to ten.

They hadn't seen her come. She'd been standing in the long window above the Great Hall steps, apparently for a good while, with her arms folded and her hat on, looking out across the lawn with the expression of someone watching a thing she was not at all sure she approved of and could not look away from. By the time somebody noticed, there were nearly sixty students on the grass, in teams of no known allegiance, in the middle of a battle so entangled that three Slytherin third years had been recruited, with their consent, as artillery.

She raised her hand. She did not shout. She did not need to.

The lawn went quiet. It took a little under four seconds. Even a snowball that had been on its way through the air seemed to feel the pressure and drop, quite gently, to the ground.

"Miss Okonkwo," said Professor McGonagall.

"Professor."

"Miss Okonkwo, the _younger_."

"Professor," said Amara, rather faintly, out of a drift.

"I should like," said McGonagall, with great precision, "a treaty."

---

It was drawn up on the front steps, on the back of Zara's Quidditch schedule, with a pencil borrowed from Mira, on the broad cold stone beside the great doors. There was a good deal of discussion. It lasted twenty minutes. There were fifteen interested parties, a number of procedural objections, and a ruling, by the Chair, that the term "hostilities" did not include "mince pies".

At the end of it, it read:

_THE TREATY OF THE FRONT LAWN_

_1. No snowballs shall be thrown in corridors._

_2. No ambushes shall be conducted on the way to meals. (Amendment: or lessons. Amendment to the amendment: or the library.)_

_3. Hostilities shall be conducted, at all other times, on the lawn, by mutual consent, with a minimum of one (1) warning._

_4. One grand and final Battle shall be fought on Saturday the 19th, on the Quidditch pitch, by the whole of the school, in pyjamas, according to the old tradition. All grievances shall be settled by this means._

"What's that?" said Zara, looking at the fourth line.

"It's Pyjama Quidditch," said Amara. "Wren says there's a tradition."

"There is," said McGonagall, from the top of the steps, in a dry, thoughtful tone. "It has not been held for some years. I believe that the Heads of House might be persuaded." She regarded Zara for a moment. "Madam Hooch will require a deputy. A referee, you might say, to supervise. It will not be a game in which the Gryffindor captain will, on this occasion, be playing."

"Professor?"

"A captain," said McGonagall, "who has never watched a match from the outside has a good deal to learn."

Zara opened her mouth. She shut it. Behind her, quite distinctly, she heard Jamie make a sound very like a cough.

"Yes, Professor."

"Sign it," said McGonagall. "All of you. I shall witness."

They signed. Zara went first, in her thick, squared, bold capitals, and Amara second, in her round hand, with a flourish under the _a_. Then a dozen others, a mass of scrawls, a drawing of a hedgehog, and in a corner, last of all, very small and neat and slightly slanting, _W. Calloway_, in green ink.

"I've never been in a snowball fight," said Wren, quietly, handing back the pencil. It came out as though it was a confession. "I mean, a proper one. In Whitby we get sleet, mostly. It comes in off the sea. You can't make anything out of it."

Amara looked at her.

It was a long look. It was a look of the most absolute, comprehensive, personal horror. She'd gone slightly green. She turned to Zara, as if for confirmation, and found that Zara had also gone very still, and then, at a speed that would have done credit to a Seeker, she bent down, scooped up a double handful of snow, packed it, hard, in two movements, and put it with great accuracy into the middle of Wren's chest.

"_That's what it's like_," said Amara.

Wren looked down at the white splodge on her coat.

She looked up. And her whole face, narrow and pale and watchful, changed, from the eyebrows to the chin, in an extraordinary way, as a window changes when somebody lights a lamp behind it.

She grinned.

---

They pinned the treaty on the wall of the Entrance Hall, in the end, between the staircase and the great doors, with four drawing pins and a sticking charm. McGonagall had insisted. It hung there, in its neat curling pencil, with its forty signatures, beside a notice about lost scarves and a poster for the Gobstones Society, and was looked at, for the rest of the afternoon, by a stream of passing students with expressions of the most intense and respectful interest.

Zara stood in front of it, with her hands in her pockets, and read it once more from the beginning.

It was a ridiculous document. It was written in pencil, on the back of a Quidditch schedule. She would never, she thought, have been so proud of anything.

"It's a good one," said a voice at her elbow. It was Finn. He'd come up quietly, with a pie in each hand, and was holding one out. "I think it'll hold."

"It's got buttresses."

"It's got _everything_."

They stood, and ate, and looked. It was warm in the Hall. Beyond the open doors, across the white lawn, there were still a few small figures, running, in the late sun.

And then, from somewhere behind them, on the great stair, there was a quick, soft, stealthy sound, a rustle, a muffled giggle, and the unmistakable creak of a small person leaning very far over a banister.

"Don't," said Zara, without turning round. "Article one. It's a corridor."

"It's a _staircase_," said Amara's voice, very clear.

"It's—"

"And you've got a warning," said Amara. "That's one. That's your one."

There was a short, loaded pause.

Then a snowball, quite perfectly aimed, dropped through the air like a falling star and struck Zara Okonkwo, captain of Gryffindor, fifth-year Chaser, signatory to the Treaty of the Front Lawn, squarely on the top of her head.
