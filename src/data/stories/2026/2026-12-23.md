Mira was reading in the Ravenclaw common room, by the last of the fire, at ten to midnight, when she heard the music.

It was faint enough to be imagined: a thread of strings coming up the long spiral of the tower from somewhere far below. She lifted her head from her book, with her glasses pushed up and a finger in her place, and it came again. It was a waltz. It was unmistakably a waltz, in three-four time, played by a very small orchestra with a great deal of enthusiasm and a violin that was slightly flat.

She put down the book.

The common room had been empty for the best part of a day, a round blue-and-silver chamber with tall windows full of stars and twelve armchairs standing about in the firelight like patient sheep. Four people were left in the whole House. One was a seventh-year, who had gone to bed at nine with a cold. One was a cellist. One was Mira. And one, a small, cautious figure in a dressing gown, with a long green scarf wound three times round her neck and a candle in a saucer, stood on the bottom step of the dormitory staircase with her head on one side.

"You've heard it," said Wren.

"I've heard something."

"It's coming from the big stairs. I couldn't sleep." In the candlelight she looked very small and very resolute. "I went to the window. I think the portraits are doing something."

"They do things," said Mira, carefully. "In general. They talk. They visit. It's quite normal."

"Is it normal for them to play the violin?"

Mira considered this.

"No," she said, and got up, and put on her slippers.

---

It was a long way down. They went by the back stairs, past dark statues, along cold corridors, through a castle that had gone quiet in a way she'd never heard. It wasn't empty. It was resting. She could feel it in the stone, the slow settling of a very large and very old animal that has finally been allowed to put its feet up. A tapestry rustled. Somewhere a door ticked in its frame like a clock.

The music grew louder. It came up out of the dark in warm gusts, with the clink of glasses in it, and laughter, and the hurried, delighted murmur of a great many people who ought not to be awake.

They came out on the landing at the top of the Grand Staircase, and stopped.

---

Mira had always thought she knew the castle. She had been in it four and a half years, and could have drawn the Grand Staircase from memory, down to the number of steps: a hundred feet of white stone winding up through the heart of the school, with portraits on every wall from the floor to the topmost gallery. Old witches and wizards, knights and kings, a few animals, a stout bishop with a ferret. They spent most of their time asleep.

They were not asleep.

They were _dancing_. The whole wall of the staircase had come alive, and the frames, which had always seemed to her the walls of small private rooms, had no sides. The figures passed through them like doorways. Two ladies in powdered wigs had waltzed out of a seascape and into a still life of pears. A dozen small monks, each in his own frame, were forming a line for a reel. A thin, cross wizard in a ruff stood at the edge of a landscape and conducted, with a stick, an orchestra of cats.

And on the landing directly in front of them, in a ball gown the size of a haystack and a pink satin sash, one hand on the shoulder of a small, stout, red-faced knight in plate armour and an enormous hat, the Fat Lady was waltzing.

"That's Sir Cadogan," whispered Wren.

"It is."

"He's stepped on her foot."

"He has."

"She's hit him with a fan."

"I'm not at all surprised," said Mira.

"Madam," Sir Cadogan was saying, rubbing his helmet, "the floor _moved_."

"The floor," said the Fat Lady, "has been where it is since 1743. You, sir, have trodden on me three times since the interval and you have apologised to my _hem_." She hit him again, without breaking step. "Again. From the top. And this time, count."

Nobody noticed the two girls in dressing gowns standing in the doorway in the middle of a ball. A duchess in conversation with a small stag swept past at arm's length and did not turn her head. A hairy troll in a pink ballet skirt, with a gentle, pensive face, stepped out of a tapestry of his own, made a small bow in their direction, and passed on.

"It's Barnabas the Barmy's," breathed Wren. "The ones from the bad ballet. They're allowed out."

"I had no idea."

"Is it all night?"

"It must be," said Mira. "It must be a, a sort of annual—" She stopped. She'd found it, and it was so simple that she nearly laughed. "They do this when we're gone."

"What?"

"The castle empties, and the portraits have it to themselves, with nobody to say _shh_." She was whispering, though she didn't know why. "They give it a night first, to be sure nobody comes back for a forgotten scarf."

"Then they don't want us," said Wren, anxiously.

"I don't know. I don't think they'd mind." She took the candle. "We'll be quiet. We'll only look."

---

It was the voice that stopped her.

It came from the landing below, over the waltz, high and dry and delighted, like a very good knife being drawn from a very old sheath. It said, in tones of uncontainable scorn, "No, no, no, my dear, I will _not_ have it. You cannot put a locative in the fourth declension. It has never been done. It is a barbarism."

"Roar," said somebody else, mildly.

"A _barbarism_. And that, I may tell you, was not even the right case for a roar."

"Roar."

"I don't care what you think about it. You are a troll. You have no standing in the matter."

Mira went down the stairs. She didn't decide to. She was simply on the steps, and then on the next landing, with Wren at her elbow, in a pool of light from three tall candles, at the foot of a very large, very dark old frame.

She had passed it a hundred times on the way to Runes. It held a tall, bony, upright woman of about fifty in a high lace collar and a grey silk gown, with a wig piled above a narrow, hawkish face, a quill tucked behind her ear and ink on her cuff. She was leaning out of the frame at a dangerous angle, like a woman over the rail of a ship, in earnest conversation with a large, shaggy troll in a tutu. He was holding a slate. It had a great many crossings-out.

"Oh," said Mira.

The woman turned her head.

It was a very sharp look. It went from Mira's slippers to her face, from her face to the book in her dressing-gown pocket, from the book to her hands, and came to rest, with a click, on the middle of her right forefinger, where there was a small, permanent, faintly blue stain.

"_Ink_," said the woman, with profound satisfaction. "My dear child, you have the ink of a person who writes things down."

"I—yes. I'm sorry. I didn't mean to interrupt—"

"Nonsense. I was losing. Stand there." She snapped her fingers at the troll, who sighed, took his slate, and went back into his tapestry. "Wake. Isolde Wake. Ravenclaw, 1763 to 1802, Chair in Runic Studies at the Ministry for eleven of those years, and an authority on anything anybody has ever bothered to carve. You may call me Dame."

"Mira Patel."

"A runologist!" said Dame Isolde. "Or something like. Don't tell me. It's in the way you hold your hands." She narrowed her eyes. "A student who has been looking for a thing to be."

It was so unexpectedly exact that Mira, who had prepared to be polite, simply stood there with her mouth a little open.

"How did you know?"

"Child," said Dame Isolde, gently, "I have been looking at students for two hundred and twenty years."

---

"I've a puzzle," said the Dame.

They were sitting on the bottom step. She had offered it in the tone of someone offering a sweet, and Mira, who had never in her life been able to resist a puzzle, had sat down at once, with Wren's candle beside her. The Dame leaned as far out of the frame as she could, chin on fist.

"Look at the frame."

It was black with age and carved with a deep border, and the border was covered, corner to corner, with small, narrow, closely cut marks. Mira had seen them every day for four years and always taken them for decoration. They were runes: bindrunes, three and four and five together, tangled like the roots of a hedge.

"I carved that myself," said Dame Isolde, "in the year 1799, with a chisel. It took four months. I intended it as a message to anybody sensible who should come after me, and I have been waiting for somebody to read it ever since. Two hundred years is a long time for a woman in oils."

"It's a message?"

"A sentence. One sentence, all the way round. There's a piece missing. In 1851 somebody spilled a bottle of Dragon Tonic down the left side and ate away eleven characters. I never had them replaced. I thought it would be an excellent test."

"A test?"

"Of whether a person can think. Anyone can read what's there." She sat back like a cat on a wall. "The question is whether you can read what isn't."

Wren hadn't said a word since they sat down. She had drawn her knees up and wound her scarf twice more round her neck, and her eyes in the candlelight were very round.

"It's the same," she said, softly. "The third-floor corridor. The carvings. They look like this. I asked you about them. You said you'd work it out."

"I did say that," said Mira, faintly.

The Dame had begun to smile.

---

It took forty minutes, and it would have taken longer, she thought afterwards, without somebody sitting beside her. She'd have said she worked best alone, in silence, with a sharp pencil. But Wren held the candle where she needed it, and wrote down each character Mira read aloud in neat green ink on the back of her hand, and when Mira said "I don't know, it might be _þ_, it might be a _d_," said at once, "It's a _d_, the stem's too straight."

They worked round the frame from the top left corner, one rune at a time, to the long pulse of the waltz. It was Old English. _Hwæt. Se þe þis rædeþ_—

"'Hark,'" said Mira. "'Whoever reads this—'" She turned a corner. "'Was—'"

"'Was asked,'" said Wren.

"Yes. Good." She had reached the eaten wood, black and smooth as a river-stone. She ran her finger along it. It was not a guess, exactly. There was a hole in the line, and the hole had edges, and every stroke on either side of it leaned towards the middle like the sides of a bridge.

"'And whoever asks,'" said Mira, slowly, "'shall answer. And whoever answers shall teach. And whoever teaches shall be—'" Her voice failed. She tried again. "'Shall be asked.'"

The waltz, somewhere above them, had come to an end. She could hear the drip of a candle, and a creak from the staircase, and the sound of a very small girl letting out a breath.

"It goes round," said Wren.

"It's a circle. It doesn't end." Mira laughed, unsteadily. "It's a whole sentence about passing a thing on."

"Eleven characters," said the Dame, complacently. "And you got every one. Do you know, child, that frame has hung here for two hundred and twenty years, and the only person who ever stopped to look at it was a Gnome."

---

It was five in the morning when they went back.

They had stayed to the end. It was impossible not to. They stayed for the second waltz and the polka and the long, ridiculous reel in which every portrait on the Grand Staircase linked arms in a single swaying chain from the bottom landing to the top. A stout monk gave them a glass of something that tasted of plums. They were taught a minuet. Sir Cadogan challenged Wren to a duel with a fish, drew a kipper from his boot with a flourish, and was beaten in four moves by a girl from a Whitby fish shop, who held a mackerel like someone who knew which end was which. He bowed from the hip and declared the honour of his family satisfied. The Fat Lady, in the next frame along, said that it was the first sensible thing he'd done since Michaelmas. And through all of it, at the edge of the light, the Dame's clever, amused face leaned out of its frame, listening to the music and watching the two of them.

"I should tell you," Mira had said, at about three, on a landing, to nobody in particular, "I've been trying to decide what to do."

"I know."

"It's a very long list. I've got fourteen things. I've wanted to do all of them. I wanted to be sure, and I think I've been waiting for one of them to tell me."

"They don't," said the Dame. "In my experience. They are only interested in being chosen." She tapped her pen on the frame. "What are you good at, child?"

"I'm good at—" Mira stopped. A hundred well-meaning adults had asked her that in a hundred forms, and she had always begun with a list. Now, on a cold dark staircase at three in the morning, with the music in her ears and a green-ink scribble on the back of a small girl's hand, she found that she didn't need one.

"I like finding things," she said. "Old things. Things nobody's looked at. And I like what happens after. When someone else looks. When you say, 'There's something here,' and show them, and their face changes." She swallowed. "I think I'd like to do that. For a living, if that's a thing. Research, and some teaching, and a place where you could put what you found so that somebody else could get to it."

"There's a word for that."

"Is there?"

"It is," said Dame Isolde, "a _library_."

---

They said goodnight in the first grey of morning.

The ball had ended suddenly, in the way of such things, with a long, tired, satisfied silence. Mira looked round on the landing and found the great white wall full of frames again, each in its place, quite still. The monks were asleep. The cats were asleep. The Fat Lady, in her haystack gown, was snoring rather ostentatiously in a frame that wasn't entirely her own, and a wounded Sir Cadogan, across the landing, was pretending not to look.

She went down the steps with Wren, along a corridor lined with sleeping faces. Every so often, as they passed, one of the portraits gave a small, over-loud, unconvincing snore. A stout wizard with his hat over his eyes was breathing in a suspiciously regular rhythm. A very old lady had put a book over her face. And a small grey whiskered man in a heavy frame opened one eye as they went by, looked at them, shut it again, and said, with great distinctness, to nobody, "_Nothing_ happened."

Wren giggled, a tiny, startled noise.

"Thank you," she said, at the foot of the stair, so low that Mira could hardly hear. She was looking up, her hands in the sleeves of her dressing gown. "For asking me to come. I thought I'd be in the way."

"You weren't in the way."

"I wrote the characters on my hand."

"You did. You were quicker than I was."

"I only did the easy bits."

"There aren't any easy bits." They had stopped at the turn of the Ravenclaw stair, in the pale light from the narrow window, with mist lying over the lake. "I meant it," said Mira. "What I said on the stairs. I wanted to say it again when the music had stopped. In case it was only the music."

Wren looked at her for a long time, with a smear of green still on her cheek. She didn't smile at once. It came slowly, from the corners, like the light in the window.

"Good," said Wren. "You'd be good at it."

She said it as if she were remarking on the weather. Mira put a hand on her shoulder.

"I'm going to write a letter," she said. "At breakfast. I've a promise to keep."

"To the lady with the trunk?"

"To the lady with the trunk."

They went up the stairs together towards the tower, past the sleeping portraits, one of whom, as they turned the corner, let out a single, enormous, utterly insincere snore.
