Mira was reading in the Ravenclaw common room, by the last of the fire, at ten to midnight, when she heard the music.

It was very faint. It was the kind of sound you think you've imagined, a thin, bright, wandering thread of strings, coming up the long spiral of the tower from somewhere far below; and she lifted her head from her book, and listened, with her glasses pushed up and a finger in her place, and it came again. It was a waltz. It was unmistakably a waltz, in three-four time, played by a very small orchestra with a great deal of enthusiasm and a violin that was slightly flat.

She put down the book.

The common room was empty. It had been empty for the best part of a day: a great, round, blue-and-silver chamber, with its tall windows full of stars, and its twelve armchairs standing about in the firelight like patient sheep. There were four people left in the whole of the House. One was a seventh-year, who had gone to bed at nine with a cold. One was a cellist. One was Mira. And one, a small, dark, cautious figure in a dressing gown, with a long green scarf wound three times round her neck and a candle in a saucer, was standing on the bottom step of the staircase to the dormitories, perfectly still, with her head on one side.

"You've heard it," said Wren.

"I've heard something."

"It's coming from the stairs. The big ones. I couldn't sleep." She looked, in the candlelight, extremely small, and extremely resolute. "I went to the window. I think the portraits are doing something."

"They do things," said Mira, carefully. "In general. They talk. They visit. It's quite normal."

"Is it normal for them to play the violin?"

Mira considered this.

"No," she said, and got up, and put on her slippers.

---

It was a long way down. They went in silence, by the back stairs, past the dark shapes of statues, along cold corridors that creaked in the stillness, through a castle that had, in the space of a single day, gone quiet in a way she'd never heard it. It wasn't empty. It was only resting. She could feel it, in the stone, the long slow settling of a very large and very old creature that has finally been allowed to put its feet up. A tapestry rustled. Somewhere a door ticked, in its frame, like a clock.

The music grew louder. It came up out of the dark in great warm gusts, with the clink of glasses in it, and laughter, and the soft, hurried, delighted murmur of a great many people who ought not to be awake.

They came out on the landing at the top of the Grand Staircase, and stopped.

---

Mira had often thought, in an idle way, that she knew the castle. She'd been in it for four and a half years. She had a very clear picture in her head of every staircase and every corridor, and she could have drawn the Grand Staircase, from memory, down to the number of the steps. It was a vast, white, curving stone flight, a hundred feet high, that wound upward through the heart of the school in a long spiral of landings, and on every wall of it, from the floor to the topmost gallery, hung portraits.

There were hundreds. She'd seen them every day. They were old witches and wizards, knights and kings, a few animals, a stout bishop with a ferret, in heavy gilt frames, who spent most of their time asleep.

They were not asleep.

They were _dancing_. It was the only word. The long white wall of the staircase, from top to bottom, had come entirely alive, in a vast, swirling, glittering, silent-footed ballroom, and the frames, which had always seemed to her to be the walls of small private rooms, were quite open. They had no sides. The figures passed through them as through doorways. A pair of ladies in powdered wigs had waltzed out of a seascape and into a still life of pears. A dozen small monks, each in his own frame, were forming up in a line for a reel. A thin, cross-looking wizard in a ruff stood at the edge of a landscape and conducted, with a stick, a small orchestra of cats.

And on the landing, directly in front of them, quite lost to the world, in a ball gown the size of a haystack and a pink satin sash, one hand on the shoulder of a small, stout, red-faced knight in plate armour and a very large hat, the Fat Lady was waltzing.

"That's Sir Cadogan," whispered Wren.

"It is."

"He's stepped on her foot."

"He has."

"She's hit him with a fan."

"I'm not at all surprised," said Mira.

They didn't know what to do. It was hardly possible to do anything. They'd come out onto the landing in their slippers and dressing gowns, with their candle, and stood quite still in the doorway, in the middle of a ball. Nobody noticed. It was astonishing. A passing duchess, who had been conducting a lively conversation with a small stag, swept by at an arm's length and did not turn her head. A hairy, enormous troll in a pink ballet skirt, with a cheerful, pensive, gentle face, stepped out of a tapestry of his own, made a small bow in their direction, and passed on.

"It's Barnabas the Barmy's," breathed Wren. "The ones from the bad ballet. They're allowed out."

"I had no idea."

"Is it all night?"

"It must be," said Mira. "It must be a, a sort of, I don't know what you'd call it, an annual—" She stopped. She'd found it. It was so simple that she nearly laughed. "They do this when we're gone."

"What?"

"The castle empties. The students go. And the portraits have the whole of it to themselves, and nobody to say _shh_." She was whispering, though she didn't know why. "It's the first night. The twenty-third. I'd have bet anything that it's the first of the year that nobody's home."

"Then they don't want us," said Wren, anxiously.

"I don't know. I don't think they'd mind." She took the candle. "We'll be quiet. We'll only look."

---

It was the voice that stopped her.

It came from the landing below, over the sound of the waltz: a dry, cutting, high, delighted voice, like a very good knife being drawn from a very old sheath. It was saying, in tones of pure and uncontainable scorn, "No, no, no, my dear, I will _not_ have it. You cannot parse a locative in the fourth declension. It has never been done. It is a barbarism."

"Roar," said somebody else, mildly.

"A _barbarism_."

"Roar."

"I don't care what you think about it. You are a troll. You have no standing in the matter."

Mira went down the stairs.

She did not decide to. She was simply on the steps, and then on the next landing, with Wren at her elbow, in a pool of warm light from three tall candles, standing at the foot of a very large, very ornate, very dark old frame.

It had been there all her life. She'd passed it a hundred times, on the way to Runes. It held a portrait of a woman, a tall, bony, extraordinarily upright woman of about fifty, in a high lace collar and a grey silk gown with a silver chain, and a wig piled high above her narrow, hawk-like, dominating face. She had a quill in one hand, tucked behind her ear. She had ink on her cuff. And she was leaning out of the frame, at a dangerous angle, in the manner of a person leaning over the rail of a ship, in earnest conversation with an enormous, shaggy, thoughtful troll in a tutu.

"Oh," said Mira.

The woman turned her head.

It was a very sharp look. It travelled, in one rapid movement, from Mira's slippers to her face, and from her face to the book still stuck in the pocket of her dressing gown, and from the book to her fingers, and it came to rest, with a click, on her right hand, where, as it happened, in the middle of the forefinger, there was a small, permanent, faintly blue stain.

"_Ink_," said the woman, in a voice of the most profound satisfaction. "You have ink. My dear child, you have the ink of a person who writes things down."

"I—yes. I'm sorry. I didn't mean to interrupt—"

"Nonsense. I was losing. Stand there. Don't move." She snapped her fingers at the troll, who, with a sigh, went back into his tapestry. "Wake. Isolde Wake. Ravenclaw, 1763 to 1802, Chair in Runic Studies at the Ministry for eleven of those years and an exceedingly well-known authority on anything that anybody has ever bothered to carve. You may call me Dame."

"Mira Patel."

"A runologist!" said Dame Isolde. "Or something like. Don't tell me. It's in the way you hold your hands." She narrowed her eyes. "A student who has, I would say, been looking for a thing to be."

It was so unexpectedly exact that Mira, who had prepared to be polite, simply stood there with her mouth a little open.

"How did you know?"

"Child," said Dame Isolde, gently, "I have been looking at students for two hundred and twenty years."

---

"I've a puzzle," said the Dame.

They were sitting on the stairs. It had come about quite naturally. She'd said it in the tone of a person offering a sweet; and Mira, who had never in her life been able to resist a puzzle, had sat down on the bottom step with Wren's candle beside her. The Dame had leaned, as far as she could, out of the great gilt frame, with her chin on her fist.

"Look at the frame."

Mira looked.

It was a very old one, black with age, with a deep carved border, a broad band that ran all the way round the edge, and the band was covered, from corner to corner, with small, narrow, closely cut marks. She'd seen them before. She had seen them every day for four years, and had always taken them for decoration. They were runes. They were lines of them, in a long unbroken row, tangled together in complicated knots, like the roots of a hedge: bindrunes, three and four and five together, so thick and so intricately overlaid that they looked more like a pattern in lace.

"I carved that myself," said Dame Isolde, "in the year 1799. With a chisel. It took four months. I intended it as a message to anybody sensible who should come after me, and I have been waiting for somebody to read it ever since." She sniffed. "Two hundred years is a very long time for a woman in oils."

"It's a message?"

"It's a sentence. A single sentence. It goes all the way round." She tapped, on the inside of the frame, with a bony finger. "There's a piece missing. In 1851 somebody spilled a bottle of Dragon Tonic down the left side and ate away eleven characters. I've never had them replaced. I thought it would be an excellent test."

"A test?"

"Of whether the person can think. Anyone can read what's there." She sat back, with the air of a cat on a wall. "The question is whether you can read what isn't."

Wren was looking at the frame. She had not said a word since they sat down; she'd drawn her knees up, and wrapped her green scarf twice more round her neck, and her eyes, in the candlelight, were extremely round.

"It's the same," she said, softly.

"Is it?"

"The third-floor corridor. Where the carvings are. They look like this. I thought they were—" She stopped, and went pink. "I asked you about them. You said you'd work it out."

"I did say that," said Mira, faintly.

The Dame had begun to smile.

---

It took forty minutes.

It would have taken longer, she thought afterwards, if there had not been somebody sitting beside her. It was a curious thing. She'd have said, if asked, that she worked best alone, in silence, with a sharp pencil and a good light. But she had a small, thin, careful girl at her elbow, who held the candle where she needed it, and wrote down, without being asked, in neat green ink on the back of her hand, each of the characters that Mira read aloud; and who, when she said "I don't know, it might be _þ_, or it might be a _d_," said at once "It's a _d_, the stem's too straight"; and they worked their way round the frame, together, from the top left corner, one rune at a time, in the glow of the candle and the long sweet pulse of the waltz.

It was a sentence in Old English. It was an extraordinary thing. She felt it coming, the way you feel a step in the dark: a long, slow, weaving thread of syllables, rising out of the knots, taking shape under her eyes. _Hwæt. Se þe þis rædeþ_—

"'Hark,'" said Mira. "'He who reads this—' no, 'whoever reads this.'" She turned a corner. "'Was—'"

"'Was asked,'" said Wren.

"'Was asked,' yes. Good. 'And whoever—'" She stopped. She'd got to the bottom. She ran her finger along the lower edge, down the whole length of the missing section, where the wood was black and eaten and smooth as a river-stone, and for a moment the whole of the sentence stood open before her like a door.

She could see it. It wasn't a guess. It was something more, a shape, an inevitability. There was a hole in the line, and the hole had edges, and everything on either side of it, every stroke, every angle, every little leaning stem, was pointing towards the middle, like the sides of a bridge.

"'And whoever asks,'" said Mira, slowly, "'shall answer.'"

"Go on."

"'And whoever answers—'" She was whispering. "'—shall teach.'"

"And?"

"'And whoever teaches shall be—'" Her voice failed. She swallowed. She tried again. "'Shall be asked.'"

It was very quiet. The waltz, somewhere above them, had come to an end. In the silence she could hear the drip of a candle, and a faint creak from the staircase, and, quite distinctly, the sound of a very small girl letting out a breath.

"It goes round," said Wren.

"It goes round," said Mira. "It's a circle. It doesn't end. It's—" She laughed, unsteadily. "It's a whole sentence about, about passing a thing on. It's an argument that it never stops."

"Eleven characters," said the Dame, complacently. "And you got them all. I never thought I'd see it." She put her head on one side. "Do you know, child, I have had that frame hung in this house for two hundred and twenty years, and the only person who has ever once stopped to look at it was a Gnome."

---

It was five in the morning when they went back.

They'd stayed to the end. It was impossible not to. They had stayed for the second waltz, and the polka, and the long, grand, ridiculous reel, in which every portrait on the Grand Staircase had linked arms, from the bottom landing to the top, in a single, swaying, sixty-yard chain. They had been given, by a stout monk, a glass of something that tasted of plums. They'd been taught a minuet. Sir Cadogan had challenged Wren to a duel with a fish. And through all of it, round the edges, the Dame's thin, clever, highly amused face had hung in its frame, leaning out into the light, listening to the music, and watching, from time to time, the two of them.

"I should tell you," Mira had said, at about three, on a landing, in a low voice, to nobody in particular, "I've been trying to decide what to do."

"I know."

"It's a very long list. I've got fourteen things. I've got, I mean, I don't—" She'd spread her hands. "I've wanted to do all of them. I wanted to be sure. And I think I've been waiting for one of them to tell me."

"They don't," said the Dame. "In my experience."

"No."

"They are only interested in being chosen." She'd said it quite crisply, with her pen between her fingers. "What are you good at, child?"

"I'm good at—" Mira had stopped. It was a question that she'd been asked, in a hundred forms, by a hundred well-meaning adults, and she'd never been able to answer it. She'd always begun with a list. Now, on a cold dark staircase at three o'clock in the morning, with the music beating in her ears and a green-ink scribble on the back of a small girl's hand, she found that she did not have to.

"I like finding things," she said. "Old things. Things nobody's looked at. And I like—" She hesitated. "I like what happens after. When someone else looks. When you say, 'There's something here,' and show them, and their face changes." She swallowed. "I think I'd like to do that. For a living. If that's a thing. Research, and some teaching, and a place where—where you could put what you found, so that somebody else could get to it."

"There's a word for that."

"Is there?"

"It is," said Dame Isolde, "a _library_."

---

They said goodnight, in the end, on the long dark stair, in the first thin grey of morning.

The ball had ended. It had ended quite suddenly, in the way of such things, with a long, tired, satisfied silence, and the soft fading-out of the orchestra. Mira had hardly noticed. She had only looked round, on the landing, and found that the hall was nearly empty. The great white wall was full of frames. They hung in their places, in their gilt and their dark wood, quite still, as they had always done. The monks were asleep. The cats were asleep. The Fat Lady, in her haystack gown, was snoring, rather ostentatiously, with her mouth open, in a frame that was not entirely her own.

She went down the steps with Wren.

It was an odd, quiet, tender journey, in the light of a thin grey dawn, along a corridor lined with sleeping faces. They walked slowly. They did not speak. Every so often, as they passed, one of the portraits gave a small, over-loud, unconvincing snore. A stout wizard, with his hat over his eyes, was breathing in a suspiciously regular rhythm. A very old lady had put a book over her face. And a small, grey, whiskered man, in a heavy frame, as they went by, opened one eye, looked at them, shut it again, and said, with great distinctness, to nobody, "_Nothing_ happened."

Wren giggled. It was a tiny, startled, helpless noise.

"Thank you," she said, at the foot of the stair, in a voice so low that Mira could hardly hear it. She was looking up, with her hands in the sleeves of her dressing gown. "For asking me. To come. I didn't think—I thought I'd be in the way."

"You weren't in the way."

"I wrote the characters on my hand."

"You did," said Mira. "You were quicker than I was."

"I only did the easy bits."

"There aren't any easy bits." She had stopped. They were at the turn of the Ravenclaw stair, in the quiet, in the first pale light through the narrow window, with the long white mist lying over the lake. "I meant it," said Mira. "What I said. On the stairs."

"I know."

"I wanted to say it again when the music had stopped. In case it was only the music."

Wren looked at her.

She looked for a long time. Her eyes were very clear and grey and watchful in the dawn, in the plain narrow face, with the smear of green still on her cheek and the ink on her fingers, and she did not smile at once. It came slowly, from the corners, like the light in the window.

"Good," said Wren. "You'd be good at it."

She said it quite simply. She might have been remarking on the weather. But Mira, standing in the cold at the turn of the stair with her book in her pocket and her heart beating up in her throat, understood, quite suddenly, that nobody had ever said it to her like that, as a plain fact, a thing already true.

She put a hand on the girl's shoulder.

"I'm going to write a letter," she said. "At breakfast. I've a promise to keep."

"To the lady with the trunk?"

"To the lady with the trunk."

And they went up the stairs together, towards the tower, in the grey early light, past the sleeping portraits, one of whom, as they turned the corner, let out a single, enormous, utterly insincere snore.
