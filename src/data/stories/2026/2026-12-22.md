The Hogwarts Express left Hogsmeade at eleven, and Zara had never in her life watched it go.

She'd always been on it. For four years it had been the thing that carried her away, in a rattle and a roar, with her trunk in the rack and her feet on the seat and her face pressed to the glass; for four years she had looked out of the window at a small, dark, empty platform and thought nothing at all. It had never occurred to her that there would be someone standing on it.

There were twenty-three of them, in the end. They had come down from the castle in two long carriages, through the cold, bright, glittering morning, with the snow squeaking under the wheels, and stood in a ragged bunch at the end of the platform, in coats and scarves and mittens, with their breath going up. They were the ones who were staying. There were the four of them, and Oliver; there were Amara and Wren, in their matching grey shirts under their cloaks, because neither of them had been willing to take them off; there was Ansel, the small second-year Slytherin, who had been told by his parents to enjoy himself and appeared to be doing it with a terrible, dogged earnestness; and a dozen others, seventh-years and thirds, a Hufflepuff who had a cold, a Ravenclaw who had brought her own cello.

The train stood by the platform, enormous and red and shining, with steam coming out of its sides in long slow plumes. Doors banged. A porter was shouting. Down the long row of windows, three hundred faces were pressed to the glass, pink-cheeked and cheerful, with scarves flying, waving to the people who were not coming.

"Look at them," said Amara. "They look so _happy_."

"They're going home."

"We're not."

"No." Zara looked down at her. She was wearing her bucket hat. It was a little the worse for wear. "Do you mind?"

Amara thought about it, honestly, with her head on one side.

"I thought I would," she said. "But I've got Nettle. And you. And Wren. And Hagrid says he's going to teach me to make a hippogriff sandwich." She frowned. "I don't know what that is. But it sounds like _somebody's_ Christmas."

---

Jamie found her at the carriage door.

She'd got a place in the very last compartment, by a window, with her trunk on the rack and her cropped head sticking out of the glass. She looked, Zara thought, a good deal more cheerful than she usually let on. She was wearing a thick blue jumper with a snowflake on it, which she had clearly been given, and she'd got something in her hand.

"I've told them," she said.

"Told them what?"

"The team. I did it at breakfast. I said, as of this morning, I am vice-captain, and I have the right to tell you all when you're wrong." She considered. "Marcus cheered. Katie cried. Tamsin asked if there'd be a badge."

"What did you say?"

"I said I'd make one." She held out her hand. In it lay a small, round, lumpy, tin-coloured thing, about the size of a button, with a pin on the back. It was hand-cut. It had been done with a bent spoon, a tin lid, a good deal of ill-judged filing, and, in the middle, in a hand a good deal steadier than the badge, a tiny, cross-eyed, deeply lopsided Snitch.

It was the worst thing Zara had ever seen. She took it at once.

"Pin it on yourself," said Jamie. "I've drawn blood twice."

She pinned it on. It went on the inside of her cloak, over her heart, where nobody could see, and it was, she thought, with her ears hot, the best thing she'd ever been given.

"See you in January, Captain."

"See you in January."

"Don't plan anything," said Jamie. "Over the holidays. Just rest. It's an order."

"I'm not going to plan—"

"You are _already_ planning," said Jamie, with total certainty, and the whistle went.

---

She saw Mira and Priya, out of the corner of her eye, and looked away.

It was a private thing. She knew that. They were standing at the far end of the platform by the last carriage door, slightly apart from everybody else, quite close together, in the cold white light. Priya had her trunk beside her. She was in her good coat, with her green scarf, and a new woollen hat that Mira had knitted, rather badly, in the last week, and she'd taken it off to be kissed. Mira was holding her hands.

It was only a minute. Zara never knew what they said. She saw Mira say something, very quietly, with her chin up, and Priya answer, and then laugh, helplessly, and put a hand over her eyes, and say something back, and Mira did a thing with her whole face that was like watching a lamp being turned up.

Then the whistle blew again, a long, shrill, ringing note, and Priya kissed her once, quick and hard, in front of the whole platform, and got on, and the door shut. The train began to move.

It went slowly, at first, with a huge clanking sigh, and then faster, and the windows slid by, and the faces, a long blur of red and green and blue and yellow, going away down the rails, and a hand in a green glove waved from the last window and went on waving, past the signal box, round the curve, until there was only a long white plume of smoke hanging over the hills.

Nobody said a word.

The platform was very large, and very quiet. A pigeon walked along the edge. Mira stood quite still at the end, with her hands at her sides, and her back very straight, looking at the place where the train had been.

It was Elliot who got there first. They hadn't said anything. They just walked down the platform, in their long dark coat, and stood next to her, quite close, without looking at her, and put their hands in their pockets. And Finn, a moment later, on her other side, with a small, awkward cough, and Oliver, and, after a decent interval, with a gruff little noise like a person getting something out of her throat, Zara.

"I'm fine," said Mira.

"We know," said Elliot.

"She's coming back on the fourth."

"We know."

"It's only twelve days."

"Thirteen," said Zara. "If you count the day she gets in."

"I wasn't going to count it," said Mira, and she took a breath, and her voice shook, and she began, quite helplessly, to laugh. "I had it all on a list."

They stood a little longer. It was cold. After a while, quite naturally, as though somebody had given a signal, they turned, all together, and walked back up the platform, towards the carriages, with Mira in the middle.

---

McGonagall was waiting in the Hall.

She stood at the top of the dais in her green cloak, in the high, cold, echoing silence of a castle that had, in the space of an hour, become three hundred people smaller, with her hands behind her back and the winter light falling on her in long bars from the windows. The great tables were bare. The benches had been put away. The twelve Christmas trees stood in their tubs along the walls, a little thin, a little forlorn, with their baubles gleaming. And in the middle of the floor, in a tall, glittering, jumbled heap, taller than a man, was a hill of lanterns.

There were eighty-one of them. Hagrid had fetched them at dawn, on a sledge, from the ice, and every one was still burning.

"Ah," said Professor McGonagall. "The stayers."

They came in, in a rather bunched and uncertain line, stamping off snow. There was a short silence, as the twenty-three of them looked at the heap, and the heap, with its eighty-one little flames, looked placidly back.

"There is a custom," said McGonagall, "which is somewhat younger than the Walk itself. It is that the lanterns, once carried, are not taken home. They are hung. They remain in the Great Hall, from the longest night until the end of the Christmas feast, and they are collected, by those to whom they belong, in January." She paused. "I have been advised by Professor Flitwick that he has not hung a lantern in this school since the nineteen-nineties, and would like very much to see it done."

"Where?" said Zara.

"The ceiling."

"The _whole_ ceiling?"

"I believe it to be the usual place," said McGonagall.

A murmur went round. It was a good one. Zara, looking up, at the great, dark, vaulted heights of the Hall, so far above that on a stormy day the clouds ran in under the beams, felt something in her stomach turn over with a small and glorious flip.

"It's a hundred feet," she said.

"Eighty-four."

"How are we meant to—"

"There are ladders," said McGonagall. She did not smile. She looked, in fact, very grave. But there was a quality in her face, a faint, curving, dreadful thing about the corner of her mouth, that Zara had seen once before, in the Entrance Hall, on a Sunday, over a treaty. "There are hovering charms. And I understand that there are persons in this school, Miss Okonkwo, who have, from time to time, been in the habit of flying." She raised one eyebrow. "Indoors, on this one occasion, and for this one purpose, with a cautious degree of discretion, I am prepared to turn a blind eye."

For one second, nobody breathed.

"Three circuits," said McGonagall. "Not four. No Quaffle. And Miss Okonkwo, if you put a single foot through a stained-glass window, I shall be most displeased."

---

It took about four hours.

Zara had never, in her whole life, led a team through anything so fine.

She did it from the middle of the floor, with her sleeves rolled up and her broom in her hand, in the clear, cool, ringing, effortless voice that she'd kept, until recently, for the pitch. It was surprisingly easy. It was a matter of looking at the people in front of you and knowing, in the second before they did, what they were good at, and she found, to her delight and her faint astonishment, that she knew. She put the tall seventh-years on the ladders, because they could reach. She put Elliot, who was good with a Levitation Charm and whose wand hand did not tremble, in charge of the lifting, and Mira in charge of the spacing, with a long ruled line of string, and a pencil, and her whole face aglow with a quiet, absorbed, intensely private joy. She put Finn on the rope, which he did with a patient, humming competence, as though hauling lanterns up to a ceiling were the thing he'd been put on earth for. She put Oliver on the second ladder, with a very strict instruction not to look down.

"Why?" said Oliver.

"It's a long way."

"I _know_ it's a long way. That's why you've said it."

"Don't look," said Zara, kindly, "and it'll be fine."

The first-years had the low ones. Amara had marshalled them, with the bucket on her head, into a chain, a long, bobbing, whispering line of small figures in cloaks, who passed up the lanterns one at a time, from hand to hand, from the heap to the ladders, with great ceremony, like the buckets at a fire. Wren was at the end of it, with a stub of pencil, writing the names on a list. "Brennan, Isla," she was saying, in her flat clear Whitby voice. "Rose. Reyes. Silver. Rose, silver, I think there's a lilac, is that—oh, lilac." A pause. "It's very nice."

It was Finn who told them. He'd been doing it since breakfast. He had told the first-years, and the seventh-years, and the Hufflepuff with the cold, and the girl with the cello; he'd told Ansel, twice, who'd said "That's lovely," with an expression of fixed and tragic gratitude. He had told Hagrid, who was holding the base of the ladder and had said "Well, I'll be." He'd told a suit of armour. He told them now, at the top of the rope, his round face red with exertion, in a voice that rang up to the beams.

"And then he says, _the shop will keep_," said Finn. "On a line by itself."

"We _know_," said about eleven people.

"And then he says _bring warm socks_—"

"I've got three pairs," said Oliver, from the top of the second ladder, with his eyes shut.

"He's been saying that for a day," said Elliot, quietly, to Zara.

"I think he's rather pleased with himself."

"He's insufferable."

"He's," said Zara, "very nice."

"That too," said Elliot.

---

The accident came at about three.

It was a very small one. It happened in the way of things, quite suddenly, out of nothing. They had got about two-thirds of the way, and the Hall, above their heads, was already half full of light: a great, high, glowing, drifting canopy of lanterns, hung at different heights, on invisible threads, like a cloud of coloured fireflies. They shone on the stone. They shone on the banner. The whole vast space, once cold and echoing, had gone mellow and warm, like the inside of a lamp.

Oliver had come down from the ladder. It was a victory. He had got to the bottom, with his eyes tight shut, and put one foot on the floor, and stepped back, in relief, with one arm swinging, and caught the ladder with his elbow.

It was a long ladder. It went over slowly. It swung, with a long, unhurried, creaking arc, into the pile, and the pile took a great breath, and out of the very top of it, quite gently, like a bird from a nest, a small amber lantern rolled off the edge and began, with a faint flutter of its flame, to fall.

"_ELLIOT'S_—" said Finn.

Zara was already moving.

She did not think. She had never needed to. It was a thing that happened in the body, below the level of the mind: a quick lean, a kick, her broom up under her before she knew it, the floor dropping away. She went across the Hall in a long flat curve, a flash of gold in the lantern light, with the wind of her own passing in her ears. The lantern turned over, in the air, once. She stretched out one hand.

She caught it six inches off the ground.

It lay in her palm, warm and heavy, and burned. It had not flickered. The little amber flame stood up in its glass, quite steady and clear, as though nothing in the world had happened at all, and she hung there, in the air, a foot above the flagstones, with her heart going like a drum, and a gleam of the old fierce delight rising in her face.

"Show-off," said Elliot, from the floor.

"You're welcome," said Zara.

"I wasn't thanking you."

"You _were_."

"I was going to," said Elliot, with enormous dignity, and their mouth twitched, and they put out a hand, and she gave the lantern into it, with great care, across the gap.

---

They hung hers last.

There was no one to argue. They'd all stopped, one by one, as the afternoon wore on and the light went out of the windows, to look up at what they'd made; and when the last lantern but one had gone into place, a little to the left of the centre, in a long, gleaming, spiralling pattern like a shell, it was Mira, quietly, who had said, "There's a space."

It was in the very middle. It was the highest point of the whole ceiling, under the great central beam, and nobody had liked to ask.

"Go on," said Finn.

"It's meant to be—"

"It's your turn," said Elliot. "Go on."

She took it out of her pocket. It was warm. She'd forgotten, for hours, that she had it; it had lain there against her thigh, through the whole of the afternoon, burning very quietly, a bright, steady orange-red. She held it in her left hand, with the broom between her knees, and pushed off.

She went up in a long slow spiral, past the lanterns, past the beams, with the whole Hall dropping away beneath her like a sea. They shone as she went by. It was like flying through a constellation, in a long, quiet, golden dusk, with the coloured lights wheeling past: a violet, a rose, a green; a very small and ferocious purple; a black, rimmed with silver; and, further down, in a cluster, the gold and the amber and the blue.

She reached the top. She put out her hand. And she hung it, in the middle of them all, on a thread as fine as a hair, where it hung, and swung a little, and settled.

She stayed there.

She was a long way up. The Hall was a great shadowy bowl beneath her, and in the bottom of it, very small, very bright, with their faces tipped up to the ceiling, stood twenty-odd people in the lamplight: two long, thin, quite still, with their hands at their sides; one round and freckled and grinning; one with a bucket on her head, shrieking, and one, next to her, small and silent and radiant, with a pencil stuck behind her ear. Hagrid, enormous in the doorway, was blowing his nose.

She hung there in the air, in the light of a hundred lanterns, a long way from the ground.

"Zara!" called Amara, from the bottom of the world. "You look like a _star_!"

"I'm a Chaser!"

"You look like a star that's a Chaser!"

"That's," said Zara, "the nicest thing anyone's ever said."

She looked down. She looked at the floor, and at her sister, and at the whole upturned circle of faces. And she thought of a platform, and a train going away, and a Hall that had, in a morning, got a great deal emptier; and of how it looked now, with everything still left in it, and everybody, and a hundred small lights.

It was not, she realised, empty at all.

It had just been waiting for somebody to hang the lamps.
