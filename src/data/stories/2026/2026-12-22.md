The Hogwarts Express left Hogsmeade at eleven, and Zara had never in her life watched it go.

She had always been on it. For four years it had been the thing that carried her away, with her trunk in the rack and her feet on the seat and her face against the glass, and she had looked out at a small dark empty platform and thought nothing at all. It had never occurred to her that someone would be standing on it.

There were twenty-three of them in the end. They had come down from the castle in two carriages through a bright, glittering morning, with the snow squeaking under the wheels, and stood in a ragged bunch at the end of the platform in coats and scarves and mittens. They were the ones staying. There were the four of them, and Oliver; Amara and Wren, in their matching grey shirts under their cloaks, because neither had been willing to take them off; Ansel, the small second-year Slytherin, who had been told by his parents to enjoy himself and appeared to be doing it with a dogged earnestness; and a dozen others, seventh-years and thirds, a Ravenclaw with a cold, another with a cello.

The train stood by the platform, red and enormous, steam sliding off its sides. Doors banged. A porter shouted. Down the long row of windows, three hundred faces were pressed to the glass, pink-cheeked and cheerful, waving at the people who weren't coming.

"They look so _happy_," said Amara.

"They're going home."

"We're not."

"No." Zara looked down at her. She was wearing her bucket hat, a little the worse for wear. "Do you mind?"

Amara thought about it honestly, head on one side.

"I thought I would," she said. "But I've got Nettle. And you. And Wren. And Hagrid says he's going to teach me to make a hippogriff sandwich." She frowned. "I don't know what that is. But it sounds like _somebody's_ Christmas."

---

Jamie found her at the carriage door. She had a place in the last compartment, by the window, with her trunk on the rack and her cropped head out of the glass. She looked a good deal more cheerful than she usually let on. She was wearing a thick blue jumper with a snowflake on it, clearly a present, and she had something in her hand.

"I've told them," she said. "At breakfast. I said, as of this morning I am vice-captain and I have the right to tell you all when you're wrong. Marcus cheered. Katie cried. Tamsin asked if there'd be a badge."

"What did you say?"

"I said I'd make one."

She held out her hand. In it lay a small round lumpy tin-coloured thing about the size of a button, with a pin on the back. It had been cut with a bent spoon and a tin lid and a great deal of ill-judged filing, and in the middle, drawn with a much steadier hand than the cutting, was a tiny, cross-eyed, lopsided Snitch.

It was the worst thing Zara had ever seen. She took it at once.

"Pin it on yourself," said Jamie. "I've drawn blood twice."

She pinned it inside her cloak, over her heart, where nobody could see. It was, she thought, with her ears hot, the best thing she had ever been given.

"See you in January, Captain."

"See you in January."

"Don't plan anything," said Jamie. "Over the holidays. Rest. It's an order."

"I'm not going to plan—"

"You are _already_ planning," said Jamie, with total certainty, and the whistle went.

---

She saw Mira and Priya out of the corner of her eye and looked away.

It was private. They stood at the far end of the platform by the last carriage door, slightly apart, close together, in the cold white light. Priya had her trunk beside her, and her good coat, and her green scarf, and a woollen hat that Mira had knitted rather badly over the last week and that she had taken off in order to be kissed. Mira was holding her hands.

It lasted a minute. Zara never knew what they said. Mira said something quietly, chin up, and Priya answered, and then laughed, and put a hand over her eyes, and said something back, and Mira's face did a thing like a lamp being turned up.

The whistle blew again, long and shrill. Priya kissed her once, quick and hard, in front of the whole platform, and got on, and the door shut. The train began to move, slowly with a clanking sigh, and then faster, and the windows slid by, and the faces, a long blur of red and green and blue and yellow, going away down the rails. A hand in a green glove waved from the last window and went on waving, past the signal box and round the curve, until there was only a white plume hanging over the hills.

Nobody said a word.

The platform was very large and very quiet. A pigeon walked along the edge. Mira stood at the end with her hands at her sides and her back very straight, looking at the place where the train had been.

It was Elliot who got there first. They didn't say anything. They walked down the platform in their long dark coat, stood beside her, a little close, and put their hands in their pockets. Finn came on her other side, with a small awkward cough, then Oliver, and then, after a decent interval, with a noise like a person removing something from her throat, Zara.

"I'm fine," said Mira.

"We know," said Elliot.

"She's back on the fourth. It's only twelve days."

"Thirteen," said Zara. "If you count the day she gets in."

"I wasn't going to count it," said Mira, and her voice shook, and she began, quite helplessly, to laugh. "I had it all on a list."

They stood a little longer. It was cold. After a while, as if somebody had given a signal, they turned together and walked back up the platform, with Mira in the middle.

---

McGonagall was waiting in the Hall.

She stood at the top of the dais in her green cloak, in the cold echoing silence of a castle that had, in an hour, become three hundred people smaller, with her hands behind her back and the winter light falling on her in long bars. The tables were bare. The benches were put away. The twelve Christmas trees stood in their tubs a little thin and forlorn, their baubles back in place. And in the middle of the floor, in a glittering jumbled heap taller than a man, was a hill of lanterns.

There were eighty-one of them. Hagrid had fetched them at dawn from the ice on a sledge, and every one was still burning.

"Ah," said Professor McGonagall. "The stayers."

They came in bunched and uncertain, stamping off snow. There was a pause while twenty-three people looked at the heap, and the heap, with its eighty-one little flames, looked placidly back.

"There is a custom," said McGonagall, "somewhat younger than the Walk itself. The lanterns, once carried, are not taken home. They are hung. They remain in the Great Hall from the longest night until the end of the Christmas feast, and are collected by those to whom they belong in January." She paused. "Professor Flitwick advises me that he has not seen a lantern hung in this school since he was a young man, and would like very much to see it done."

"Where?" said Zara.

"The ceiling."

"The _whole_ ceiling?"

"I believe it to be the usual place."

A murmur went round. Zara looked up into the dark vaulted heights, so far above that on a stormy day the clouds ran in under the beams, and felt her stomach turn over with a small glorious flip.

"It's a hundred feet."

"Eighty-four."

"How are we meant to—"

"There are ladders," said McGonagall. She did not smile. But there was a thing at the corner of her mouth that Zara had seen once before, in the Entrance Hall, on a Sunday, over a treaty. "There are hovering charms. And I understand that there are persons in this school, Miss Okonkwo, who have been known, from time to time, to fly." One eyebrow went up. "Indoors, on this one occasion, for this one purpose, with a cautious degree of discretion, I am prepared to turn a blind eye."

For one second nobody breathed.

"Three circuits," said McGonagall. "Not four. No Quaffle. And Miss Okonkwo, if you put a single foot through a stained-glass window I shall be most displeased."

---

It took four hours, and Zara had never led a team through anything so fine.

She did it from the middle of the floor with her sleeves rolled and her broom in her hand, in the voice she had kept, till recently, for the pitch. It turned out to be easy. You looked at the people in front of you and knew, a second before they did, what they were good at. She put the tall seventh-years on the ladders. She put Elliot, who was good with a Levitation Charm and whose wand hand didn't tremble, in charge of lifting, and Mira in charge of spacing, with a long ruled string and a pencil and an expression of quiet, intensely private joy. She put Finn on the rope, which he worked with a humming, patient competence, as though hauling lanterns to a ceiling were what he'd been put on earth for. She put Oliver on the second ladder, with strict instructions not to look down.

"Why?" said Oliver.

"It's a long way."

"I _know_ it's a long way. That's why you said it."

"Don't look," said Zara, kindly, "and it'll be fine."

Amara had the first-years in a chain, bucket on her head, passing the low lanterns up from hand to hand like buckets at a fire. At the end of it Wren, with a stub of pencil, was keeping the list. "Brennan, Isla," she was saying, in her flat clear Whitby voice. "Rose. Reyes. Silver. Rose, silver, I think there's a lilac, is that—oh, lilac." A pause. "It's very nice."

Finn was telling them. He had been telling people since breakfast. He had told the first-years, the seventh-years, the Ravenclaw with the cold and the girl with the cello; he had told Ansel twice, who said "That's lovely" with fixed and tragic gratitude. He had told Hagrid, who said "Well, I'll be." He had told a suit of armour. He told them again now, from the top of the rope, red in the face, in a voice that rang off the beams.

"And then he says, _the shop will keep_. On a line by itself."

"We _know_," said about eleven people.

"And then he says _bring warm socks_—"

"I've got three pairs," said Oliver, from the second ladder, with his eyes shut.

"He's been saying that for a day," said Elliot quietly, to Zara.

"I think he's pleased with himself."

"He's insufferable."

"He's," said Zara, "very nice."

"That too," said Elliot.

---

The accident came at about three, and it was a small one. They were two-thirds done, and the Hall was half full of light: a high glowing drifting canopy of lanterns hung at different heights on invisible threads, like a cloud of coloured fireflies. It shone on the stone. It shone on the banner. The whole cold echoing space had gone mellow and warm, like the inside of a lamp.

Oliver had come down from the ladder. It was a victory. He had reached the bottom with his eyes tight shut, put one foot on the floor, and stepped back in relief with one arm swinging, and caught the ladder with his elbow.

It was a long ladder, and it went over slowly. It swung in a creaking arc into the pile, and the pile took a deep breath, and out of the top of it, gently, like a bird from a nest, rolled a small amber lantern. It tipped off the edge and began, with a flutter of flame, to fall.

"_ELLIOT'S_—" said Finn.

Zara was already moving. She did not think. It was a thing the body did below the level of the mind: a lean, a kick, her broom up under her, the floor dropping away. She crossed the Hall in a long flat curve, a flash of gold in the lantern light. The lantern turned over once in the air. She stretched out a hand.

She caught it six inches off the ground.

It lay in her palm, warm and heavy, and burned. It hadn't so much as flickered. She hung there in the air a foot above the flagstones, her heart going like a drum, and the old fierce delight rose in her face.

"Show-off," said Elliot, from the floor.

"You're welcome."

"I wasn't thanking you."

"You _were_."

"I was going to," said Elliot, with enormous dignity, and their mouth twitched, and she gave the lantern into their hand across the gap.

"Is it over?" said Oliver, from somewhere near the ladder. "Can I open them?"

"Not yet," said Zara.

---

They hung hers last. Nobody argued. As the afternoon wore on and the light went from the windows, they had all stopped, one by one, to look up at what they'd made, and when the last lantern but one had gone into place, in a long spiralling pattern like a shell, it was Mira who said quietly, "There's a space."

It was in the very middle, the highest point of the ceiling under the great central beam, and nobody had liked to ask.

"Go on," said Finn.

"It's meant to be—"

"It's your turn," said Elliot. "Go on."

It was the last one left in the heap, and she had kept away from it all afternoon, the way you keep away from the last biscuit. It was warm, burning very quietly, orange-red. She picked it up, held it in her left hand with the broom between her knees, and pushed off.

She went up in a slow spiral, past the lanterns, past the beams, with the whole Hall dropping away beneath her like a sea. They shone as she passed. It was like flying through a constellation: a violet, a rose, a green; a small and ferocious purple; a black, rimmed with silver; and further down, in a cluster, the gold and the amber and the blue.

She reached the top. She put out her hand and hung it, in the middle of them all, on a thread as fine as a hair, where it swung a little and settled.

She stayed there. It was a long way up. The Hall was a great shadowy bowl beneath her, and in the bottom of it, very small, with their faces tipped up, stood twenty-odd people in the lamplight: two long thin ones with their hands at their sides, one round and freckled and grinning, one with a bucket on her head, shrieking, and one beside her, small and silent, with a pencil behind her ear. Hagrid, enormous in the doorway, was blowing his nose.

"Zara!" called Amara, from the bottom of the world. "You look like a _star_!"

"I'm a Chaser!"

"You look like a star that's a Chaser!"

"That," said Zara, "is the nicest thing anyone's ever said."

Far below, a small voice, with its eyes shut, rose from the foot of a ladder. "Is it _over_ now?"

"Nearly," said eleven people, and Zara stayed where she was, up among the lanterns, for a little longer.
