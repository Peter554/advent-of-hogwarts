The note from Professor Sprout was waiting on his pillow when Finn got up, and it said:

_Mr MacLeod. The late bed opens tonight, between midnight and one. You will remember what I said about the lamps. If you can find three others, bring them to Greenhouse Four at a quarter to twelve. If you cannot, I shall manage with the Gnome. —P. Sprout_

He read it twice, in his pyjamas, with one sock on, and sat down on the edge of the bed.

It was exactly the sort of note Sprout wrote: short, dry, and leaving the next move entirely to you. It had also arrived, he couldn't help thinking, at the right moment. He had been lying awake since half past one going over Wednesday: the way Mira had stood in the middle of the floor with a plate of shortbread saying _it's fine_ in her careful voice, and the way Zara had looked at him from her cushion, as if he were the one person in the room who could say something. And he had said, _I'll sit on the floor_.

He'd opened the door for her. It was all he could think of. He had stood in the doorway afterwards, listening to the kettle going on the hob, with a feeling in his stomach like a dropped stitch.

_Four lamps_, he thought.

It didn't solve anything, and it wasn't meant to. But it had a shape, which was more than he'd had at two in the morning, and it was something to do, which beat lying still. He got up and found the other sock.

---

He asked Mira first, because he knew where she would be.

She was at the end of the Ravenclaw table with a boiled egg, a notebook and the frowning, slightly unravelled look of someone who hadn't slept, and when she saw him coming down the long blue aisle she put down her spoon.

"Finn."

"Hi. It's a small thing. Is it all right if—"

"Yes," said Mira.

"You don't know what I'm going to ask."

"No. Yes." She had gone pink. She looked at her egg. "I mean, I think I'd _rather_ know before I say it, it's not something I've decided, and in principle—" She stopped, and took one very small, very deliberate breath. "I'm doing it again. What is it?"

He told her in about eight sentences: frostblooms, the late bed, midnight, four lamps at four corners. She listened to the end and didn't write anything down.

"Yes," she said. "Please."

"It's a school night."

"I know."

"It might be cold."

"Finn." She lifted her eyes. They were dark and tired and rather bright. "Can I ask you something first?"

"Of course."

"Are you all right? Properly? I haven't got a list, I just wanted to know." She hesitated. "I should have asked on Wednesday."

It wasn't what he'd expected, and it caught him just under the ribs. He looked at the notebook with its margin full of tiny careful crossings-out, at the braid, at the egg, at the plain, anxious face of someone who had written _Ask_ on a piece of paper and was trying very hard to do what it said.

"I'm all right," he said. "Tired. I've got something on my mind that I'll tell you tonight, if that's okay."

"That's okay."

"And I'm all right about Wednesday. I was only worried about you."

"I know," said Mira. "That's what made it worse."

She reached into her robes and took out a small card, folded once. It was his name card from the ring of cushions, a little bent. Under _Finn_, in pencil, she'd added _(sit wherever)_.

"I've been carrying it about," she said. "I didn't know what else to do with it."

He put it in his top pocket, where it stuck out like a handkerchief, and found he was grinning at her with his whole face.

---

Zara took longer.

He found her on the steps of the Quidditch changing rooms just after four, with her boots in her lap and her braids tied up in a green scarf, looking at nothing across the white pitch. It was snowing a little. The hoops stood against a sky the colour of old pewter, and a handful of Gryffindors in red robes were straggling off the field.

He explained. She listened with her head on one side. When he got to _midnight_ she looked down at her boots.

"On a school night."

"It's the last of them. They only open once."

Her thumb moved on a lace.

"Is Mira going?"

"She said yes."

"Right." A snowflake settled on the back of her hand and melted. "We had a bit of a—" She shrugged, as if the sentence had got away from her. "It was a bit rubbish. On Wednesday."

"It was a bit."

"I made a joke," said Zara. "I thought it was funny."

"It was funny."

"It wasn't, though. It was the _wrong funny_." She rubbed her face with her sleeve. "I don't like it when it's weird. With us. I'm no good at it."

"Come and hold a lamp," said Finn.

She gave him a long look. Then she nodded, once, and said, "A quarter to twelve."

"A quarter to."

"I'll bring biscuits," said Zara, in a voice that wobbled very slightly, and was herself again.

---

He found Elliot last, by the simple method of not looking.

They came up behind him in the corridor outside Charms at the end of the afternoon, tall and dark-clad and rather startled, with a great deal of blue on one cuff. It was ultramarine, all the way up the left sleeve in a long dried splash as if they had dipped an arm in the sky, and in their hair, and in a thin line under one fingernail, and on the cover of the sketchbook they were clutching to their chest.

"Don't," said Elliot, before he'd said a word. "It's a long story, it's not a good one, and I've got to be somewhere in ten minutes."

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were looking."

"I was looking at the paint."

"That's _looking_." But the corner of their mouth had moved. "What do you want, MacLeod? I'm busy."

He told them. They listened with a face that gave nothing away, and Finn, who had had five years' practice at reading Elliot, thought for a moment they were going to say no. It was in the set of the jaw, and in the way their eyes went briefly to the ceiling.

"Fine," said Elliot.

"Is that a—"

"It's a yes. Quarter to twelve. Four lamps." They said it quickly, to the air, like a cough. Then, to his surprise, they put out a hand and touched him on the elbow, very briefly, and said, "Thanks for asking."

It was gone before he could think about it. A flick of blue, a swirl of coat, and they had turned the corner.

---

Greenhouse Four at midnight was the most beautiful place Finn had ever stood in.

It wasn't like the first time. The frost on the glass had gone in the afternoon thaw, and the night had come down so clear and still that the roof seemed made of stars. The air was cold, with a faint high note in it, as though somebody had touched a glass. In the bed at the far end, in rows, the late frostblooms stood waiting, taller than the early ones, on silver-grey stems, with buds as round and tight as pearls.

There was a lamp at each corner of the bed.

They were old copper things with glass chimneys, hung on iron hooks, and Sprout had left them lit. She had left a flask, a basket and a note as well. The note said, _Take your time. Do not hurry them. I shall be in the staff room, if the sky falls. —P. Sprout_

"She isn't coming?" said Zara.

"She says it's better with four."

"She's got a thing about four," said Elliot.

"She's got a thing about everything," said Mira. "It's a Herbologist's prerogative."

They stood in the doorway in a huddle of coats and scarves. They had come separately, from different directions, and as the cold came in round their ankles nobody seemed to know what to do with their hands.

Zara did. She had a biscuit tin under one arm, with a label on the lid in thick marker: _O.W.L.s (Oat With Lemon)_. She held it out to Mira at arm's length, without a word, with her chin up.

Mira looked at the label for some time.

"Is that a technical term?" she said.

"It's a recipe."

"It's a pun."

"It's _both_." Zara went on holding it out. Mira took one. She ate it, in full, with great seriousness, and then said, "Lemon is a bold choice," and took another, and Zara let out a breath that went up in the cold air like a small cloud.

"Corners," said Finn.

Mira took the lamp nearest the door. Zara went to the far end. Elliot, with a lamp in each hand for a moment, set one down and took the other to the left, and Finn took the last, on the right, and stood with the warm brass handle in his glove and the long dark bed between them.

It was silent. A drip fell somewhere. The four small flames burned steady in their chimneys and the whole bed lay lit from four sides in a clean gold glow, like something in a painting.

At five past twelve the first bud opened.

It began at the top. It shuddered and unrolled, and a single silver petal peeled back from the heart, and then a second and a third, until the whole flower stood in the light like a star. It rang as it opened, a long, clear, trembling note, higher than the early ones', that hung in the air for several seconds after the last petal settled.

"Oh," said Zara, very softly.

"Don't pick it," said Finn. "Not yet. Wait for the fourth."

The second opened under Elliot's lamp with a sound like a small bell, and the third in front of Mira, with a chime that was almost a word. By the fourth nobody was talking. You couldn't, he thought, not loudly, not in a voice that mattered. You could only stand and hold your lamp and let it be.

Elliot hissed. "Hot," they said. The brass handle of their lamp had been warming steadily, and they swapped it from hand to hand, and shook the one that wasn't holding it.

Mira, across the bed, was already feeling in her robes. She came out with a long sheet of folded parchment, three inks, a table, a footnote. She crossed the bed on tiptoe between the flowers and wrapped Elliot's handle in it, twice round, and tucked in the end.

"Draft three," she said.

Elliot looked at the lamp, and at the schedule going slowly brown at the corners, and then at her. "Finally," they said, "a use for it."

Zara made a noise like a kettle. Mira, with her back to them, was pink to the ears. And a frostbloom in front of Finn, as if it had been waiting for exactly that, rang out loud and bright and opened all at once.

"It had a question mark on your pasties," said Mira, to the flowers.

"I don't make pasties."

"I _know_," said Mira. "That's why it was a question mark."

"I like pasties," said Elliot.

"Right," said Zara. "That's everybody sorry. Biscuit."

---

It went on like that for an hour, and not as a conversation he'd have recognised. It was more of a slow trade, small things passed from hand to hand across the dark. Mira told them about Dr Lund and the trunk of thousand-year-old bindrunes, with her hands going so that her lamp swung, and Zara said, "A _trunk_? She's given you a trunk?" and Elliot said, "It's the first time in history anyone's been delighted by a trunk." Zara told them about Jamie and the silence in the changing room and said, "I think I'm going to ask her something. On Saturday," and nobody asked what.

And Elliot, in the middle of a sentence about nothing, said, "I've been painting six pennants."

"Six what?"

"For the lake path. McGonagall asked me. Tuesday, in the Hall." They were looking at their lamp. "I wasn't hiding it. Exactly. Every time I thought about telling you, it felt like putting it on the table. And if I put it on the table, someone would look."

"We'd have looked," said Mira. "We'd have looked _very hard_."

"That's what I mean."

Mira opened her mouth, and Finn could see her start to say _I'm sorry_. She shut it again. She took a breath.

"What colour?" she said.

Elliot blinked. "What?"

"The pennants. What colour are they?"

"Blue." A pause. "So much blue. I never want to see blue again."

"And the pattern?"

"Lanterns. Small ones. One on each, upside down by mistake on the third, and I decided to leave it."

"Can I see one?"

"Monday," said Elliot. "By the lake. Not before." They looked up from their lamp for the first time all night. "And you're not allowed to say anything intelligent."

"I'll try," said Mira. "I can't promise."

They passed the biscuits round the bed, over the open flowers, and it astonished Finn how much better it was. It wasn't mended, exactly. It was only that the thing in the room that had been tight and thin had stretched, and given, and let in some air.

---

The last bloom opened at ten to one.

It was the one at the end of the bed, by his own lamp. He had been watching it for twenty minutes. It was a little smaller than the others, and rounder, and it opened in one easy curl, rang a single plain note, and was still.

He had his hand on the warm brass. In the glow he could see the other three across the length of the bed, each lit gold from beneath, and each looking at him quietly, as if they'd been waiting.

"I've got a thing," said Finn.

"You said," said Mira.

"It's good. I think it's good." He cleared his throat. "Professor Sprout put me in for a placement. At Fenmoor Gardens, in the fens. Six weeks in July. They take six students. She did it in October without telling me." It came out in a rush. "I've got to say yes or no by the middle of January."

For a moment nobody said anything.

"_Finn_," said Mira.

"Oh my _days_," said Zara. She had put her lamp on the ground and thrown up both arms. "Oh my _days_. That's _amazing_—"

"Of course she did," said Elliot. They were smiling, a real one, rare and helpless, and it changed their whole face. "You're the only person who's ever got a Mimbulus to say hello."

"It wasn't a hello."

"It was a very definite hello."

"I don't know what to do," said Finn, laughing, and his lamp was rattling in its chimney because his hand was not quite steady. "It's July. It's the shop. I've got the second bench, I've got the rowan, Dad's been so—and I don't know how to tell him that I want—" He stopped. "Sorry."

"Don't say sorry," said Zara, at once, fiercely.

"Have you told him?" said Mira.

"No."

"Are you going to?"

He looked at the last flower. It stood in the lamplight with its petals spread, its note quite gone, a cold silver dew beginning to gather at its edge. He thought of a letter he hadn't written, and of Tibby's ginger biscuits, and of a low voice saying _he'll want to know_.

"Tonight," said Finn.

---

It was past two when he sat down to write.

The dormitory was dark and warm. The other four boys were asleep with their curtains half drawn, breathing like a slow tide. On the table by his bed a candle burned in a saucer, and beside it, propped against the pillow on the quilt, the lantern was lit.

It wasn't as bright as it had been. It had been dimming for days, and now it burned low and steady, like a banked fire, throwing a small warm ring across the bedspread.

He took a sheet of parchment and sat a while with the pen above it. Then, because there wasn't a good way to say it, and because Dibby in a tea-towel hat had said a request only asks them to hold the door, he began.

_Dear Dad,_

_I've been trying to write this letter for two weeks._

_Professor Sprout has put my name forward for a placement at Fenmoor Gardens. It's in the fens, near Ely. It's six weeks in July and half of August. They take six students. I'd like to take it._

_I know what July is. I know about the second bench, and the rowan. I think about it every day. I'm not saying no to the shop, I don't think I could, I don't know what it would be like not to be there. I'm only saying that I'd like to find out what I'm like when I'm somewhere else. I'd rather tell you now than in January._

_Please don't answer quickly. Take your time. Like with a wand._

_Love, Finn._

_P.S. Oliver says to say it's dragon heartstring._

He read it through. He didn't change a word.

He sealed it with a stub of red wax and wrote the address on the front in his big round hand, and sat with it in his lap for a long time while the candle burned down and the stars went round and the night, at its own pace, began to turn to morning.

At a quarter to eight, at the window, a tawny owl with a bent left feather arrived on the sill, glared at him, and put out its leg.

He tied it on.

He watched it go, out across the grey grounds, low over the white lawn and the frozen lake, a small brown shape in the huge pale light, towards the north and the hills and a long low house with a bench by the window, where somebody would be up already, lighting the stove.
