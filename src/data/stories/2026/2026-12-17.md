The note from Professor Sprout was waiting on his pillow when Finn got up, and it said:

_Mr MacLeod. The late bed opens tonight, between midnight and one. You will remember what I said about the lamps. If you can find three others, bring them to Greenhouse Four at a quarter to twelve. If you cannot, I shall manage with the Gnome. —P. Sprout_

He read it twice, in his pyjamas, with one sock on, and sat down on the edge of the bed.

It was a very good note. It was exactly the sort of note that Sprout wrote: short, dry, unfussy, with a faint air of leaving the next move entirely to you. And it had arrived, he couldn't help thinking, at precisely the right moment. He had been lying awake since half past one, going over a thing in his mind. It was the sound of a door, in a cold room, closing very gently; the way Mira had stood in the middle of the floor with a plate of shortbread, saying _it's fine_ in her particular careful voice; the way Zara had looked at him, from her cushion, as if he were the one person in the room who could possibly say something, and he had said, _I'll sit on the floor_.

He had opened the door for her. It was all he could think of. He'd stood there, in the corridor, after she had gone, with the cards still on the cushions and the kettle going on the hob, and a feeling in his stomach like having dropped a stitch.

_Four lamps_, he thought.

It didn't solve anything. It wasn't meant to. But it had a shape to it, which was more than he'd had at two o'clock in the morning, and it was something that needed doing, which was better than lying still.

He got up, and found the other sock.

---

He asked Mira first, because he knew where she would be.

She was at the Ravenclaw table, at the end, with a boiled egg and a notebook and the grave, frowning, slightly undone look of someone who had not slept enough, and when she saw him coming down the long blue aisle between the benches she put down her spoon.

"Finn."

"Hi. I—it's a small thing. Is it all right if—"

"Yes," said Mira.

"You don't know what I'm going to ask."

"I don't mind. Yes." She had gone slightly pink. She looked at her egg. "I mean, I should say, I'd like to know what it is first, it's not a thing I've decided, I think I'd _rather_ know before I say it—" She stopped. She took a very small, very deliberate breath. "I'm sorry. I'm doing it again. What is it?"

He told her. It was about eight sentences, about frostblooms and the late bed, and midnight, and four lamps at the four corners. She listened to the end. She didn't write anything down.

"Yes," she said, "please."

"It's on a school night."

"I know."

"It might be cold."

"Finn." She lifted her eyes. They were dark, and tired, and rather bright. "Can I ask you something first?"

"Of course."

"Are you all right? Properly? I don't mean, I'm not—" She spread her hands. "I haven't got a list. I just wanted to know. I should have asked, on Wednesday, and I didn't. I'd like to now."

It was not at all what he'd been expecting. It hit him in some tender, unguarded place just under the ribs, and for a moment he couldn't say anything. He looked at her: at the notebook with its margin full of tiny, careful crossings-out, at the long dark braid, at the egg. At the plain, anxious, wide-open face of a person who had written _Ask_ on a piece of paper and was now trying, very hard, to do what it said.

"I'm all right," he said. "I'm tired. And I've got a thing on my mind that I'll tell you tonight, if that's okay."

"That's okay."

"And I'm all right about Wednesday. Honestly. I was only worried about you."

"I know," said Mira. "That's what made it worse."

She smiled. It was a small, wavering, rueful sort of smile, and he found that he was grinning back at her, quite helplessly, with the whole of his face.

---

Zara took longer.

He found her on the steps of the Quidditch changing rooms, just after four, with her boots in her lap and her braids tied up under a green scarf, looking at nothing in particular across the white of the pitch. It was snowing a little. The hoops stood up against a sky the colour of old pewter, and a handful of Gryffindors in red robes were straggling off the field in twos and threes.

He explained. She listened with her head on one side. When he got to _four lamps_, she said nothing; when he got to _midnight_, she looked down at her boots.

"On a school night."

"It's the last of them. They only open once."

There was a pause. Her thumb moved on the lace.

"Is Mira going?"

"She said yes."

"Right." Another pause. A small snow-flake settled on the back of her hand and melted. "It's only, we had a—it was a bit—" She stopped, and shrugged, rather helplessly, as if the sentence had got away from her. "It was a bit rubbish. On Wednesday."

"It was a bit."

"I didn't know what to say."

"No."

"I made a joke," said Zara. "I thought it was funny."

"It was funny."

"It wasn't, though. It was the wrong—it was the _wrong funny_." She rubbed her face with her sleeve. "I don't like it when it's weird. With us. I'm no good at it."

"Come and hold a lamp," said Finn.

She looked at him. It was a long look. And then she nodded, once, and put her boots on the step beside her, and said, "A quarter to twelve."

"A quarter to."

"I'll bring biscuits," said Zara, in a voice that wobbled very slightly, and was herself again.

---

He'd found Elliot last, in the end, by the simple method of not looking.

They'd come up behind him in the corridor, outside the Charms classroom, at the end of the afternoon: a tall, thin, dark-clad figure with a rather startled air and a great deal of blue on the cuff. It was ultramarine. It was all the way up the left sleeve, in a long dried splash, as though somebody had dipped a hand in the sky, and it was in their hair, and in a thin line under one fingernail, and on the cover of the sketchbook they were clutching to their chest.

"Don't," said Elliot, before he had said a word. "It's a long story, and it's not a good one, and I've got to be somewhere in about ten minutes."

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were looking."

"I was looking at the paint."

"That's _looking_." But the corner of their mouth had moved. They shifted the sketchbook. "What do you want, MacLeod? I'm busy."

He told them. They listened with a face that gave nothing away. When he had finished there was a short, dry, considering pause, and Finn, who had had five years of practice in reading Elliot, thought, for a moment, that they were going to say no. It was in the set of the jaw. It was in the way their eyes had gone, briefly, to the ceiling.

"Fine," said Elliot.

"Is that a—"

"It's a yes. Quarter to twelve. Four lamps." They said it quickly, to the air, as if it had been a cough. And then, to his enormous surprise, they put out a hand, and touched him, very briefly, on the elbow, and said, "Thanks for asking."

It was gone before he could think about it. A flick of blue, and a swirl of long dark coat, and they had turned the corner.

---

Greenhouse Four, at midnight, was the most beautiful place that Finn had ever been.

It was not like the first time. The frost on the glass had gone, in the thaw of the afternoon, and the night had come down clear and still and so full of stars that the whole of the roof seemed to be made of them. The air was cold. There was a faint, high, singing note in it, as though somebody had touched a glass. And in the bed at the far end, in rows, in the dark soil, the late frostblooms stood waiting, taller than the others, with long silver-grey stems, and buds as tight and round as pearls.

There was a lamp at each of the four corners of the bed.

They were old, battered, copper things, with glass chimneys, hung on iron hooks, and Sprout had left them lit. She had left, too, a flask, a note, and a basket. The note said, _Take your time. Do not hurry them. I shall be in the staff room, if the sky falls. —P. Sprout_

"She isn't coming?" said Zara.

"She says it's better with four."

"She's got a thing about four," said Elliot.

"She's got a thing about _everything_," said Mira. "It's a Herbologist's prerogative."

They stood, for a moment, in the doorway, in a little huddle, in their coats and scarves. It was a quarter to twelve. They'd come separately, all of them, from different directions, and for a second or two, as the cold came in around their ankles, nobody seemed to know what to do with their hands.

Then Finn said, "Corners," and they went.

Mira took the one nearest the door. Zara went to the far end. Elliot, with a lamp in each hand for a moment, set one down and took the other to the left, and Finn took the last, on the right, and stood there with the warm brass handle in his glove and the long dark bed between them.

It was silent. A drip fell somewhere. The four small flames burned steady in their chimneys, and the whole length of the bed lay lit from four sides, in a clean, gold, shadowless glow, like something in a painting.

At five past twelve the first bud opened.

It began at the top. It shuddered, and unrolled, and a single silver petal peeled back from the heart of it, and then a second, and a third, until the whole thing stood in the light like a star. It rang as it opened: a long, clear, trembling note, higher than the others, that hung in the air for several seconds after the last petal had settled.

"Oh," said Zara, very softly.

"Don't pick it," said Finn. "Not yet. Wait for the fourth."

It was the second that was the loveliest. It opened under Elliot's lamp, with a sound like a small bell; and then the third, in front of Mira, with a chime that was almost a word. By the fourth, they were not talking at all. It was the curious quality of the thing, he thought. You couldn't speak, not loudly, not in a voice that mattered. You could only stand, and hold your lamp, and let it be.

It was the standing that did it.

It was half an hour before anybody said anything, and when it came it was Zara. She said it to the bed.

"I'm sorry about the O.W.L. thing."

"It's fine," said Mira.

"It isn't. It was dumb. It's only that I'd been running for forty minutes, and I'd had a bad practice, and I came in and it was all so, so _tidy_." She swallowed. "And I thought, she's done this for us. And I thought, I'd like to do the thing back. And I didn't know how, and so I made a joke instead. It's what I do."

"I'm sorry about the schedule."

"It was a good schedule."

"It had a question mark on your pasties."

"I don't make pasties."

"I _know_," said Mira. "That's why it was a question mark."

There was a small, startled pause. And then Elliot, from the far side of the bed, said in a very dry voice, "I like pasties," and Zara snorted, and Mira laughed, a thin, shaky, helpless sort of laugh, and a frostbloom in front of Finn, as if it had been waiting for exactly that, rang out loud and bright and opened all at once.

---

It went on like that for an hour.

It was not a conversation, in any way he'd have recognised. It was more like a long, slow trade of small things, passed from hand to hand across the dark. Mira told them about Dr Lund, and the trunk, and the eleven-hundred-year-old bindrunes, with her hands going in the air, so that her lamp swung; and Zara said, "A trunk? She's given you a trunk?" and Elliot said, "It's the first time in history that anyone has been delighted by a trunk." Zara told them about Jamie, and the silence in the changing room, and said, "I think I'm going to ask her something. On Saturday," and nobody asked what. And Elliot, after a long time, in the middle of a sentence about nothing, said, "I've been painting six pennants."

"Six what?"

"For the lake path. McGonagall asked. On Tuesday, in the Hall. I should have said." They were looking at their lamp. "I wasn't hiding it. Exactly. It was a sort of—I don't know. Every time I thought about telling you, it felt like putting it on the table. And then I thought, if I put it on the table, someone would look."

"We'd have looked," said Mira. "We'd have looked _very hard_."

"That's what I mean."

"I'm sorry I didn't ask."

"I'm sorry I didn't say."

"Oh, for goodness' sake," said Zara. "We're all sorry. Can we be sorry and have a biscuit?"

They had biscuits. They passed them round the bed, in the lamplight, over the open flowers, and it was extraordinary to Finn how very much better it was. It wasn't mended, exactly. It was only that the thing in the room that had been tight and thin had somehow stretched, and given, and let in the air.

---

The last of them opened at ten to one.

It was the one at the end of the bed, by his own lamp. He'd been watching it for twenty minutes. It was a little smaller than the others, and rounder, and it opened all in one movement, in a long, easy, unhurried curl, and rang a single, plain, clear note, and was still.

He was holding his lamp. He had his hand on the warm brass. In the glow of it, across the length of the bed, he could see their three faces, Mira's, Zara's and Elliot's, each lit gold from beneath, and each looking at him, quite quietly, as though they had been waiting.

"I've got a thing," said Finn.

"You said," said Mira.

"It's good. I think. I think it's good." He cleared his throat. "Professor Sprout put me in for a placement. At Fenmoor Gardens, in the fens. It's six weeks in July. They take six students. She did it in October without telling me." It came out in a rush. "I've got to say yes or no by the middle of January."

For a moment nobody said anything at all.

"_Finn_," said Mira.

"Oh my _days_," said Zara. She'd put her lamp on the ground. She'd thrown up both arms. "Oh my _days_. That's, Finn, that's _amazing_—"

"Of course she did," said Elliot. They were smiling. It was a real one, a rare one, quite helpless, and it changed the whole of their face. "Of course she did. You're the only person who's ever got a Mimbulus to say hello."

"It wasn't a hello."

"It was a very definite hello."

"I don't know," said Finn, and he was laughing now, and also, somehow, to his horror, crying a little, with his lamp in one hand and his other pressed to his mouth. "I don't know what to do. It's July. It's the shop. I've got the second bench, I've got the rowan, Dad's been so—and I don't know how to tell him that I want—" He stopped. He wiped his face on his sleeve. "Sorry."

"Don't say sorry," said Zara, at once, fiercely.

"Have you told him?" said Mira.

"No."

"Are you going to?"

He looked at the last flower. It stood in the lamplight with its petals spread, and its note was quite gone, and a faint, cold, silver dew had begun to gather on its edge. He thought of a letter in his pocket. He thought of the ginger biscuits, and a greenhouse floor, and a long, low, steady voice saying, _He'll want to know_.

"Tonight," said Finn.

---

It was past two when he sat down to write.

The dormitory was dark and warm. The other four boys were asleep, with their curtains half drawn, and the breathing of them went on in the dark like a slow tide. On the table by his bed a candle burned in a saucer. Beside it, on the quilt, where he had propped it against the pillow, the lantern was lit.

It was not burning as brightly as it had. It had been dimming for days, by degrees, and now it was a low, steady, clear gold, like a banked fire, quite calm, throwing a small warm ring across the bedspread.

He took a sheet of parchment. He sat for a while with the pen above it. And then, because there wasn't any good way to say it, and because Dibby, in a tea-towel hat, had said that a request only asks them to hold the door, he began.

_Dear Dad,_

_I've been trying to write this letter for two weeks._

_Professor Sprout has put my name forward for a placement at Fenmoor Gardens. It's in the fens, near Ely. It's six weeks in July and half of August. They take six students. I'd like to take it._

_I know what July is. I know about the second bench, and the rowan. I think about it every day. I'm not saying no to the shop, I don't think I could, I don't know what it would be like not to be there. I'm only saying that I'd like to find out what I'm like when I'm somewhere else. I'd rather tell you now than in January._

_Please don't answer quickly. Take your time. Like with a wand._

_Love, Finn._

_P.S. Oliver says to say it's dragon heartstring._

He read it through. He did not change a word.

He sealed it with a stub of red wax, and wrote the address on the front, in his big, round, careful hand, and then sat, with it in his lap, for a very long time, as the candle burned down, and the stars went round, and the long night, at its own slow pace, began to turn to morning.

At a quarter to eight, at the window, a tawny owl with a bent left feather arrived on the sill, and glared at him, and put out its leg.

He tied it on.

He watched it go. It flew out across the grey grounds, low over the white lawn and the frozen lake, a small brown shape in the huge pale light, towards the north, and the hills, and a long, low, warm house with a bench by the window, where somebody would be up already, and lighting the stove.
