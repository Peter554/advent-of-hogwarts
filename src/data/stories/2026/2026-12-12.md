Elliot found the note under their plate at breakfast.

It was a small square of parchment, folded in four, with their name on the outside in a neat, upright, familiar hand. They'd have known it anywhere. It had once, in the third year, corrected the spelling of "grandiloquent" in the margin of an essay of theirs, in red, with a small apologetic smiley face.

They unfolded it under the table.

_I saw. No list. No questions. I'm in the library until three if you want me, and I'm not if you don't. —M._

Elliot read it once. They read it again. Then they folded it back along its creases, and put it in the inside pocket of their jacket, next to the sketchbook, and ate a piece of toast with great concentration, in a Great Hall that was loud with the noise of three hundred people planning to go to Hogsmeade.

They had not, in the end, talked to their mother the night before. They'd stood in the little anteroom behind Slughorn's hall, among a lot of coats, for about four minutes, under a poor gas lamp, and she had said, in her quick, level, impatient voice, "I have been on a train for eleven hours and your professor has given me three glasses of a thing he calls mead. I am not able, tonight, to say a sentence correctly in either of my languages." She'd put one hand to the bridge of her nose. "Tomorrow. After I have lectured. We will walk. Yes?"

"Yes," Elliot had said.

"Good." And she had stood there for a second, in the lamplight, with her coat over her arm, and looked at them in a way that they could not read at all, and said, "You've cut your hair," and Elliot had said, "In February," and she had said, "Yes. It suits you," and that had been all.

---

The lecture was at nine.

It was held in the Potions classroom, a long, cold, stone room in the dungeons with tall windows set high up in the wall, and by five to, every one of the twenty-two fifth years who took the subject was in their seat. Nobody was talking. It was unusual. Slughorn had said that this was a rare opportunity; the older students had said that you did not, as a rule, get a Master of the Apothecaries' Guild to come and stand in front of a class on a Saturday morning, and there was a feeling in the air, a small, thin, attentive hum, like the feeling before an examination.

Elliot sat at the back. They'd chosen the seat, and then wished they hadn't. They had their notebook open on the desk and a pencil in their hand, and the pencil was moving, a little, without their having told it to.

She came in at nine.

She hadn't brought a note. She hadn't brought anything but a small black leather case, which she set on the bench and unlocked with a snap, and a set of silver tongs, and a flask of something clear. She looked, Elliot thought, as she always did before work, like a person who'd had a cold bath: sharp, upright, calm, pared down to what was needed. She wore a plain grey dress and a long black apron, and her hair was pushed back from her face with one pin, and she didn't smile.

"Good morning," said Lucia Novak. "I understand that you are fifth years. That means you are fifteen. That means that you believe, at this moment, that you know everything about Potions that there is to know, and I would like, if I may, to spend the next two hours changing your minds. I will try to be quick."

There was a small, appreciative ripple.

"Today I am going to make a draught," she said, "which I have made perhaps four thousand times. It is called the Draught of Mending Sleep. It is given to patients who have been through a great shock, to the body or the mind, and it does one thing. It lets them rest. That is all. It is not glamorous. It will not win you a prize. It is a bottle of something blue that a very tired person drinks at the end of a very bad day."

She turned to the cauldron.

And Elliot, at the back of the room, felt something in their chest that they hadn't expected, and didn't have a name for, as they watched her hands.

---

They were extraordinary hands.

Elliot knew this. They had always known it, in the way that you know things about your own house. But they had not watched them, properly, with the whole of their attention, since they were about nine. She moved with no wasted gesture. She cut a root in two strokes and the pieces fell in a line. She lifted a flask by the base, not the neck, and tipped it so the contents slid out in a single clean ribbon and met the surface of the cauldron without a ripple. She stirred, and the potion, which had been the colour of weak tea, went a pale, clear, trembling blue.

"This is where it turns," said their mother.

She'd stopped stirring. She was holding the silver spoon in the air, just above the cauldron, with her wrist bent and her fingers loose. She was speaking, quite quietly, to nobody in particular, and the whole class had leaned in.

"It is a very small moment. A second, perhaps two. You have to feel it. The potion is thinking whether it will be what you wish. It is not yet sure. If you hurry it, it is not a draught, it is just a liquid. If you wait too long it is the same. And at that moment, you must make a decision, and it is entirely yours, and there is nobody who can make it for you." She smiled, very slightly, and it was the first time. "I have done it four thousand times. And every time, I think: I do not know. I do not know if it will turn. And then it does."

She lowered the spoon. The blue deepened, and went through the whole of the cauldron like a breath.

There was a silence. It was the sort of silence that has a sound.

And Elliot, in the back row, with a pencil in their hand and a pulse in their throat, looked down at their notebook, and found they had drawn it. They'd drawn the hand: the long, loose, hovering fingers, the bent wrist, the spoon held in the air above the cauldron and the thread of blue that rose from it. It was a very quick drawing. It had taken them perhaps forty seconds. And it was, quite possibly, the best thing that they had ever done.

They knew exactly what they loved about it. They loved _that_. The turning. The little held moment when a thing was not yet decided. They loved the precision, the stillness, the way a pair of hands could say, without a word, _wait_.

They did not love the part that came after. It was the patients. It was the tired person at the end of the bad day, the person for whom the whole thing was done, whom she talked about with such care. Elliot was not, at heart, someone who wanted to fix people. They wanted to look at people. They wanted to draw the way a person's hands rested when they were thinking, and the way a coat sat on a shoulder. They had known that for a long time, and it was one thing to know it and another to see it laid out, in the grey Saturday light, in front of the person you most wanted to be good enough for.

The class applauded.

It was a real, warm, startled sort of applause, ragged at first and then thunderous, with a few of the boys at the front thumping their desks. Lucia Novak stood at the cauldron, with the spoon in her hand, and she looked at them all as if somebody had just told her a very unlikely joke.

"Oh," she said. "That is not necessary."

It was said so honestly that the applause doubled.

---

She found them afterwards, in the corridor, in the thin crowd of students going up the stairs.

"Elliot."

"Mama."

"I thought," she said, "that you might not want to have lunch with Professor Slughorn."

"He's very—"

"Yes. He is very." She put on her coat, a long, dark, severe one, and turned up the collar. She did not look at them. "I have two hours before I must go. I should like to see the lake, if it is still there."

"It's still there."

"Then we will walk," said their mother. "Yes?"

---

It was cold. It was the sort of cold that gets in by the wrists. The sky over the grounds was low and white, and the lake lay under it like a sheet of beaten pewter, flat and unmoving, with a rim of ice along the edge and the black bones of the trees on the far bank. They walked down to it by the long, trampled, snowy path, side by side, a foot apart, with their breath going up in two clouds. For a long time neither of them said a word.

"I have been wanting," said Lucia Novak, at last, "to say something to you for several weeks. And I have been trying to find it, and I find that it does not exist. It is not in either language." She stopped at the edge of the water. "So I will do it badly. I hope you will forgive me."

"You don't have to."

"I do." She had her hands in her pockets. She was looking at the lake. "Last Monday I opened a drawer. It was the drawer in the dresser, in the kitchen, where we put the post that is not urgent. And in it there was a photograph. From your ball." She breathed out. "I had not looked at it. It came in January, in an envelope, with no letter, and I put it away. I do not know why. I think I was afraid of what it would say."

Elliot said nothing.

"I took it out. I put it on the dresser, where I could see it. And I looked at it for a week." She turned her head. "You look happy."

It wasn't a question. Elliot, standing there with their hands jammed in their coat, found that they could not speak at all.

"It is a very simple thing to say. But I did not know it. I had not seen it for years, I think. A long time. And then there it was, in a photograph, in a dress robe that somebody has altered, with a silver brooch, and a boy with red hair on one side of you, and a girl with braids on the other. And you are _laughing_." She was speaking more quickly now. "And I thought: that is my child. And I do not know what to call them."

The wind was coming off the water. It lifted a strand of hair across her face, and she did not move it.

"I would like to know," said Lucia, "what to call you."

---

It was very quiet.

There's a particular quality of silence that occurs when something you've been afraid of for years happens in a calm and ordinary way. Elliot had imagined this. They had imagined it in the middle of the night, in a dozen versions, with shouting, and cold, and long silences, and a slammed door in a kitchen in Prague. They hadn't imagined a grey lake, and a woman with a streak of silver in her hair standing with her hands in her pockets, waiting, with her shoulders very straight and her whole face tight with the effort of asking.

"Elliot," they said.

"Yes. I know that. I mean—" She gestured, impatiently, at the air. "In the sentence. When I speak about you. In Czech there is no such word, not for a person. And in English I have been practising." She took a breath. "On the train. Eleven hours. I said it to the window."

"What did you say?"

"They." She said it very carefully, as though it were a glass she might drop. "They are my child. They are talented. They are late." She frowned. "I do not think that is right. The verb is strange."

"It's right," said Elliot.

"Is it?"

"It's _they are_. It's—yes. It's right." Their voice had gone a bit odd. "It's they. It's Elliot. It's just Elliot, and they."

"Elliot," said their mother. "They." She tried it again, slowly, a second time, with the stress in the wrong place. She tried it a third time. It still came out as though she were reading it off a card. But she said it, in the cold, to a lake, with the whole of her attention, and it was possibly the most beautiful sentence that Elliot had ever heard.

They looked away. They looked at the ice on the water, and the black trees, and the white, flat, enormous sky, until the sting in their eyes had gone down.

"Thank you," they said.

"It is nothing. I will be bad at it for a year."

"That's all right."

"I will say it in the wrong place."

"I'll tell you."

"You are very patient," said Lucia. "You did not get that from me."

---

They'd walked a little way along the shore before Elliot found the rest of it.

It came out sideways, as such things do, in the middle of a remark about the ice. They hadn't planned to say it. They'd gone over it, in the dormitory, in the dark, a hundred times, in a dozen different words. And in the end it was just a sentence.

"I don't think I want to be a potioneer," said Elliot.

She stopped.

"I'm good at it. I know I am. I like it, in a way. I liked watching you, this morning. You were—" They swallowed. "But it's not what I want to do. It's not what I want to _be_. I've wanted to tell you for a year. I wrote you a letter. It was awful. It's still in my trunk."

Lucia Novak didn't say anything. She stood on the path with her hands at her sides, and the wind moved her coat, and her face did something that Elliot had never seen it do. It didn't crumple. It didn't harden. It went, quite slowly, very still, like a pond when the wind drops.

"I wished," she said, after a long time, "to give you every door."

"I know."

"I had very few. My mother, your babička, she worked, and she worked, and she gave me what she could. And when I was your age, I thought: if I ever have a child, I will not make them choose. I will give them everything. I will open every door I can find, and hold it, and they will walk through whichever one they like." Her voice was quite level. "And I think, perhaps—" She stopped. "I think that in holding the doors, I held one of them shut."

"You didn't—"

"I did. A little. A very little. I held the one I knew best." She looked at the lake. "It is a thing that you do not see until someone says it. It is, I think, the worst kind of mistake. It is a mistake made out of love."

Elliot's throat had closed.

They did the only thing they could think of, which was to reach inside their coat, and take out the sketchbook, and hold it out.

"I drew this," they said. "This morning. In your lecture."

---

It was a long time before she looked at it.

She took the book in both hands, and she held it for a moment as if it might be hot, and then she opened it, to the page that was folded back at the corner, and was still.

It was the hand. It was the long, loose, hovering fingers, the bent wrist, the spoon held in the air. There was no cauldron, or only a hint of one, a curve, a few lines. There was only the moment, caught, between two decisions: the held breath. And a single, thin, rising line of blue, pencilled in with the back of a crayon, like smoke.

She looked at it for what felt like several minutes.

Elliot stood perfectly still. They were aware of the wind, and the cold, and the pounding in their ears, and the long grey lake beyond her shoulder. And of a lantern, in a bag at their hip, which was, they dimly realised, lit; it was throwing a soft amber glow across the snow, and neither of them, for once, was looking.

"I have never seen my hands," said their mother, at last.

"You've seen them every day."

"Not like this." She touched the edge of the page with one finger. "This is the part that I love. I do not know how to say it. It is the moment when it is not yet decided. And you have—" She stopped. "You have drawn it. Without being told."

"I'm sorry. I didn't ask—"

"Do not be sorry." She closed the book, carefully. She held it out. She did not let go at once. "Show me more," said Lucia Novak. "Another time. Not now. I am not ready. But another time. Yes?"

"Yes," said Elliot.

"Good."

They stood there, the two of them, with the book between them, each holding an end of it, in the cold white light.

And out on the lake, a long way from the shore, the water began to move.

It happened very slowly. A great, smooth, dark swell rose in the middle of the pewter, a hundred yards out, and broke the skin of the ice with a sound like a sigh. Something came up through it: something enormous, and slow, and the colour of wet slate, with a ridge of suckers along its length, that curled up into the white air, and turned, a little, as if looking about it. Rings spread outwards from it across the water. A few last flakes of ice fell from its side and tinkled on the surface.

They watched it. Neither of them said anything.

The giant squid hung there, in the hush, for perhaps half a minute, a long dark question mark against the sky. And then, quite unhurried, as though it had found what it had come for, it folded itself down, and sank, and the water closed over it, and went still.

For a while longer, they stood side by side, a foot apart, and watched the rings go out.
