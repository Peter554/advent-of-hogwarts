Elliot found the note under their plate at breakfast.

It was a small square of parchment, folded in four, with their name on the outside in a neat, upright hand they would have known anywhere. In the third year it had corrected the spelling of "grandiloquent" in the margin of one of their essays, in red, with a small apologetic smiley face.

They unfolded it under the table.

_I saw. No list. No questions. I'm in the library until three if you want me, and I'm not if you don't. —M._

Elliot read it twice. Then they folded it along its creases, put it in the inside pocket of their jacket next to the sketchbook, and ate a piece of toast with great concentration, in a Great Hall loud with three hundred people planning to go to Hogsmeade.

They had not, in the end, talked to their mother the night before. They had stood in the anteroom behind Slughorn's hall, among a lot of coats, for about four minutes under a poor gas lamp, and she had said, in her quick, impatient voice, "I have been on a train for eleven hours and your professor has given me three glasses of a thing he calls mead. I am not able, tonight, to say a sentence correctly in either of my languages." She had put a hand to the bridge of her nose. "Tomorrow. After I have lectured. We will walk. Yes?"

"Yes," Elliot had said.

"Good." She had stood there a second longer, coat over her arm, looking at them in a way they could not read, and said, "That is your father's jacket," and Elliot had said, "It's mine now," and she had said, "Yes. It suits you," and that had been all.

---

The lecture was at nine.

It was held in the Potions classroom, a long stone room in the dungeons with tall windows set high in the wall, and by five to, all twenty-two fifth-year potioneers were in their seats. Nobody was talking, which was unusual. Slughorn had said it was a rare opportunity. The older students had said you did not, as a rule, get a Master of the Apothecaries' Guild to stand in front of a class on a Saturday morning. The room hummed like the minutes before an examination.

Elliot sat at the back. They had chosen the seat and then wished they hadn't. Their notebook was open and the pencil was moving a little, without their having told it to.

She came in at nine, carrying nothing but a small black leather case, which she set on the bench and unlocked with a snap, a pair of silver tongs, and a flask of something clear. She looked, Elliot thought, as she always did before work: sharp, upright, pared down to what was needed. A plain grey dress, a long black apron, her hair pinned back with one pin. She didn't smile.

"Good morning," said Lucia Novak. "I understand that you are fifth years. That means you are fifteen. That means you believe, at this moment, that you know everything about Potions that there is to know, and I would like, if I may, to spend two hours changing your minds. I will try to be quick."

There was a small, appreciative ripple.

"Today I will make a draught which I have made perhaps four thousand times. It is the Draught of Mending Sleep. It is given to patients who have had a great shock, to the body or the mind, and it does one thing. It lets them rest. It is not glamorous. It will not win you a prize. It is a bottle of something blue that a very tired person drinks at the end of a very bad day."

She turned to the cauldron, and Elliot, at the back, watched her hands.

---

They knew those hands. They had always known them, the way you know things in your own house. But they had not watched them properly since they were about nine.

She cut a root in two strokes and the pieces fell in a line. She lifted a flask by the base, not the neck, and tipped it so that the contents slid out in one clean ribbon and met the cauldron without a ripple. She stirred, and the potion, which had been the colour of weak tea, went a pale, trembling blue.

"This is where it turns," said their mother.

She had stopped stirring. She held the silver spoon in the air just above the cauldron, wrist bent, fingers loose, and spoke quietly, to nobody in particular. The whole class leaned in.

"It is a very small moment. A second, perhaps two. You have to feel it. The potion is thinking whether it will be what you wish. If you hurry it, it is not a draught, it is only a liquid. If you wait too long, the same. And at that moment you must decide, and nobody can decide for you." She smiled, very slightly, for the first time. "I have done it four thousand times. And every time I think: I do not know if it will turn. And then it does."

She lowered the spoon. The blue deepened and went through the cauldron like a breath.

There was a silence with a sound in it. And Elliot, in the back row, looked down at their notebook and found they had drawn it. The hand: the loose fingers, the bent wrist, the spoon held in the air above the cauldron and a thread of blue rising from it. It had taken perhaps forty seconds. It was quite possibly the best thing they had ever done.

They knew exactly what they loved about it. The turning. The little held moment when a thing was not yet decided, the way a pair of hands could say, without a word, _wait_.

They did not love what came after. The patients. The tired person at the end of the bad day, whom she talked about with such care. Elliot did not want to fix people. They wanted to look at them: the way a person's hands rested when they were thinking, the way a coat sat on a shoulder. They had known that for a long time, and it was one thing to know it and another to see it laid out in the grey Saturday light in front of the person you most wanted to be good enough for.

The class applauded, ragged at first and then loud, with a few boys at the front thumping their desks. Lucia Novak stood at the cauldron with the spoon in her hand and looked at them as if somebody had told her an unlikely joke.

"Oh," she said. "That is not necessary."

She said it so honestly that the applause doubled.

---

She found them afterwards in the corridor, in the crowd going up the stairs.

"Elliot."

"Mama."

"I thought that you might not want to have lunch with Professor Slughorn."

"He's very—"

"Yes. He is very." She put on her coat, a long, dark, severe one, and turned up the collar. She did not look at them. "I have two hours before I must go. I should like to see the lake, if it is still there."

"It's still there."

"Then we will walk," said their mother. "Yes?"

---

It was the sort of cold that gets in by the wrists. The sky was low and white, and the lake lay under it like a sheet of beaten pewter, with a rim of ice along the edge and the black bones of the trees on the far bank. They walked down the trampled path side by side, a foot apart, their breath going up in two clouds. For a long time neither of them spoke.

"I have been wanting," said Lucia Novak at last, "to say something to you for several weeks. I have been trying to find it, and I find that it does not exist. It is not in either language." She stopped at the edge of the water. "So I will do it badly. I hope you will forgive me."

"You don't have to."

"I do." Her hands were in her pockets. She was looking at the lake. "On the last day of November I opened a drawer. The drawer in the dresser, in the kitchen, where we put the post that is not urgent. And in it there was a photograph. From your ball." She breathed out. "It came in January, in an envelope, with no letter, and I put it away. I do not know why. I think I was afraid of what it would say."

Elliot said nothing.

"I took it out. I put it on the dresser, where I could see it. And I looked at it for a week." She turned her head. "You look happy."

It wasn't a question. Elliot, hands jammed in their coat, found that they could not speak.

"It is a very simple thing to say. But I did not know it. I had not seen it for years, I think. And then there it was, in a dress robe that somebody has altered, with a silver brooch, and a boy with red-brown hair on one side of you, and a girl with braids on the other. And you are _laughing_." She was speaking faster now. "And I thought: that is my child. And I do not know what to call them."

The wind came off the water and lifted a strand of hair across her face. She did not move it.

"I would like to know," said Lucia, "what to call you."

---

Elliot had imagined this, in the middle of the night, in a dozen versions, with shouting, and long silences, and a slammed door in a kitchen in Prague. They had not imagined a grey lake, and a woman with a streak of silver in her hair standing with her hands in her pockets, shoulders very straight, her face tight with the effort of asking.

"Elliot," they said.

"Yes. I know that. I mean—" She gestured impatiently at the air. "In the sentence. When I speak about you. In Czech there is no such word, not for a person. And in English I have been practising." She took a breath. "On the train. Eleven hours. I said it to the window."

"What did you say?"

"They." She said it very carefully, as though it were a glass she might drop. "They are my child. They are talented. They are late." She frowned. "I do not think that is right. The verb is strange."

"It's right," said Elliot.

"Is it?"

"It's _they are_. Yes. It's right." Their voice had gone odd. "It's Elliot. It's just Elliot, and they."

"Elliot," said their mother. "They." She tried it again, slowly, with the stress in the wrong place. She tried it a third time. It still came out as if she were reading it off a card. But she said it, in the cold, to a lake, with all her attention, and it was possibly the most beautiful sentence Elliot had ever heard.

They looked away, at the ice and the black trees and the flat white sky, until the sting in their eyes had gone down.

"Thank you."

"It is nothing. I will be bad at it for a year."

"That's all right."

"I will say it in the wrong place."

"I'll tell you."

"You are very patient," said Lucia. "You did not get that from me."

---

They had walked a little way along the shore before Elliot found the rest of it.

It came out sideways, in the middle of a remark about the ice. They had gone over it in the dormitory a hundred times, in a dozen different words, and in the end it was just a sentence.

"I don't think I want to be a potioneer," said Elliot.

She stopped.

"I'm good at it. I know I am. I like it, in a way. I liked watching you this morning. You were—" They swallowed. "But it's not what I want to do. It's not what I want to _be_. I've wanted to tell you for a year. I wrote you a letter. It was awful. It's still in my trunk."

Lucia Novak said nothing. She stood on the path with her hands at her sides, and the wind moved her coat, and her face did something Elliot had never seen it do. It didn't crumple and it didn't harden. It went slowly still, like a pond when the wind drops.

"I wished," she said, after a long time, "to give you every door."

"I know."

"I had very few. My mother, your babička, worked and worked, and gave me what she could. And when I was your age I thought: if I ever have a child, I will not make them choose. I will open every door I can find, and hold it, and they will walk through whichever one they like." Her voice was level. "And I think, perhaps, that in holding the doors, I held one of them shut."

"You didn't—"

"I did. A little. I held the one I knew best." She looked at the lake. "You do not see it until someone says it. It is a mistake made out of love, which is the worst kind. They are so hard to be angry with."

Elliot's throat had closed. They did the only thing they could think of, which was to reach inside their coat, take out the sketchbook, and hold it out.

"I drew this," they said. "This morning. In your lecture."

---

It was a long time before she looked at it.

She took the book in both hands and held it for a moment as though it might be hot. Then she opened it to the page folded back at the corner, and was still.

The hand, with its loose fingers and bent wrist and the spoon held in the air. There was no cauldron, or only a curve and a few lines. There was only the moment, caught between two decisions, and a single thin line of blue, pencilled in with the back of a crayon, rising like smoke.

She looked at it for what felt like several minutes. Elliot stood perfectly still, aware of the wind and the cold and the pounding in their ears. In the bag at their hip the lantern had lit, and was throwing a soft amber glow across the snow, and for once neither of them was looking at it.

"I have never seen my hands," said their mother.

"You've seen them every day."

"Not like this." She touched the edge of the page with one finger. "This is the part that I love. I do not know how to say it. It is the moment when it is not yet decided. And you have drawn it. Without being told."

"I'm sorry. I didn't ask—"

"Do not be sorry." She closed the book, carefully, and held it out. She did not let go at once. "Show me more. Another time. Not now, I am not ready. But another time. Yes?"

"Yes," said Elliot.

"Good."

They stood there with the book between them, each holding an end of it, in the cold white light.

And out on the lake, a long way from the shore, the water began to move.

A smooth dark swell rose a hundred yards out and broke the ice with a sound like a sigh. Something came up through it, slow and enormous and the colour of wet slate, with a ridge of suckers along its length. It curled up into the white air and turned a little, as if looking about. Rings spread across the water. A few last flakes of ice slid from its side and tinkled on the surface.

Neither of them said anything. The giant squid hung there for perhaps half a minute, a long dark question mark against the sky. Then, unhurried, as if it had found what it had come for, it folded itself down and sank, and the water closed over it and went still.

They stood side by side, a foot apart, and watched the rings go out.
