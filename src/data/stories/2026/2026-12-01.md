By the first of December, Finn MacLeod had counted the tables so many times that he could do it with his eyes shut, which on Tuesday morning he very nearly was, with his chin propped on one hand and his porridge going cold.

Four houses, four long tables, and one of each of his friends at every one. Mira was at Ravenclaw with her revision notes fanned around her plate like a hand of cards. Zara sat at the Gryffindor table with her elbows planted among the salt cellars, rearranging them into what was either a Quidditch formation or an argument. Elliot was at the end of the Slytherin table, hood up, a duelling manual propped against the teapot, looking like someone who had paid good money to be left alone.

It hadn't been decided. That was what Finn kept coming back to. Nobody had said, _right, from October we sit with our own year_. It had just happened, the way a tray of jelly sets in a cold larder: wobbling at first, and then, quite suddenly, not.

"You're doing it again," said Oliver.

"Doing what?"

"The thing with your lips." Oliver Fenwick demonstrated, moving his mouth silently and pointing at each table in turn. He had sandy hair that stuck up at the back, and a flashcard in one hand that said _Mandrake: three uses_ on the front. He had been holding it for ten minutes without turning it over. "You count. It's like being on a school trip with someone who keeps doing headcounts."

"I don't count."

"You counted four just then. I saw you get to four."

Finn picked up his spoon and put it down again. Above them the enchanted ceiling was the colour of wet slate, and a thin sleet was coming down out of it that never quite reached the candles. First of December, and not so much as a snowflake worth the name. On his other side, the chair at the end of the bench was pushed in neatly, as it had been for weeks. It was the visitors' chair. Last year it had mostly been Zara's, who used to turn up halfway through breakfast with her plate in one hand and her toast in the other and sit down without asking, as though the Hufflepuff table were an annexe of Gryffindor.

"Mandrakes," Finn said, nodding at the card. "Three uses."

Oliver turned it over, read it, and turned it back. "Don't change the subject. Also, I've forgotten all three."

"Screaming, stew, and the thing for petrification."

"Is it stew?"

"It's _not_ stew," Finn admitted. "Don't put stew."

They went to Charms together.

---

Professor Flitwick's classroom was a long, tall room with windows that ran almost to the ceiling, and Professor Flitwick himself, as usual, was standing on a stack of books. There were four of them today, evidently stolen from the nearest shelf and arranged by height, and he was clapping his small hands for order with the air of a man who intended to enjoy himself.

"Settle, settle! Fifth years! I have something to show you that I have been looking forward to since September."

At the front of the room, Professor Thorne was hauling a wooden crate onto a desk. She was Flitwick's assistant, a cheerful, rangy woman with a pencil stuck through her hair, and she was doing it with the particular care of someone who had already dropped it once. The crate was old and had a lid that wouldn't quite shut. Out of the gap spilled a gleam of brass.

"Every fifth year at Hogwarts," said Flitwick, "has traditionally, and I do mean traditionally, for rather longer than the school has had O.W.L.s, built a lantern."

There was a ripple of puzzlement. Somebody at the back said, "A _what_?"

"A lantern, Mr Reyes. A lantern. It is carried on the solstice, which this year is Monday the twenty-first, on a walk from the castle to the lake at dusk. Your seniors will remember—" He stopped. "No. They won't. It fell out of use, for a while, owing to some paperwork, and I regret to say I let it. Professor Thorne found the box in the back of the cupboard in October and, well." He beamed. "We're bringing it back."

Thorne flipped the lid open. Inside, bedded in old straw, were the lantern parts: brass frames the size of a loaf of bread, each with a hinged door and a little hook at the top; panes of thin clear glass, wrapped in paper; a coil of grey thread for wicks. They looked as though they had been waiting a long time.

"You will build them over the next three weeks," said Flitwick. "The frame, the glass, the charm-work to hold them together. That is the easy part. The hard part, I am afraid, is the light."

He paused to let them wonder about this, which they obligingly did.

"You cannot light it," he said. "Not with _Incendio_, not with _Lumos_, not with any flame you carry in your wand. I would not try. A lantern in this tradition lights itself, from the thing you carry into the dark."

A hand went up. It belonged to Isla Brennan, who was always first to ask. "Carry what, sir?"

"Ah," said Flitwick, delighted. "That is rather the point. Most people don't know until it tells them."

---

It took ten minutes for the first frame to be passed around the room, and about eight seconds for the first student to say it was a trick.

The wicks were the first problem. They were soft, grey and slightly damp, and had to be threaded through the little brass holder in the base. The second problem was that everyone's instinct was to light them. Wands went up and spells went off all round the room: little yellow _Incendios_, a cluster of _Lumos_, one student who tried a Fire-Making Charm out of a book and got nothing but a smell of singed toast. Flitwick clambered from stack to stack murmuring, "No, no, put it away, it won't take it, you'll only insult it."

Finn had threaded his wick and screwed the holder into its socket, and he lifted the frame with both hands to see if it looked straight.

The wick caught.

It didn't flicker or hesitate. One moment it was a grey thread and the next a flame stood up at the end of it, a solid, steady gold, the colour of the inside of a good loaf. It lit the brass. It lit the underside of his chin and the freckles on the backs of his hands. It lit the desk, the wall behind the desk and a slice of Oliver's astonished face.

"Finn," said Oliver.

"I didn't do anything."

"I know. I was watching you not do anything. It lit as soon as you picked it up."

All round the room, heads were turning. Finn clapped his hand flat over the front of the lantern, which was like trying to cover a sunrise with a saucer, and the light came out between his fingers. He stuffed it into his bag, among the Herbology notes, and the bag glowed. He shoved the bag under the desk. The desk glowed.

"Very good, Mr MacLeod," said Flitwick, passing by. He did not stop, and he did not look, and he did not say any of the things Finn had been bracing for. He merely patted the edge of the desk, as he might have patted a horse. "You may keep it covered, if you like. But it's yours, you know."

Finn said nothing at all.

The others took a good deal longer.

Oliver's lantern lit at about a quarter past, a modest pale yellow, rather like the first light under a door. He looked at it for a while with his head on one side. "I think," he said, "I might be carrying a sandwich."

"You're not carrying a sandwich."

"It's the right colour for a sandwich."

Over by the windows, Isla Brennan had got hers to flare, and then to go out, and then to flare again, with a stricken look, as though something was changing its mind about her. Two Slytherins were looking at theirs and each other with unconcealed suspicion. And at the far end, a boy called Callum Reyes, who had been first to say "a what?", was staring into his lantern with a faintly shocked expression and not saying anything.

---

They met in the corridor afterwards, because it would have been odd not to.

Finn got there first and stood by the foot of the stairs, with the bag held close to his side so that its light didn't leak out through the stitching. After a moment Mira arrived with her lantern held at arm's length, in the careful grip of somebody carrying a very large and slightly suspect egg.

"It hasn't lit," she said. "I want to say at the outset that I've followed the instructions exactly. It may be the glass, which I don't believe is a flawed batch, though I've been wrong about glass. Or it may be the wick. Or, and I think this is the least likely, it may be me."

"It's you," said Zara, coming down the stairs two at a time.

"I said least likely."

"Mine's a dying firework." Zara thrust her own lantern at them. It sat in the crook of her arm, coughing small, bad-tempered sparks, which landed on the stone floor and went out. One had apparently set fire to the end of her braid; she slapped at it without breaking stride. "It goes off. And then it stops. And then it goes off. Thorne said that was normal. She said it in the voice you use for a goat."

"Mine's fine," said Elliot, materialising from nowhere in the way they had. They were holding their lantern by the ring at the top, very slightly away from their body, as if it might be catching. It was lit, just, a low amber glow like the last of a coal.

"That looks quite good," Mira said.

"It was better a minute ago. Then Zara looked at it." The glow had, in fact, dimmed to nothing while they spoke. Elliot raised an eyebrow at it. "It doesn't like being watched. I find that relatable."

"It's a Slytherin lantern," said Zara.

"It has taste."

Mira peered at the dark brass, then at Finn's bag. "Yours?"

"Oh, you know," said Finn. "Fine. Brass. Wick."

A very small silence. Zara looked at the bag, and then at Finn. The bag, at that moment, brightened by one shade, as though in sympathy. She said, mildly, "Is it on fire?"

"It's a light bag."

"Is it—"

"I've got Herbology," said Finn, and then, because that sounded like a retreat, "so do you, don't you, no, you've got—"

"Practice," Zara said. "Till seven, probably. I've got a new set of plays and a team who've stopped laughing at them. I'll grab something on the way." She shifted her lantern, which spat. "Sorry. I'd say come and watch, but it's freezing and I'd have to talk to you the whole time."

"I've got Arithmancy group," said Mira. "In the library. It's Priya's, really, but I said I'd go, and then there's a Runes problem set, which isn't hard but is, how can I put this, long—"

"I'm going to hide in the library," said Elliot. "In a different bit."

There was a pause in which anyone might have said, _dinner, though_. Or _the Hufflepuff table's got room_. Finn looked at the three of them. He felt the words arrive in his mouth, and then he felt them sit there, and the moment, as moments do, stepped past.

"Right," he said. "Sure. See you."

"See you," said Zara, and was already half-way up the stairs. Mira said something about tomorrow, and a quill, and went. Elliot gave a small two-fingered salute with the hand not holding the lantern, and then they were gone too, round the corner, and the corridor was empty and rather cold.

Finn looked down at his bag. Through the leather, through the stitching, light was coming out in thin gold threads.

---

He stayed late in Greenhouse One after Herbology, long after anybody else had gone. It wasn't strictly necessary. The seedlings could have waited, and Professor Sprout had told them twice to go and eat. But a greenhouse in December was a good place to be a person on their own. The glass was beaded with damp and the pipes ticked, and the winter basil on the bench had that warm, green, faintly peppery smell that made everything else a bit less loud.

He set the lantern on a potting bench. It lit the leaves from underneath and cast a ring of gold across the seed trays. A sleeping Mimbulus in the corner stirred slightly and went back to sleep.

"I don't know what you want," Finn told it.

The lantern said nothing. It burned.

He had known the minute it lit. That was the odd part. Not _known_ the way you know a fact, but the way you know a plant is thirsty before you've checked the soil: a small, specific, bodily certainty. Flitwick had said _the thing you carry_, and Finn had gone through his head like a man patting his pockets, looking for it, and found it at once. It had been in there for weeks. It was the table count. It was a chair pushed in. It was the sense that something that had been growing well had, without anybody noticing, stopped getting enough light.

He didn't say any of that out loud to the basil.

By the time he came up the steps into the Entrance Hall, the dinner crowd was long gone and the lanterns on the walls had burned down to a low and sleepy glow. The doors of the Great Hall stood open. Inside, the house-elves were clearing the last of the plates in total silence, and the four long tables lay in rows beneath the slate-grey ceiling, the benches empty, the candles guttering.

Finn went in. He didn't quite know why. He walked down the length of the Hufflepuff table, with the lantern held at his side so that its gold ran along the wood, and stopped at the end of the bench.

The visitors' chair was still pushed in.

He looked at it for a moment. Then he put his free hand on its back, and drew it out, a little, from the table, about as far as you would for somebody coming in late with a plate in one hand and toast in the other. That was all. He didn't say anything. There was nobody to say it to.

At the far end of the Hall a house-elf glanced up, and then politely glanced away.

Finn lifted the lantern, and went out through the side door and down the stone steps toward the kitchens, with the light swinging gently ahead of him on the wall.
