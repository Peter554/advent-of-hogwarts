The carp was called Jaroslav, and he had been in the bath for two days.

Elliot had not meant to name him. It was the sort of thing that happens with a goldfish at a fair: a man in an apron in Hogsmeade had held up a large, grave, golden-brown fish by the gills and said, "Christmas Eve?" and Elliot, who had planned all fortnight to be sensible, had heard themselves say, "Yes. Do you do it in a bucket?" He was installed in the great marble bath of the prefects' bathroom on the fifth floor, which according to the notice on the door was not at all against the rules, and had spent two days going slowly round among the taps, gazing up at the mermaid in the window. At no point had it occurred to him that he had a purpose.

"_That's_ the fish?" said Zara.

"That's the fish."

"He's huge."

"He's a very good size," said Elliot, with some pride. It was two in the afternoon and they were in the kitchens, at the long central table, sleeves rolled, with a bucket at their feet. "In Prague they sell them in the streets on the twenty-third, out of tubs. Everyone has one. It lives in the bath until the evening and then somebody has to—" They stopped. "It's a very old tradition."

"What do you do with it?"

"Well," said Elliot, "that's the question."

---

The kitchen was as big as a barn, with a hearth at one end you could have parked a carriage in, and at forty different stations, with a great range of skill and complete confidence, most of the professors of Hogwarts were cooking.

Hagrid had the roast. It had been a turkey at some earlier stage; it now stood in an iron tray like a piece of furniture, with drumsticks the size of cricket bats, stuffed with chestnuts, sage and most of a small tree. It would not go in the oven. Hagrid, Oliver and two seventh-years had been trying for ten minutes to persuade it, while Zara, perched on a barrel with a long fork, offered advice.

"I think it needs another hour."

"Yeh think?"

"I'm a captain."

"Righto," said Hagrid, and the three of them put it back in the oven, which took four.

Professor Sprout presided over nine hundred sprouts, under a note pinned above the bench in a small brisk hand: _Cut a cross in the stalk end. It is the only way. Trust me. —P.D._ "Who," said Sprout, to the room, "is P.D., and why does she have opinions about my sprouts?"

"She's right, though," said Oliver, from the next bench, where he was peeling potatoes with the focus of a man defusing something.

"I know she's right. That's what's so irritating."

Flitwick, on a stack of five books, stirred a pudding in a copper cauldron the size of a bath, by charm, humming. Slughorn stood beside him with a napkin round his neck and a spoon in each fist, engaged on the most serious job of the afternoon, which was tasting. "Outstanding," he said, with his mouth full. "Flitwick, my dear fellow, you've done it again."

"I haven't added the brandy."

"Then it's outstanding _without_ the brandy."

And moving between them all, quick and cheerful, with a tray of Winter Blend and a stack of recipe cards in his pocket, went Finn.

There were no elves at the stoves. At four, the whole kitchen staff of Hogwarts would sit down at the end of the table on a row of small stools that Finn had set out, and for the first time in a thousand years do nothing. They had been arriving for some time, in ones and twos, in clean grey waistcoats, with the wary air of people invited to a wedding who aren't sure what to do with their hands. Tibby had polished her silver _T_. Dibby had her tea-cosy on straight. They sat in a row with their feet not touching the floor, looking at the stoves, and at intervals one of them half rose, and Finn said "Sit," and they sat.

Elliot's koláče had gone in at a quarter to three. They had made them from memory, or nearly: small soft yellow pastries with a hollow in the middle for poppy seed, plum or sweet white cheese. The weights were on a card in their pocket, soft and stained and creased, written in a narrow upright hand. It had been their grandmother's. They had it out to check the oven and left it on the table by the tray.

---

The owl arrived at ten to five.

He came in through the open top of the casement in a slow, overweight swoop, shedding feathers, with a letter in his beak and an expression of deep injury, and landed on the nearest thing, which was Slughorn's pudding. He sank to the knees. He looked at it. With great dignity he did not move.

"Good gracious," said Slughorn.

"Sorry, Professor. He's from home."

The letter was thick, sealed with grey wax in exactly the place and at exactly the angle their mother always used. On the front, in a hand they knew as well as their own, it said _Elliot_.

They did not open it at the table. There was a pantry behind the dresser, a small cold cell hung with the carcasses of several very large birds. They shut the door, stood among the jam in the smell of apples, with the clatter of the kitchen going on through the wall, and broke the seal.

Four pages, in English, in handwriting slowed down for the purpose.

_Elliot,_

_Your drawings came on Wednesday. I have looked at them every evening. I do not know how to say what I think in either language, so I will say it badly: they are very good. They are better than very good. I am not able to be objective, and I do not care._

_I am learning. Elliot. They. I say it to the cat. The cat has not complained._

_I must tell you something I should have told you at the lake. I did not, because I was afraid it would sound like a reason and not a gift._

_Your babička drew._

_All her life. She kept the books at the bottom of the wardrobe in her flat, under the winter coats, and nobody knew: not your grandfather, not me. I found them when she died, and I did not know what to do with them, and I put them in the cupboard under the stairs with the boots. I did not open it for three years. Then your photograph went up on the dresser and I could not stop looking at it, and on Monday I opened the cupboard._

_You told me once that she was the only person who understood your drawings. I did not know why. I do now._

_I enclose some pages. Your father photographed them (he says the photographs are poor; they are not). I should like to show you the rest at Easter. Come home. Bring your sketchbook. We shall have a great deal to show each other._

_Mama_

_P.S. Your father asks whether there is a carp at Hogwarts. I have told him not to ask._

Elliot read it twice: first fast, with their eyes ahead of their mind, and then slowly with their back against the wall.

There were six small square photographs, a little blurred, taken at an angle on a kitchen table. A window. A woman's hands. A cat asleep on a cushion. The roofs of Prague in the rain. A man with a very large nose, reading, glasses pushed up on his forehead. A plum tree. They were not clever or grand. They were quick and exact and extremely good, and every line was in the hand that Elliot had spent fifteen years trying to find in themselves.

They stood there a long time. Then they put the photographs in order and the letter on top of them, in the inside pocket of their jacket over the sketchbook, and breathed in. It came out shaky.

It was only at the door, with a hand on the latch, that they remembered the card.

---

It lay on the table where they had left it, among the flour. _Koláče. 500 g flour. 250 ml milk._ They had looked at it for years. They knew every smudge: a thumbprint of jam in one corner, a ring where a cup had stood, and down the margin, beside each of the fillings, a row of tiny marks.

They had always thought they were doodles. They had thought, if they had thought at all, that she'd been the sort of woman who scribbled while she was thinking, like someone tapping a foot.

They looked again.

Beside _mák_ there was a poppy head, an inch high, in three fine lines and a grey shadow. Beside _povidla_, in the same hand, a plum, with its bloom and one bright curve of light on its side. Beside _tvaroh_, a milk jug. And at the bottom of the card, tucked into the corner where a hand rests while it reads, a very small bird on a twig, its head turned a little to one side, as if listening.

They had had it for six years. They had never once bent their head to see.

"Oh," said Elliot, aloud, to the empty table.

Behind them, somebody screamed.

---

He had got out.

Nobody could say how. Some thought the owl had startled him; others, that he'd been nursing the idea for some time. But the bucket was empty, and a large, wet, gleaming shape was crossing the kitchen floor in a series of astonishing leaps.

"_Jaroslav!_"

He went through the legs of the roast. He went under the table. He went in a long slippery arc over a bowl of breadcrumbs and a sack of flour and the stool where Oliver had been peeling, and Oliver went one way and the potatoes another. Zara threw herself at him in a flying tackle and missed. Hagrid lunged with a ladle. Sprout shouted "_Stand back!_" and threw a colander. Flitwick, on his books, cried "Do be careful of the pudding!" in a very high voice, and Slughorn put his own spoon down to guard the sprouts. A cloud of flour went up. Finn knelt on the flagstones with a tea-towel and his arms out, saying "Come on, mate," in the low coaxing voice you use on a nervous horse.

"Don't hurt him!" cried Amara from the doorway.

"I'm not going to _hurt_ him, I'm going to _catch_ him," said Zara, and went the whole length of the floor on her stomach.

It lasted ten minutes. It went round the whole kitchen in a thundering circle, through the legs of eleven professors and out along the row of stools where the elves sat, who with perfect unanimous calm lifted their feet. Somewhere behind the dresser, with flour in their hair, Elliot discovered that they were laughing too hard to stand up.

At five o'clock the big door opened.

It opened inward with a long measured creak, and on the threshold, in her green cloak, with her hat straight and a list in her hand, stood Professor McGonagall. She had come to inspect. She had said so. She had a pencil.

The carp did not see her. He had chosen that moment for a supreme effort. He took off from the middle of the floor in a long, slow, shining arc above the flour and the potatoes and the faces turned to watch, and with the sure instinct of a creature that has looked all his life for somewhere to belong, came down full length in the arms of the Headmistress of Hogwarts.

There was a very long silence.

She looked at him. He looked at her. His round calm golden eye regarded her without alarm, and his wide mouth opened and shut twice.

"Novak," said Professor McGonagall.

"Yes, Headmistress?"

"I believe this is yours."

---

They let him go at dusk.

It was Amara's idea. She said, in a small flat voice, that he was a very nice fish and hadn't done anything, and that in her opinion it was not the sort of fish you could eat after it had jumped on the Headmistress. Elliot said that in Prague nobody would see it that way. Hagrid said he'd a nice bit of river trout hanging in the larder, if anyone wanted to be traditional, and it would do just as well.

They carried him down in the bucket across the blue snow, in a small procession: Elliot with the handle, Amara with a hand on the side, Wren with a lantern. The sky was a pale green with one star in it, and the frozen lake lay below like a sheet of glass. At the end of the jetty there was a round black hole in the ice that Hagrid had cut that morning.

"Say goodbye," said Amara.

"Na shledanou," said Elliot to the fish, rather huskily. "Jaroslav. I'm sorry. It was a good bath."

He went in with a splash. For a moment he hung there, a long gold shape in the dark under the ice, and then with a lazy, magnificent flick of his tail he was gone.

"He'll be all right," said Wren, holding the lantern low over the water. "There's a squid."

"A very large squid."

"They'll be friends."

"I'd like to think so," said Elliot.

They stood a while on the jetty in the green dusk with the pale lake going away on every side, and up in the castle, in the long grey bulk against the sky, a window lit, and then another, and then a hundred.

---

The table ran the whole length of the Hall.

It had been put together in the half hour before dinner by Hagrid, five seventh-years and a good deal of unspoken magic: one long straight table with a white cloth and a hundred candles and no head to it, laid on both sides for a hundred and twenty. There were bowls of holly and platters of glazed ham, a pyramid of trout, a mountain of sprouts every one of which had been cut across the stalk, and in the middle, in a glass dish, a heap of small round yellow pastries with a hollow in each.

Above them the lanterns burned: eighty-one of them, hung in their long spiral in the dark, gold and rose and green, silver and violet; and in the middle an orange-red, and a little way off an amber, a gold and a blue. They shed a soft mingled light over the cloth and the faces.

Everyone was there. There were the twenty-three, and the staff, and in the very middle of the table, in a row of small chairs with their feet dangling, in clean waistcoats and clean ears, every house-elf in the school. Tibby sat at McGonagall's right hand with her hands in her lap, looking at the hundred candles and the ceiling and the great yellow fire, and her eyes were round.

"Headmistress," she said, faintly. "There are so _many_ lights."

"Yes," said Professor McGonagall. "I believe that is what they are for."

They ate. It was a long, loud, luxurious meal. Hagrid carved the turkey with a sword. Slughorn said "Outstanding" eleven times. Flitwick's pudding came in flaming, with a sprig of holly, and drew an applause that rang off the stone. The koláče went from hand to hand and Zara ate six. Oliver, who had cut his potatoes into pieces of precisely equal size, said with awe, "I peeled _those_." Mira, with green ink on her cuff, who had written to Dr Lund at breakfast, looked at the window for an owl eleven times and pretended she hadn't.

And somewhere in the middle of it, Elliot found that they had stopped eating.

---

It was Wren they noticed first.

She came in late. She had gone to put away her lantern and take off her wet shoes, and now she stood in the great doorway on her own, in her good dark dress with her fringe pinned back, looking at the table. She had the very well-behaved look of someone who has come to a party and found that everyone has arrived and the places are full.

There was a chair. It stood at the very middle of the long white cloth, opposite Elliot's own, between Finn and Amara: an ordinary chair, pulled out a hand's breadth from the table. Through the soup and the roast and the pudding, as the table filled and the laughter rose and people leaned across each other for the gravy, nobody had touched it. Elliot had seen it without seeing it, for an hour, at the edge of their eye, like the place in a picture where the colour has been left out.

Finn had set it. They saw that now. He was looking at Wren across the table with his hand flat on the cloth, and he said nothing. He only moved his head, very slightly, towards the chair.

Wren hesitated. She looked at the chair. She looked at the faces turned half-way towards her in the lantern light: Mira's, Zara's, Amara's. And Elliot, who knew exactly what it was to stand at the edge of a room and wait to be sure, found they were holding their breath.

She crossed the floor in her flat black shoes, with her back very straight, and put her hand on the back of the chair. And Amara, without a word, with a scrape of wood, shifted along her bench to make room.

"It's _soup_," said Amara, in a loud urgent whisper. "You've missed the soup. There's loads left. Have mine."

"Thank you," said Wren.

"Have the roll."

"Thank you."

"Have the _trout_."

"I think," said Wren, "I'll start with the soup."

---

Later, when the plates had gone and the candles had burned down by a third and the Hall had gone quiet in the way of a room full of people who have eaten all they can and are not ready to leave, Elliot took out the sketchbook.

They hadn't planned to. It had always been private. It lay in the inside pocket of their jacket, among the folded pages, next to a small soft crumpled card.

They put it on the table. It made a faint flat sound against the cloth. Nobody looked at it at once. There was a careful silence, like the one that falls when somebody puts down a very full glass.

They opened it at the beginning and didn't explain. They pushed it gently along the white cloth to Zara. She looked at them. She took it. On the flyleaf, where for four years there had been a tiny _E.N._ in the bottom corner, there was another, in the middle of the page, in pencil capitals an inch high. It had been done that afternoon, in the pantry, among the jam.

Finn, reading over her shoulder, said nothing. He looked at Elliot across the table.

"Don't," said Elliot.

"Middle," said Finn, very quietly.

"I said _don't_." But the corner of their mouth had gone.

Zara turned a page, and another, and a long slow _oh_ went out of her.

It went round the table.

It went to Oliver, who looked for a long time with his head bent and handed it on without a word. It went to Finn, who said he had something in his eye and said so twice. It went across to Mira, who held it in both hands, like something that could fall, and turned each page by the corner and read every one: Slughorn's rooms and a clock with a bad temper; a hand holding a spoon; a lake and a lantern; a girl in a green scarf asleep over a book; a very tall figure in a tweed coat with a moustache; four people at a rail. At the back, folded in four, were six small square photographs of a woman's drawings: a window, a plum tree, the rain. It reached Amara, who said "That's _me_," and then, softer, "That's Nettle." It reached Wren, who said nothing at all.

It went on along the table, from hand to hand, past the wreck of the turkey and the holly, to the elves, who looked at it with their small hands flat on the page in a reverent silence; to Hagrid, who said "Well, I'll be—" and had to stop; to McGonagall, who held it a long time, glasses pushed up on her forehead, lips pressed together, and passed it on with a small dry nod.

And Elliot sat in the middle of it with their hands in their lap and a pulse in their throat, and did not say a word. It had been in the dark for a very long time. It was out now, in the air, among the people it was for, going round the long bright table like something meant to be passed, and nobody was holding it at arm's length or making it small.

It came back to Zara. She put it down with a careful thud and looked at Elliot. She didn't smile. She picked up her glass and held it out a little over the table.

"To the Wonder Seekers," said Zara.

"To the Wonder Seekers," said Mira, and Finn, and Oliver, and Amara, and Wren, and, after a moment, in a ragged delighted chorus all down the length of the table, somebody else.

Above them the lanterns burned. It was getting late. And outside, over the white lake, beyond the dark glass of the high windows, it had begun, very softly, to snow.
