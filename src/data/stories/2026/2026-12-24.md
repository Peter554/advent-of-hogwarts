The carp was called Jaroslav, and he had been in the bath for two days.

Elliot had not meant to name him. It was the sort of thing that happens with a goldfish at a fair: a man in an apron in Hogsmeade had held up a large, grave, golden-brown fish by the gills and said, "Christmas Eve?" and Elliot, who had planned all fortnight to be sensible, had heard themselves say, "Yes. Do you do it in a bucket?" He had been installed in the great marble bath of the prefects' bathroom on the fifth floor, which, according to the notice on the door, was not at all against the rules, and had spent two days going slowly round among the taps, gazing up at the mermaid in the window with every appearance of contentment. At no point had it occurred to him that he had a purpose.

"_That's_ the fish?" said Zara.

"That's the fish."

"It's _enormous_."

"He's a very good size," said Elliot, with some pride. It was two in the afternoon, and they were in the kitchens, at the long central table, with their sleeves rolled and a bucket at their feet. "In Prague they sell them in the streets, from tubs, on the twenty-third. Everyone has one. It sits in the bath until the evening and then somebody has to—" They stopped. "It's a very old tradition."

"What do you do with it?"

"Well," said Elliot, "that's the question."

---

It was the most extraordinary kitchen they had ever seen. It was as big as a barn, with a huge hearth at one end and a ceiling hung with copper, and in it, at forty different stations, with enormous variety of skill and complete confidence, worked most of the professors of Hogwarts.

Hagrid was in charge of the roast. It had been, at some earlier stage, a turkey; it now stood in a vast iron tray like a piece of furniture, with drumsticks the size of cricket bats, stuffed with chestnuts, sage and most of a small tree. Zara, who had spent the afternoon at his side as promised, was perched on a barrel with a long fork, saying with great authority, "I think it needs another hour."

"Yeh think?"

"I'm a captain."

"Righto," said Hagrid, and put it back in the oven, which took three of them.

Professor Sprout presided over nine hundred sprouts, beneath a note pinned above the bench in a small, brisk, upright hand: _Cut a cross in the stalk end. It is the only way. Trust me. —P.D._ "Who," said Sprout, to nobody in particular, "is P.D., and why does she have opinions about my sprouts?"

"She's right, though," said Oliver, from the next bench, where he was peeling potatoes with the concentration of a man handling a bomb.

"Oh, I know she's right. That's what's so irritating."

Flitwick, on a stack of five books, was making pudding in a copper cauldron the size of a bath, stirring it by charm and humming. Slughorn, beside him, with a napkin round his neck and a spoon in each fist, was engaged on the most serious task of the afternoon. He was tasting. "Outstanding," he said, with his mouth full. "Flitwick, my dear fellow, you've done it again."

"I haven't added the brandy."

"Then it's outstanding _without_ the brandy."

And moving between them all, quick and cheerful and unobtrusive, like a small round gardener among his plants, with a tray of Winter Blend and a stack of recipe cards in his pocket, went Finn.

There were no elves at the stoves. At four o'clock the whole kitchen staff of Hogwarts would sit down at the end of the central table, on a row of small stools that Finn had set out, and for the first time in a thousand years do nothing at all. They had been arriving for some time, in ones and twos, in clean grey waistcoats, with the wary, respectable air of people invited to a wedding who aren't sure what to do with their hands. Tibby had polished her silver _T_. Dibby had her tea-cosy on straight. Finn had set out cups.

It was a quarter to three. The kolače were in.

Elliot had made them from memory, or very nearly: small, soft, yellow pastries with a hollow in the middle that you filled with poppy seed and plum and sweet white cheese. They had a card in their pocket with the exact weights. It was soft and stained and creased, in a close, narrow, upright hand, and it had been their grandmother's. They had taken it out to check the oven, and it lay on the table by the tray.

And a very large shadow fell across the window, and somebody said, "_Oh_."

---

It was Bohumil. He came in through the open top of the casement in a great, slow, overweight sweep, shedding feathers, with a letter in his beak and an expression of deep injured weariness, and landed on the nearest object, which was Slughorn's pudding. He sank to the knees. He looked at it. With enormous dignity he did not move.

"Good gracious," said Slughorn.

"Sorry, Professor. He's from home."

The letter was thick, sealed with a stub of grey wax, in the particular place and at the particular angle that their mother always used. On the front, in a hand they knew as well as their own, it said _Elliot_.

They did not open it at the table. There was a pantry behind the long dresser: a small cold stone cell lined with jam and hung with the carcasses of several very large birds. They shut the door, and stood among the jars in the smell of apples, with the clatter of the kitchen going on beyond the wall, and broke the seal.

It was four pages, written carefully, in English, in a hand that had been slowed down for the purpose.

_Elliot,_

_Your drawings came on Wednesday. I have looked at them every evening. I do not know how to say what I think in either language, so I will say it badly: they are very good. They are better than very good. I am not able to be objective, and I do not care._

_I am learning. Elliot. They. I say it to the cat. The cat has not complained._

_I must tell you something which I should have told you at the lake, and I could not. I was afraid it would sound like a reason, and not a gift._

_Your babička drew._

_All her life. She kept the books at the bottom of the wardrobe in her flat, under the winter coats, and nobody knew: not your grandfather, not me. I found them when she died, and I did not know what to do with them, and I put them in the drawer in the dresser where the photograph was. I did not open it for two years. I opened it on Monday._

_You told me once that she was the only person who understood your drawings. I did not know why. I do now._

_I enclose some pages. Your father photographed them (he says the photographs are poor; they are not). I should like to show you the rest at Easter. Come home. Bring your sketchbook. We shall have a great deal to show each other._

_Mama_

_P.S. Your father asks whether there is a carp at Hogwarts. I have told him not to ask._

Elliot read it twice: first fast, in a blur, the way you read when your eyes are ahead of your mind; and then slowly, a line at a time, with their back against the cold wall.

There were six photographs, small and square and a little blurred, as if taken at an angle on a kitchen table: a window; a woman's hands; a cat asleep on a cushion; the roofs of Prague in the rain; a man with a very large nose, reading, with his glasses pushed up on his forehead; a plum tree. They were not clever, or grand. They were quick, quiet, loving, exact, and extremely good, and every line of them was in the hand that Elliot, for fifteen years, had been trying to find in themselves.

They stood a long time with the pages in their hands. Then they put the photographs back in order, and the whole thing in the inside pocket of their jacket, over the sketchbook, and breathed in. It came out shaky.

It was only at the door, with their hand on the latch, that they remembered the card on the table.

---

It lay where they'd left it, among the flour and the tray of golden pastries. _Koláče. 500 g flour. 250 ml milk._ They had looked at it for years. They knew every smudge: a thumbprint of jam in one corner, a ring where a cup had stood, and, down the side, in the margin beside each of the fillings, a series of tiny marks.

They had always thought they were doodles. They had thought, if they'd thought of it at all, that she'd been a woman who scribbled when she was thinking, like a person who taps their foot.

They looked again.

It was a poppy-head, an inch high, in three fine lines and a grey shadow, beside _mák_. Beside _povidla_, in the very same hand, a plum, with its stalk and its bloom and one bright curve of light on its side. Beside _tvaroh_, a milk-jug. And at the bottom of the card, tucked into the corner where a person's hand rests while they read, a very small bird on a twig, with its head turned a little to one side, as if listening.

It was so alive that Elliot could almost hear it. They'd had it for six years. They had never once bent their head to see.

"Oh," said Elliot, aloud, to the empty table.

And somebody, in the kitchen behind them, screamed.

---

It was the carp.

He had got out. Nobody could say how. Some thought he had been disturbed by the owl, others that he'd been nursing the idea for some time; but the bucket was empty, and a large, wet, shining shape was coming across the kitchen floor in a series of immense and astonishing leaps.

"_Jaroslav!_"

He went through the legs of the roast. He went under the table. He went in a long slippery arc over a bowl of breadcrumbs and a sack of flour and the stool where Oliver had been peeling, and Oliver went one way and the potatoes another. Zara threw herself at him in a flying tackle, and missed. Hagrid lunged with a ladle. Sprout shouted "_Stand back!_" and hurled a colander. A cloud of flour went up. Finn knelt on the flagstones with a tea-towel and his arms out, saying "Come on, mate," in a low coaxing voice, as to a nervous horse.

"Don't hurt him!" cried Amara, from the doorway.

"I'm not going to _hurt_ him, I'm going to _catch_ him," said Zara, and went full length across the floor.

It lasted nearly ten minutes. It went round the whole of the kitchen in a great, thundering, laughing circle, through the legs of eleven professors and out along the row of stools where the elves had assembled, who with perfect unanimous calm lifted their feet. And somewhere behind the dresser, with flour in their hair, Elliot found that they were laughing so hard that they couldn't stand up.

At five o'clock the big door opened.

It opened inward with a loud, measured creak, and on the threshold, in her green cloak, with her hat on straight and a list in her hand, stood Professor McGonagall. She had come to inspect. She had said so. She had a pencil.

The carp did not see her. He had made, at that moment, a supreme effort. He took off from the middle of the floor with a great flip of his golden tail, in a long, slow, shining arc above the flour and the potatoes and the faces turned to watch, and with the unerring instinct of a creature that has been looking all its life for somewhere to belong, came down, full length, in the arms of the Headmistress of Hogwarts.

There was a very long silence.

She looked at him. He looked at her. His round calm golden eye regarded her without alarm, and his wide mouth opened and shut twice.

"Novak," said Professor McGonagall.

"Yes, Headmistress?"

"I believe this is yours."

---

They let him go at dusk.

It was Amara's idea. She said, in a small flat voice, that it was a very nice fish, and had not done anything, and that in her opinion it was not the sort of thing you could eat after it had jumped on the Headmistress. Elliot said that in Prague nobody would have seen it that way. And Hagrid said he'd a nice bit of river trout hangin' in the larder, if anyone wanted to be traditional, an' it'd do just as well.

They carried him down in the bucket, across the blue snow, in a little procession: Elliot with the handle, Amara with her hand on the side, Wren with a lantern. The sky was a pale tender green with a single star in it, and the great frozen lake lay below them like a sheet of glass. There was a round black hole in the ice at the end of the jetty, which Hagrid had cut that morning.

"Say goodbye," said Amara.

"Na shledanou," said Elliot, to the fish, rather huskily. "Jaroslav. I'm sorry. It was a good bath."

He went in with a splash. For a moment he hung there, a long gold shape in the dark under the ice, and then he turned, and with a lazy, magnificent flick of his tail, was gone.

"He'll be all right," said Wren, quietly, holding the lantern low so the light fell on the water. "There's a squid."

"There's a very large squid."

"They'll be friends."

"I'd like to think so," said Elliot.

They stood on the jetty a little while, in the green dusk, with the pale lake going away on every side. And up in the castle, in the long grey bulk against the sky, a window lit, and then another, and then a hundred.

---

The table ran the whole length of the Hall.

It had been put together in the half-hour before dinner, by Hagrid and five seventh-years and a good deal of unspoken magic: a single long straight table, with a white cloth and a hundred candles, and no head to it. It was laid on both sides for a hundred and twenty. There were bowls of holly and platters of glazed ham; the vast shining turkey, a pyramid of trout, a mountain of sprouts every one of which had been cut across the stalk; and in the middle, in a glass dish, a heap of small round yellow pastries with a hollow in each.

Above them the lanterns burned: a hundred and one, hung in their long spiral shell in the vaulted dark, gold and rose and green, silver and violet; and in the middle one orange-red, and a little way off an amber, a gold, and a blue. They shed a soft mingled rainbow light over the white cloth and the faces, a great jewelled dusk in which the candles made a long bar of warmth.

They were all there. There were the twenty-three, and the staff, and in the very middle of the table, in a long row of small chairs with their feet dangling, in clean waistcoats and clean ears, with an expression of ineffable, trembling, awed delight, every house-elf in the school. Tibby sat at McGonagall's right hand with her hands in her lap, looking round at the hundred candles and the ceiling and the great yellow roar of the fire, and her eyes were enormous.

"Headmistress," she said, faintly. "There are so _many_ lights."

"Yes," said Professor McGonagall. "I believe that is what they are for."

They ate. It was a long, loud, luxurious meal. Hagrid carved, with a sword. Slughorn said "Outstanding" eleven times. Flitwick's pudding, brought in flaming with a sprig of holly, drew an applause that rang the whole stone Hall. The kolače were passed from hand to hand. Zara ate six. Oliver, who had been cutting his potatoes into pieces of a perfectly even size, said with an air of awe, "I peeled _those_." Mira, with a smear of green ink on her cuff, who had written to Dr Lund at breakfast, had looked at the window for an owl eleven times and was pretending she hadn't.

And somewhere in the middle of it, Elliot found that they had stopped eating.

---

It was Wren they noticed first.

She had come in late. She'd gone to put away her lantern, and take off her wet shoes, and now she stood in the great doorway, quite alone, in her good dark dress, with her fringe pinned back, looking at the table. She had the grave, blank, extremely well-behaved look of a person who has come to a party and found that everyone has arrived already, and the places are full.

There was a chair. It was at the very centre of the long white cloth, directly opposite their own, between Finn and Amara: an ordinary chair, pulled out a hand's breadth from the table. All through the soup and the roast and the pudding, as the table filled and the laughter rose and people leaned across each other for the gravy, nobody had touched it. Elliot had seen it without seeing, for an hour, on the very edge of their eye, like the place in a picture where the colour has been left out.

Finn had set it. They saw that now. He was looking at Wren across the table, with his freckled face quite grave and his hand lying flat on the cloth. He didn't say anything. He only moved his head, very slightly, towards the chair.

Wren hesitated. She looked at the chair. She looked at the faces, lit by the lanterns, turned half-way towards her: Mira's, Zara's, Amara's, round and bright and very still. And Elliot, who knew exactly what it was to stand at the edge of a room and wait to be sure, found they were holding their breath.

She crossed the floor, quite slowly, in her flat black shoes, with her back very straight. She put her hand on the back of the chair. And Amara, without a word, with a small scrape of wood, shifted along her bench to make room.

"It's _soup_," said Amara, in a loud urgent whisper. "You've missed the soup. There's loads left. Have mine."

"Thank you," said Wren.

"Have the roll."

"Thank you."

"Have the _trout_."

"I think," said Wren, with her whole face lit, "I'll start with the soup."

---

It was later, when the plates had been cleared and the candles burned down by a third, and the Hall had gone quiet in the way of a room full of people who have eaten as much as they can and are not yet ready to leave, that Elliot took out the sketchbook.

They hadn't planned to. It had always been a private thing, and it lay in the inside pocket of their jacket, among the folded pages, beside a small soft crumpled card.

They put it on the table. It made a faint flat sound against the cloth. Nobody looked at it at once. There was a small, careful, tender silence, like the one that falls when somebody puts down a very full glass.

They opened it at the beginning, and didn't explain. They only pushed it, gently, along the white cloth, to Zara. She looked at them. She took it. She turned a page, and a second, and a long slow _oh_ went out of her.

It went round the table.

It went from Zara to Oliver, who looked for a long time with his head bent and handed it on without a word. It went to Finn, who'd got something in his eye and said so. It went across to Mira, who held it with both hands, like a thing that could fall, and turned each page by the corner, and read every one: Slughorn's rooms and a clock with a bad temper; a hand holding a spoon; a lake and a lantern; a girl in a green scarf asleep over a book; a very tall figure in a tweed coat with a moustache; four people at a rail. At the back, folded in four between the last page and the cover, were six small square photographs of a woman's pencil drawings, a window and a plum tree and the rain. It reached Amara, who said "That's _me_," and then, softer, "That's Nettle." It reached Wren, who said nothing at all.

It went on. It went along the table, from hand to hand, in the candlelight, under the lanterns, past the wreck of the turkey and the heaps of holly, to the elves, who looked at it with their small hands on the page in awed and reverent silence; to Hagrid, who said "Well, I'll be—" and had to stop; to McGonagall, who held it a long time, with her glasses pushed up on her forehead and her lips pressed together, and passed it on with a small, dry, upright nod.

And Elliot sat in the middle of it with their hands in their lap and a pulse in their throat, and did not say a word. They didn't need to. It had been in the dark for a very long time. It was out now, in the air, among the people it was for, going round the long bright table from hand to hand like a thing that is meant to be passed, and nobody was holding it at arm's length, or making it small.

It came back to Zara. She put it down with a quiet careful thud, and looked at Elliot. She didn't smile. She only picked up her glass, and held it a little way out over the table.

"To the Wonder Seekers," said Zara.

"To the Wonder Seekers," said Mira, and Finn, and Oliver, and Amara, and Wren, and, after a moment, in a ragged delighted uncertain chorus, all down the length of the table, somebody else.

Above them, the lanterns burned. It was getting late. And outside, over the white lake, beyond the dark glass of the high windows, it had begun, very softly, to snow.
